#3084. Honeywell Aerospace vs. GE Aerospace: Which Pure-Play Aerospace Stock Is the Better Buy Now That Both Stand Alone?

One looks like it has hit the ground running, while the other is still trying to get its footing.

GE Aerospace (GE +1.55%) is being led by the same CEO who orchestrated the breakup of General Electric into three separate businesses. Honeywell Aerospace (HONA -0.28%) is not being led by the CEO who oversaw the breakup of that iconic industrial conglomerate. That doesn't mean Honeywell Aerospace is a bad company, but it's worth noting that GE Aerospace is achieving more impressive results right now. If you are looking at these two aerospace companies, here's why you might want to focus your attention on GE Aerospace.

Wall Street goes through phases. Sometimes creating conglomerates is all the rage; other times, the focus is on breaking companies into smaller pieces. Recently, the industrial sector has been focused on simplification, leading to the breakup of both General Electric and Honeywell, two iconic names in the sector. That's how GE Aerospace and Honeywell Aerospace were born.

Both, however, are still fairly new businesses on a stand-alone basis. That creates a big problem when it comes to analyzing either business. For example, as of this writing, GE Aerospace's price-to-sales ratio is 6.3x, and its price-to-earnings ratio is 36x. Is that high or low? If you compare those valuation metrics to the five-year averages of 2.2x and 28.5x, respectively, they look high. But those averages include years when GE Aerospace was still the old General Electric.

For comparison, Honeywell Aerospace's P/S ratio is 2.7x, and its P/E is 44x. There are no longer-term averages because the stock was created by Honeywell's corporate breakup. That said, based on the P/S ratio, it appears to be a more attractive value than GE Aerospace, but the opposite is true when you look at the P/E. There's no easy answer to which is the better value.

That said, there is a clearer difference when you look at each aerospace company's third-quarter financial performance. GE Aerospace reported that revenues advanced 16% year over year in the quarter. Sales in the company's commercial business rose 21%, while sales in its defense business jumped 7%. Roughly 75% of GE Aerospace's commercial business is related to services, with services accounting for 50% of its defense business (services are 70% of overall sales), creating a reliable foundation of recurring income. Honeywell Aerospace saw sales increase 2%. Its commercial original equipment sales jumped 7%, commercial aftermarket sales rose 2%, and its defense business's sales improved by 1%.

To be fair, GE Aerospace has been a stand-alone business for longer. So Honeywell Aerospace is still working through the complexity of becoming a stand-alone business. In some ways, it is still finding its footing, while GE Aerospace is clearly running at full stride. But for an investor, that's not a subtle point.

Given the relative success GE Aerospace is seeing right now compared to Honeywell Aerospace, you can understand why the CEO of GE chose to stay on and run GE Aerospace. As an investor, GE Aerospace appears to be the better-positioned business. It is also much larger, with a market cap of around $320 billion compared to Honeywell Aerospace's roughly $50 billion.

It probably wouldn't be a mistake to buy Honeywell Aerospace. However, you would be buying it while it was still in the middle of a big transition. GE Aerospace is clearly performing better as a business, at least partly because it has had more time operating as a stand-alone company. Both have solid long-term growth prospects as people travel more globally, but for moderate and more conservative investors, larger and better-performing GE Aerospace will likely be the better choice right now.

#3080. Schwab's Dividend ETF Keeps Hitting Records. I'd Still Buy It Now.

Its yield has slipped under 3%. How did buyers do the last time a strong year left it there?

The Schwab U.S. Dividend Equity ETF (SCHD -0.97%) ended Friday, Oct. 30, at $35.78. That was the fund's 28th record close in 2026, and it traded near $36 as of this writing.

On price alone, the fund is up about 31% this year, while the S&P 500 has climbed about 11%. With dividends, the fund has returned about 34%, leaving it on track for its best calendar year since it started in 2011. Its strongest full year yet was 2013, at around 33%.

But for a fund of dividend-paying value stocks that most people buy for income, a higher price means a lower yield for anybody buying now. After all, the payout doesn't grow just because the share price does. And since mid-October alone, the price has climbed around 5%.

I still think the fund is worth buying. I'd just expect less than this year has brought.

The fund doesn't set its own dividend. It passes on what its 102 holdings pay quarterly, minus an expense ratio of 0.06%. Its last four payments, including the one made on Sept. 28, come to around $1.05 a share, according to Schwab.

At today's price, that's a yield of around 2.9%. It was around 3.1% in mid-October and about 3.8% at the end of 2025.

In dollars, $1,000 invested now buys around $29 a year of income at the current payout rate. The same $1,000 at the end of last year bought about $38.

But the fund has had a yield this low before.

The closest recent match is the end of 2021. The fund gained around 30% that year including dividends, finished the year just under its record close, and yielded about 2.8% at year-end -- a little less than today.

The next nine months were tough. By Sept. 30, 2022, the fund's price had fallen around 18% from where 2021 ended. And after a brief climb in early January 2022, it didn't close above that year-end mark again until July 2024. Counting dividends, the fund still lost around 3% in 2022.

The payout, though, kept growing. The fund's 2022 payments rose about 14% over 2021's. Still, a buyer who reinvested every dividend didn't get back to even for good until January 2024.

In all, that buyer is up around 58% with dividends reinvested, or about 10% a year. And with the yearly payout up about 41% since 2021, the income on that original purchase now works out to a yield of about 3.9%.

Of course, the 2021 buyer got help from fast payout growth. The fund's yearly payout climbed around 11% in 2021, then another 14% in 2022.

Now, the payout's growth is cooling. Yearly growth slowed from around 12% in 2024 to about 5% in 2025. And the last four payments are only about 2% higher than the four before them.

Put another way, a buyer today is counting on the payout picking up again, and it might not do so quickly.

The holdings' valuation doesn't look rich, at least. Schwab showed the fund's price-to-earnings ratio at around 18 at the end of September. For the Schwab U.S. Large-Cap ETF, which tracks the broader large-cap market, the number was around 25.

That same day, the dividend fund's trailing distribution yield was about 3.2%, versus around 1% for the large-cap fund. The dividend fund has gained about 10% since then while the S&P 500 has been nearly flat, so both gaps have probably shrunk.

Yes, a fund up around 31% over 10 months might lose a big chunk of that, like it did in 2022. But the 2021 buyer kept collecting a rising payout through that drop, and a buyer now should still collect one, even if it grows slower.

Is the fund still worth buying at a record? I think so. A yield just under 3% is less income than buyers got in January, but it's near what 2021's buyers paid, and the holdings seem to trade at a lower price-to-earnings ratio than the broad market. I'd buy shares at this price, maybe adding a bit at a time. I just wouldn't count on another year like 2026.

A Republican president overseeing a split Congress has delivered outlier S&P 500 returns since 1926.

Today, Americans charted a path forward for our great country by heading to the polls or mailing in their ballots. In addition to shaking up Congress, voters may also be, inadvertently, altering the near-term outlook for the iconic Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI -0.38%), broad-based S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.36%), and tech-driven Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC +0.83%).

President Trump will almost certainly be working with a divided Congress come January 2027. Image source: Official White House Photo by Daniel Torok.

Nearly 100 years of history says a worst-case scenario is unfolding for the stock market on Election Night. However, most investors are overlooking an election silver lining that isn't getting enough attention.

Although no predictive tool can ever guarantee a short-term directional move in the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq Composite, close to a century of S&P 500 return data calculated by online retirement planning platform Retirement Researcher, spanning 1926 to 2023, points to a choppy road ahead.

If prediction markets are correct, the historical worst-case scenario, a split Congress with a Republican president, will take shape over the next two years.

Two factors could drive this underperformance.

DEMOCRATIC SWEEP? UBS SEES 50% ODDS. UBS assigns a 50% probability to Democrats winning both chambers, while prediction markets put Democratic control of the House at 94% and Senate at 77%. UBS warns a sweep could complicate Trump's fiscal agenda and 2027 debt-ceiling negotiations, while tariffs and AI investment remain largely insulated.

Firstly, debt-ceiling negotiations are more challenging when Congress is divided. If Democrats retake one or both houses of Congress, the odds of a government shutdown in the first half of 2027 (i.e., when the debt ceiling is next projected to be reached) go up.

Secondly, and perhaps most importantly, Democrats favor more restrictive regulations for artificial intelligence (AI). Anything that draws interest to AI regulation could be bad news for Wall Street's No. 1 catalyst.

While history doesn't always offer the rosiest of short-term outlooks for the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq Composite, there are silver linings in this data that simply aren't getting enough attention.

For example, a 7.33% average annual return is still stellar compared to the average returns of Treasury bonds, commodities, real estate, and certificates of deposit (CDs). In other words, perspective changes everything.

Furthermore, the S&P 500 has been higher in November in each of the last 19 midterms, dating back to 1950.

While political shake-ups can be scary and create short-term turbulence, corporate earnings and major innovative trends, such as AI, tend to be the primary forces driving equities. If investors widen their lens, they'll see that major elections are nothing to fear.

#3065. To Defend Against AI Attackers, Start Thinking Like One

Yair Grindlinger is CEO and Co-Founder of Surf AI.

AI is not black magic. It still has to work through real systems, identities, applications and infrastructure, which means its limits are knowable. That matters, because fear is only useful when it’s aimed at the right things.

For enterprises, the right thing to fear is how AI changes the attacker. AI-driven attacks are faster, more persistent and more sophisticated, and they can exploit a single critical weakness or chain together a handful of small ones until they find a way in. ​​

There are several obvious ways AI changes attacks. First is speed. We’re seeing attacks happen in hours instead of days or weeks because AI systems can operate 24/7. The window for defenders is already shrinking. CrowdStrike’s 2026 Global Threat Report found that the average time it took criminal attackers to move from initial access deeper into a network​ fell to 29 minutes, and the fastest observed breakout took just 27 seconds.

But I think stamina may be even more important than speed. A typical human attacker might spend a few hours trying to get into an organization. If nothing works, they may move to the next victim. AI doesn’t necessarily have that limitation. If you tell an AI system to find a way in, it can keep working 24/7 until it finds something.

Then there is breadth of knowledge. Human attackers are typically good at something, whether it’s social engineering, zero-days or a particular system. AI can have the whole playbook. The same system can work across identity, software vulnerabilities, configurations and other attack surfaces. Because it has both stamina and breadth of knowledge, it can connect those weaknesses together.

That ability to connect weaknesses changes how we need to think about exposure. An attack might begin with an exposed secret, then move to an asset with a risky configuration, a software vulnerability or an identity problem. Each issue might look relatively insignificant on its own. You might classify each one as medium or low risk and decide none is critical enough to demand immediate attention. But together, they can create a critical attack path.

Microsoft made essentially the same point in its July 2026 “Secure Future Initiative“ progress report, noting that attackers can use “frontier models to discover vulnerabilities, chain attack paths, and scale exploitation faster than manual approaches allow.” It also observed that serious security failures can emerge when identity gaps, unmanaged assets and inconsistent configurations sit next to one another.

The Hugging Face intrusion offers a vivid example. In July 2026, the company disclosed that an autonomous AI agent, which had escaped an AI lab’s evaluation environment, carried out an end-to-end intrusion into part of its production infrastructure, with no human directing its steps. The attack moved from code-execution weaknesses to credential harvesting and lateral movement across internal clusters. Hugging Face later reconstructed roughly 17,600 attacker actions as the system searched for a viable path.​ ​Most of those actions failed. The agent simply kept going until something worked.

A human attacker may look for one big open window and move on if it isn’t there. AI will find that window faster, and if it isn’t there, it will patiently look for the little things and chain them together.

Attackers and defenders often see the environment differently. From a defender’s perspective, we frequently manage security in islands. There may be a cloud vulnerability team, an identity team, IT teams and someone else responsible for third-party applications. The attacker doesn’t care about those organizational boundaries. To the attacker, all of those things are part of the same attack surface.

Suppose a team is looking at a cloud vulnerability. On its own, it might not appear urgent. But what if the asset can be accessed by someone whose MFA is misconfigured and who also uses a highly privileged third-party application? If the teams responsible for those areas don’t know the issues are connected, they don’t understand the real priority.

That becomes especially important as AI gives defenders visibility into more exposures. In many cases, these weaknesses were probably always there. We simply know about more of them now. That’s a good thing because defenders can use powerful tools to find the same types of exposures attackers are looking for.

The problem is that organizations will get a lot of results. It will be impossible to manage everything at once. Prioritization therefore has to change. Instead of looking only at how severe an individual vulnerability appears to be, ask whether it is exposed, how it connects to other weaknesses and whether it forms part of an attack chain.

A critical exposure on an important asset still demands attention, whether or not it’s part of a chain. But something that appears lower-risk may also offer a quick win because fixing it breaks a larger chain.

AI has made finding vulnerabilities faster and easier than ever. Closing them still depends on knowing who owns the affected asset, understanding what a fix could affect and getting approval, and that’s where most organizations get stuck. In many of my conversations with CISOs, we never get to attack chains. They have critical exposures they can’t close.

One CISO at a large enterprise told me that 25% to 35% of employees leave or change roles in a given year. Many of them own systems, and the asset inventories meant to track that are almost never up to date. So security teams spend their days chasing people to find out who can approve a fix. That isn’t the job anyone signed up for, and it’s the bottleneck that keeps exposures open. Before you can think in chains, you have to be able to close the basics quickly.​

I see two fundamental requirements for responding to this shift. First, organizations need to know their assets: what they have, who owns each one and what a change could affect. Second, they need automation operating continuously on top of that knowledge.

If attackers are operating at machine speed, defenders cannot depend entirely on human-speed processes. Organizations need systems that continuously collect information, connect exposures and help security teams identify which problems matter most.​​ Then they need to get each fix to the right people, because a prioritized list nobody can act on doesn’t reduce risk.

This matters even for threats no one has seen yet. You can’t patch a zero-day before you know it exists, but you can shrink your blast radius every day by closing the exposures around it, so the next one matters less before it’s even disclosed. And when it does land, you can be ready to answer “are we exposed?” in hours, not weeks.​

For CISOs, my practical advice is to pause and rethink exposure management. The way we have managed exposure until today isn’t necessarily going to work for the AI era. Assign someone, or create a team, whose job is to think about exposure holistically. Then reconsider the people, process and technology required to support that approach.

AI creates real reasons for concern. But the most useful fear is a specific one: attackers are becoming faster, more persistent and more sophisticated. Defenders need to respond in kind: know their assets, shrink their blast radius every day and be ready for the next one.

In other words, we need to start thinking a little more like the attackers.​

Kioxia's outlook is still worth remembering, though, because the next sign of slowing prices might hit the stock just as hard.

CoreWeave shares moved roughly 2% higher on Tuesday, after tumbling over the past several days and hitting a 52-week low earlier in the session. Its bond yields and spreads remain highly elevated.

Lloyd believes the selloff is a bump in the road for the neocloud company.

CoreWeave leverage is high with a debt-to-equity ratio of 702%, up from 290% a year ago. Moreover, CoreWeave will spend an estimated $36.4 billion on capital expenditures this year against Ebitda of $7.5 billion, according to consensus estimates.

Lloyd isn't necessarily worried about CoreWeave's ability to finance its capital expenditures, despite the fact that its capex outweighs its free-cash flow.

"Next year … they're going to be generating operating free-cash flow that'll support some of that capex need," he said.

The company can also turn to other methods of funding, like more GPU financing, which will allow it to borrow against its graphics processing units or rental contracts, he noted. The Federal Reserve Board on Wednesday finalized changes to enhance the transparency and public accountability of its stress test and reduce volatility in its stress test-related capital requirements. The two final rules are largely similar to the proposed rules from 2025. The Board also requested comment on a proposal to better capture differences in banks' business models to generate fee income.

The Board conducts stress tests to ensure that large banks are sufficiently capitalized and able to lend to households and businesses even in a severe recession. In December 2024, the Board announced it would modify the test to improve its resilience.

"The stress test is an essential component of our regulatory capital framework," said Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle W. Bowman. "Today's changes preserve its resilience by ensuring that it is transparent, granular, and risk-sensitive. The public will now have greater assurance that the risks banks take will be reflected appropriately in their stress test losses and their capital requirements."

The first final rule requires the Board to invite public input annually on the stress test scenarios and any material model changes. It also updates the framework that guides the design of the hypothetical scenarios and adopts the models that will be used for the 2027 stress test. In addition, the final rule makes several adjustments to the stress test calendar. The final rule also updates the global market shock component, which is a set of shocks to specific market components applied to banks with large trading books. These banks will now be tested against two global market shock components each year, and the Board will use the shock that produces the largest losses for each firm to calculate the firm's stress test results.

The second final rule requires the Board, when calculating stress capital buffer requirements, to average the results from the two most recent annual supervisory stress tests for firms subject to the stress test in both years. The Board will begin averaging stress capital buffer requirements in 2028 to ensure that only models incorporating public input are used in the calculation.

The proposal would request public comment on a revision to the Board's noninterest income model that would improve the stress test's ability to capture business model diversity across firms. If adopted, these adjustments would replace the current noninterest income model that projects each bank's fee income under stress. Comments are due 60 days after publication in the Federal Register.

Together, the changes are likely to reduce year-over-year volatility in capital requirements by approximately 50 percent and are not expected to materially affect aggregate capital requirements.

#2971. BlackRock's Rick Rieder sees 'historic' opportunity in high-quality bonds

Investors may be wary of the volatility hitting parts of the bond market right now, but they have an "historic" opportunity to grab attractive yields in high-quality credit, Rick Rieder, BlackRock's chief investment officer for global fixed income, told CNBC in an exclusive interview.

Hypothetically, it's now possible for investors to build a diversified portfolio that yields more than 7% with less than three years of duration and an average credit quality of A-, he said. Duration is a measurement of a bond's price sensitivity to fluctuations in rates, and issues with shorter maturities tend to have lower duration.

"Not only is it historic in that these yields are where they are, but the only time these yields are ever here is when there's some problem, like an economic problem or default problem," Rieder said in an interview about his fourth-quarter outlook, released Wednesday.

That isn't the case now, where the economy remains strong, he said. "So you don't really have to worry about defaults of any significance."

While Rieder didn't specifically identify the credits in the aforementioned portfolio, the iShares Aaa - A Rated Corporate Bond ETF (QLTA) has a 30-day SEC yield of 5.68% and an effective duration of 6.48 years — higher than Rieder's low duration focus.

The iShares Flexible Income Active ETF (BINC), which is managed by Rieder, has a 5.66% 30-day SEC yield and three-year effective duration, but its portfolio extends beyond investment-grade credit.

Rieder sees the best opportunity right now in investment-grade corporate bonds — which he pointed out he hasn't said "in a really long time." Investment grade bonds are rated AAA through BBB- by S&P and Fitch, while Moody's rates them Aaa through Baa3.

That includes hyperscaler bonds, which have flooded the market with issuance this year as they ramp up their AI infrastructure. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta Platforms and Oracle have issued a total of about $228.4 billion in bonds year to date, as of Oct. 20, according to LSEG.

"They're very low leverage. They have tremendous amounts of cash flow coming in," Rieder said of the hyperscalers in general. "You're now getting to buy these entities which are higher quality, paying you a very attractive yield to get it."

Rieder's iShares Flexible Income Active ETF has upped its allocation to investment-grade corporates to 14.3% of its portfolio. In July, that stood at 8.5%.

That said, investors need to be patient and sit tight since rates could still move higher from here, he advised.

"Your starting point is by far your best barometer of what your return is going to be," he noted. "So if you get seven and a quarter, maybe you get eight, maybe you get six, but ... if your holding period is a year or longer, you're going to get that yield."

Caution should also be exercised outside of investment-grade credit, which requires more selectivity and deep research, he warned. That includes AI names.

"There's parts of the data center supply that's not hyperscaler guaranteed or hyperscaler backed," he said. "That we've been more cautious around, and we'll continue to be."

The bond market, particularly high-yield credit, has been rocked as investor concern grows over the massive amount of debt some companies are taking out to fund their artificial intelligence build-out.

Junk-rated CoreWeave has become the poster child, seeing its stock drop, bond yields jump and credit-default spreads widen. Oracle, which is sitting in the lower end of investment grade and is seen as a bellwether on the broader AI market, has also taken some hits.

Rieder is also keeping an eye on the Federal Reserve, which is expected to raise its federal funds rate again in December to combat inflation. The market is currently pricing in a nearly 90% probability that the central bank will hike at that time, according to the CME Group FedWatch tool.

The money manager expects that to happen, although he does not agree with it. Higher rates are hurting parts of the economy, like housing, while not having much of an impact on other areas — such as tech companies' capital expenditures, which have become a big driver of growth, he said.

"They're not borrowing off the overnight funds rate," Rieder noted. "There's a lot of floating rate finance in housing, credit card, auto loan finance, and you're seeing those areas already under pressure."

Instead, the market is and will continue to price the AI supply coming into the market, he added.

Rieder sees a number of possible risks ahead, including rising levels of government debt across the globe. In the United States, the budget deficit has grown to $2 trillion for fiscal 2026.

"The more the central banks raise the rates, the cost of debt to these to already indebted governments is something we've got to be careful about over time," he said.

Bond supply coming from AI companies should also be watched.

"With the amount of supply that's coming — data center, hyperscaler — there's some crowding-out effect that's taking place," he said. "With government rates moving higher, that is something that we just got to keep an eye on."

In the meantime, Rieder would just continue to reap the rewards of high investment-grade yields "and smile a lot," he said.

#2939. Topps’ New 1/1 Baseball Autographs Come With a 10-Card Catch

The biggest chase in 2026 Topps Cosmic Chrome Baseball starts with a card you can pull from a pack. To finish it, you'll need nine more of the same player.

For sports card collectors, the twist is on the back. Topps is hiding single-use codes beneath a black peel on its Supernova inserts. The first collector to redeem 10 distinct Supernovas of the same player and complete verification wins that player's one-of-one Black Hole autograph.

Topps laid out the promotion and official rules on its Black Hole page. There are 25 subjects and 25 on-card autographs, one per player. The Supernovas come from packs. The Black Hole autographs are awarded through the promotion.

Each Supernova has a unique code under the black peel. Collectors can scan the QR code on the card or go directly to the promotion page, create a free account and enter their codes. Topps says collectors must verify their email to be eligible to win.

Each player has a separate leaderboard. A Shohei Ohtani Supernova counts toward Ohtani's autograph, while an Aaron Judge Supernova counts toward Judge's. Collectors can pursue several players at once, but each prize requires 10 cards of that player.

Paul Skenes, Bobby Witt Jr. and Yoshinobu Yamamoto are among the 25 names on the checklist. So are Jacob Misiorowski, Roman Anthony and Munetaka Murakami. The official rules list 1,250 eligible cards in aggregate. That works out to an average of 50 per player across the checklist, though the aggregate alone doesn't establish an individual print run.

Topps lists the start of the entry period as on or about Nov. 6, 2026. It runs through July 31, 2036, or until all 25 prizes are successfully claimed. Each player's race can effectively end much sooner once a winner clears verification.

Topps allows collectors to buy eligible Supernovas on the secondary market. That makes it possible to chase one player without relying entirely on packs. It also creates a question a photo of the card front can't answer. Has someone already redeemed the code?

A Supernova with a used code still exists as a collectible, but it can't advance another collector's entry. The official rules put the risk of a previously submitted code on the buyer. For someone paying a premium to get from nine cards to 10, the condition of the peel and whether the code is still usable matter alongside the condition of the card.

Reaching 10 on the site starts another step. Topps requires potential winners to possess all 10 physical cards and verify ownership. Its rules give winners seven days to respond to the notification email and specify that the prize goes to the winner's Fanatics Collect Vault account.

That leaves two things to check before paying a chase premium for a Supernova. The card needs a usable code, and the player's autograph still needs to be available. Once that prize is claimed, the next copy is a Supernova for the collection.

"Having the ability to control and turn off, and make a decision and decide, I think that's really important for this next chapter of AI adoption," Chief Business Officer Daniel Bernard told CNBC.

A string of recent high-profile cyber incidents has added urgency to calls for some kind of emergency shutdown mechanism.

The Hugging Face breach orchestrated by rogue OpenAI agents in July was particularly alarming for businesses. Last week, Binance disclosed the largest crypto cyberattack on record and hackers have recently targeted hundreds of refrigerators and freezers at supermarkets in France.

Over the summer, U.S. representatives introduced bipartisan legislation that would require AI companies to build shutdown capabilities into their models.

Bernard said the kill switch operates like a circuit breaker in systems where customers use its Falcon platform. The process can detect internal agent activity but requires a human to decide to cut the agent off and reinstate access.
