Most identity theft online starts with a password. Someone reuses one, a site gets breached, a fake login page collects a few more, and suddenly a stranger is inside an email account resetting everything else.

Passkeys are the industry’s attempt to remove that weak link. Apple, Google, and Microsoft all support them now, and plenty of big sites already offer them at sign-in. But do they actually make identity theft harder, or do they just move the problem somewhere else?

The honest answer is a bit of both. This article looks at how passkeys work under the hood, which attacks they shut down, which ones they don’t touch, and what you need to get right if you’re adding them to your own site.

Passwords have one basic flaw. They’re a shared secret. You know it, the server knows it (or a hash of it), and anyone who gets a copy can use it from anywhere.

That one flaw feeds most of the account takeover attacks you hear about:

Two-factor authentication helps, but not as much as people think. SMS codes can be intercepted or stolen through SIM swaps. One-time codes from an app can still be phished, because a fake site can simply ask for the code and pass it along in real time.

A passkey is a pair of cryptographic keys made for one specific website. It’s built on the WebAuthn standard from the W3C and the FIDO Alliance.

Here’s the short version of what happens:

Nothing secret ever crosses the network, and nothing secret is stored on the server. That small change has big consequences.

This is the biggest win. Every passkey is tied to the domain it was created for. Your browser won’t use a passkey for example.com on examp1e-login.com, no matter how real the page looks. That protection only holds if the attacker can’t fall back to a password or a weak recovery path, which is why the rollout advice later in this article matters.

With a password, the user is the one who has to spot the fake. With a passkey, the browser checks the domain for them. People get tired and click bad links. The browser doesn’t.

Each passkey is unique to one site, so there’s nothing to reuse. An attacker can’t take a passkey from one breach and try it somewhere else, because there’s no shared value to try.

If an attacker dumps a site’s database, they get public keys. A public key can check a signature, but it can’t make one. So the stolen data can’t be used to sign in as anyone.

This doesn’t make a breach harmless (more on that below), but it does remove the part that usually leads straight to account takeovers.

This is where a lot of articles get too optimistic. Passkeys protect the login step. Identity theft is a much bigger problem than that.

Every site needs a way back in for people who lose their phone. If that recovery path is an SMS code, an email link, or a support agent who can be talked into resetting things, attackers will go after that instead.

A site with passkeys and a weak recovery flow is only as strong as the recovery flow. This is the part developers most often underestimate. The OWASP Forgot Password Cheat Sheet is a good checklist for hardening it.

A breach that exposes names, addresses, birth dates, or government ID numbers is still a breach. Criminals don’t need to log in to your accounts to open new ones in your name. They can apply for credit, file fake tax returns, or run scams with that data alone.

Passkeys do nothing about this kind of identity theft. It’s why credit monitoring, freezes, and alert services are still worth knowing about even when your logins are locked down. If you want a broader look at how those tools compare, these theft protection insights from Cybernews cover what different services actually monitor.

Someone can still call a person, pretend to be their bank, and talk them into approving something. Passkeys make that harder because there’s no code to read out loud, but scammers adapt. User education still matters.

Passkeys close off phishing, credential stuffing, and password database leaks, which are the most common ways criminals take over accounts. That alone makes them one of the most meaningful security upgrades the web has seen in years.

But identity theft is bigger than logins. Weak recovery flows, stolen sessions, and leaked personal data all still lead to real harm, and passkeys don’t fix any of them on their own.

If you’re building for the web, treat passkeys as a strong foundation, not the whole house. Add them, make recovery just as solid, keep your sessions protected, and collect as little personal data as you can. That combination does far more for your users than any single feature.

Analytics and error logging are where good intentions leak. A crash report that includes the full page URL may contain a session token in the query string. A “usage event” may capture page titles from internal tools.

The most secure approach is to collect only the data a feature needs and to process the data locally if possible. When data has to be sent, it should be stripped down to the absolute minimum. Remote code is another trap. Manifest V3 restricts remotely hosted code for a good reason. A script fetched at runtime can modify its behavior after the fact, and one can’t audit what it will do in the future. Bundle everything and the code that is reviewed will be identical to the code that is run.

A good extension today can become a risky one next year. Two patterns keep repeating.

Ownership changes. Popular extensions get sold, and the new owner ships an update with tracking or worse. The Great Suspender, a widely used tab tool, ended up flagged as malware after a change of hands.

Compromised publisher accounts. If an attacker phishes the developer account, they can push a malicious update to every existing user. Cyberhaven’s extension was hit by a phished administrator account in late December 2024.

The defenses are mostly boring, which is a good sign. Hardware-key two-factor authentication on the publisher account, a short list of people allowed to publish, and a review step before each release stops most of it. Lockfiles, pinned versions and regular dependency audits cover the code side.

Teams that only install extensions can apply the same thinking. Keep an approved list, review updates that change permissions, and remove anything nobody uses. Following security news is worthwhile too, since incidents involving popular extensions tend to be reported publicly.

It is only one layer, though. A VPN protects traffic in transit. It does nothing about an add-on that reads page content inside the browser, so it never replaces a proper permission review.

Simpler habits do more here. A separate browser profile for development keeps test extensions away from personal accounts. Fake or low-privilege accounts for testing avoid exposing real credentials. And installers for any tool, VPN clients included, should come from the official site or store, never from a mirror.

Safe extensions come down to restraint. Ask for less access, collect less data, send less off the device, and explain more to the user. Developers who treat every permission as a liability end up with extensions people can trust, and teams that review what they install avoid the worst surprises.

Think of a fingerprint as a stack of clues rather than a single value. Your time zone is shared by millions of people. So is your screen resolution. Put twenty clues like that together, though, and the crowd shrinks fast.

Here’s roughly where the clues come from.

HTTP headers. Before any JavaScript runs, every request already tells the server a lot. The User-Agent string and the Accept-Language header give away your browser, its version, your OS, and your languages. Chromium browsers also send Client Hints.

JavaScript APIs. Plenty of everyday APIs leak device details. screen.width and screen.height, navigator.hardwareConcurrency (your CPU core count), navigator.deviceMemory (Chromium only), navigator.platform, and your time zone all add something. Plugin lists used to be a goldmine, but modern browsers now return a fixed, generic list, so that one’s mostly dead.

Rendering. This is the clever bit. Ask two computers to draw the exact same text, and the pixels won’t match perfectly. Fonts, graphics drivers, and the GPU itself all nudge the output slightly. Same browser, same version, different hardware, different result.

Network details. Your IP address, connection type, and even the way your browser sets up a TLS connection can help. IPs change, so these clues are less reliable. Trackers mostly use them to back up a match they already suspect.

How unique does this make you? Back in 2010, the EFF’s Panopticlick study tested around 470,000 browsers and found that about 84% of them had a unique fingerprint. (The project still exists, now called Cover Your Tracks.) Later studies with bigger samples found lower numbers, especially on phones, since a lot of phones share near-identical hardware. The percentages move around, but the basic idea hasn’t changed.

Your third-party scripts can do it without asking you. Analytics tags, ad scripts, chat widgets, social buttons. Each of them can call the same APIs your own code can. Most JavaScript code loads data first and processes it afterward. That works for a list of ten items, but it falls apart when the list has a million entries, never ends, or arrives one network page at a time. Generators and iterators offer another approach: produce values only when someone asks for them. This article explains the iteration protocol, shows how generators build on it, and finishes with async generators for paginated data.

An iterator is any object with a next() method. Each call returns an object with two properties: value, the next item, and done, a boolean that becomes true when the sequence ends. An iterable is an object that hands out an iterator through a method keyed by Symbol.iterator. Arrays, strings, Maps and Sets are all iterables, which is why for...of, spread syntax and destructuring work on them.

Spread syntax calls [Symbol.iterator]() once, then calls next() until done is true. The code is correct but noisy. You manage the state yourself, and every new sequence needs the same scaffolding.

A generator function, declared with function*, returns an iterator without the manual bookkeeping. The yield keyword pauses the function and sends a value to the caller. The next call to next() resumes execution exactly where it stopped, with all local variables intact.

Calling countdown(3) does not run the body. It creates a generator object, and the body runs in slices as the consumer asks for values. Generator objects are iterable too, so spread and for...of work on them directly. The suspended state is the whole trick: the function body acts as a small state machine that the engine writes for you.

Others watch real users click around and then replay what they did. Playwright, for instance, now ships built-in test agents: a planner that explores your app and writes a test plan, a generator that turns the plan into test files, and a healer that runs the suite and tries to repair failing tests.

Old-school automation needs a person to write every script. Then a developer moves one button and the whole thing falls over. Somebody loses a morning to repairs, and QA engineers hate that part of the job.

Quality is the big worry. Generated tests can look clever and prove nothing. A tool might confirm that a checkout page opens and then mark checkout as covered, but opening a page is not testing it. Even Playwright's documentation says generated tests may contain initial errors and that you should review what the healer changes before accepting it. A person has to read the output every time.

Business logic is the next problem. The tool has no idea that your refund window dropped from 30 days to 14, and it cannot feel that a signup form is confusing. Intent lives in people's heads.

Coverage numbers can mislead. A dashboard says 90%, and the room relaxes. Meanwhile, the odd edge cases nobody thought of sit untouched. Bugs do not read dashboards.

Check fit first. Does it cover web, mobile and API tests, and does it work with your stack? Ask before any sales call, not after. Then test its honesty: when something fails, a good tool tells you why. A vague red mark costs your team time because nobody can verify it. Read the pricing page slowly, too, since some vendors charge per seat and some per test run.

Finally, throw your ugliest feature at it. Demos use tidy little apps, but your product has pop-ups, slow pages and odd corners. Watch how the tool handles those.

Expecting instant results. People switch the tool on and wait for magic. What they get is noisy reports and grumpy engineers. Some managers even cut QA headcount right away, which is a bad idea: somebody has to tune the tool and handle the cases it cannot. Good testers become more valuable, not less.

Skipping training. Your team needs to learn how the tool behaves. Two short workshops and a shared doc go a long way.

Trusting machine-written text without a second look. AI tools now draft test descriptions, bug reports and release notes too. If your team wants to know how much of a document was machine-generated, an AI-content checker such as ZeroGPT can give a rough signal. Treat the result as a hint rather than proof, because such detectors make mistakes and publish their own accuracy claims.

Agents are the obvious next step. Instead of running fixed scripts, they poke around your app like a curious user, clicking, typing and reporting anything strange. Early versions already exist, and they are rough but improving.

Editors will change as well. Your code editor may soon suggest a test the moment you finish a function, and some bug reports may arrive with a fix attached. The wall between development and QA is getting thin.

Yes, but carefully. AI testing tools will not replace your QA team. They take the boring work off testers' hands and spot patterns people might miss, but they still need a human to say "that result is wrong." Start small, measure what happens and keep your testers close to the tool. Do that, and you will ship faster without losing control.

#10601. AI, DevOps and the Cost of Hosting: From Idea to Production for $0

Have 30-200 Employees? Make $50k-$500k selling your data for AI Training.

Have 30-200 Employees? Make $50k-$500k selling your data for AI Training.

An AI coding agent rebuilt my photo-collage app as a distributed system and deployed it to a free server. The hard part turned out to be hardware: one free ARM CPU, 538 collages created during load testing, zero failed jobs, and $0 a month.

Like most developers, I have never been short of product ideas. Hosting them has always been the problem. I have tried shared hosting with SmartWeb, Azure free credits, Google Compute Engine’s free VM instances, SmartWeb’s virtual private servers, and Heroku dynos. None of them left me satisfied. The worst experience was a $200 charge for an AI address API on Google Cloud—for an application that never earned me a single kobo.

I am not a cheapskate, but I learned quickly that one of the easiest ways to kill a potentially good idea is to get the finances wrong. An application has to stay online until it reaches a critical mass of users, and hosting an untested, unverified product on expensive hardware can be wasteful.

Agentic AI coding has changed how quickly I can move from idea to production. Over about five weeks of intermittent coding—amounting to roughly three days of full work with Claude—my photo-stitching app went from an Angular 8 project that would not even build to a distributed system running in production.

Along the way, Claude upgraded the front end from Angular 8 to Angular 22 and fixed the build on Node 24. It set up RabbitMQ with Docker Compose so the message broker configures itself on startup, and added real-time notifications over SignalR with a progress spinner. It fixed a crash loop caused by an unreadable iPhone photo. It redesigned the system so any server can process any job, with switchable database and file storage, direct browser-to-storage uploads, and photos resized in the browser before upload. It added reliability work that a team might debate for a sprint: a dead-letter queue, confirmed publishing, idempotent workers, recovery of stuck jobs, and automatic clean-up. It fixed the stitching so photos keep their proportions and borders are even. It wrote 41 automated tests and scrubbed leaked passwords and personal photos from the Git history.

Coding, QA, DevOps, and production support used to be separate phases, often handled by separate people. Here they happened in one conversation. When code becomes this cheap to produce, the bottleneck moves. For me, it moved to hardware.

To check what the AI builds, you have to run it locally. A distributed app means running the whole system locally: a web app, a background worker, a message broker, a database, and storage emulators. Because Claude adds features quickly, and each one can add another container, it is easy to exhaust a laptop’s RAM and CPU.

My setup made this lesson concrete. I work on a MacBook Air, with Visual Studio in a Windows virtual machine. Docker cannot run inside that VM on Apple Silicon, so Claude ran Docker on the Mac and drove it from Windows over SSH. Later, my mobile connection started dropping large packets and broke uploads from the VM, so Claude moved the load testing onto the server itself. The software problems took minutes. The hardware and network problems took longer.

Then comes hosting. Heroku looked like the easy route, but it has had no free dynos since 2022. The cheapest option is an Eco dyno at about $5 a month, plus about $5 for Postgres. That is affordable, but running it for an idea that has no users yet did not seem prudent.

Claude suggested Oracle Cloud’s Always Free tier: an ARM server with up to 4 CPUs and 24 GB of RAM, free with no end date. I created a small instance with 1 CPU and about 6 GB. Claude wrote a production Docker Compose file and a one-command setup script: Caddy as the gateway, the web app, the stitching worker, RabbitMQ, and PostgreSQL, with data that survives restarts. My repository is private, so rather than put a GitHub token on a public server, it copied the code over SSH and deployed it. The last blocker was mine to fix: opening ports 80 and 443 in Oracle’s network firewall. Then the site was live.

I did not want to guess, so I asked Claude to load-test the live server. Simulated users ran the full flow: upload two photos, wait for the real-time notification, and download the stitched collage.

The server handled 64 people creating collages at the same moment without a single failed job: 538 collages, zero errors. Its ceiling was about 62 collages a minute—one per second. Beyond that, users queue. With 4 people creating at once, 95% of collages arrived within about five seconds; at 64, each person waited about a minute. If each active user makes a collage every couple of minutes, one CPU comfortably serves around 85 people at once, and a few hundred if they use it less often.

The test also showed what runs out first, and it is not the CPU. Each collage stores about 5 MB, mostly the PNG output, so at full speed the disk would fill in under two hours. Switching to JPEG and shortening retention fixes that, and when traffic grows, three more free CPUs are waiting. That is the value of measuring before paying: I know what to change, and when.

Oracle isn’t being charitable. Servers need applications to run—ideally ones that go viral—and the free tier is a play for loyalty. Oracle wants successful projects to stay and grow into its paid services instead of moving off the platform once they reach critical mass. It can also reclaim free instances that sit idle.

I think the bet is smart, and it points to a bigger trend. AI-generated code still needs energy to run and hardware to host it, even when far fewer people work on each project. As more people turn ideas into working software, demand for compute will keep growing, and I expect hosting and data centres to become a very profitable business.

For now, the combination works: Claude orchestrates the coding, QA, and DevOps, and Oracle’s free server keeps an idea online long enough to find out whether it deserves to grow.

#10599. Mayor wins “really weird” election after name gets picked out of bag

A Minnesota mayor has been declared the winner of an election after his name was randomly pulled from a bag, breaking a tie between two candidates who received exactly the same number of votes.

The unusual process took place in Spring Lake Park, a suburb of Minneapolis, after incumbent Mayor Robert Nelson and challenger Halyn Roth each received 1,401 votes in the November 3 election.

With neither candidate securing a victory at the ballot box, city officials turned to a method permitted under Minnesota law to determine the winner. However, the result could still change following a requested recount.

Nelson and Roth attended a special city council meeting on November 9, where officials prepared to break the tie by drawing lots.

City administrator Daniel Buchholtz explained that he had folded pieces of paper containing each candidate’s name in the same way before placing them inside identical film canisters.

The canisters were then placed inside a blue fabric grocery bag, prompting Nelson to joke, “Got a rabbit in there?”

“I’m a city administrator, not a magician,” Buchholtz replied.

City council member Barbara Goodboe-Bisschoff was tasked with selecting a canister from the bag, which she handed back to Buchholtz to reveal the winning name.

Nelson’s name was inside, allowing the incumbent mayor to retain his position, pending the outcome of a recount.

According to Minnesota Public Radio, Goodboe-Bisschoff wasn’t aware she would be responsible for the drawing until she arrived at the meeting. She said Nelson had avoided telling her beforehand because he was concerned she might not attend due to nerves.
