#10764. How Motion Design Improves SaaS Onboarding and Feature Adoption

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

Most SaaS products don't lose users because the software is bad. They lose users in the first few minutes, before anyone has seen what the product can actually do. Someone signs up, lands on an empty dashboard, feels a flicker of confusion, and quietly leaves. The industry has a name for the gap between signing up and getting value: activation. Closing it is one of the hardest problems in product design.

Motion design is one of the most underused tools for closing that gap. Used well, animation isn't decoration sprinkled on at the end. It's a layer of communication that tells users where to look, confirms that their actions worked, and makes a complex interface feel learnable instead of overwhelming. This article looks at why motion influences behaviour during onboarding, the specific patterns that move the needle on activation and feature adoption, and how to build them responsibly as a developer — with an eye on performance and accessibility throughout.

Motion works in onboarding because it speaks to how human perception actually operates, not because it looks modern. A few principles do most of the heavy lifting.

It directs attention. The eye is drawn to movement before it reads a single word. A gently pulsing button or an element that slides into view tells a new user where to go next without a paragraph of instructions. On a crowded first-run screen, that guidance is the difference between a confident first action and a confused exit.

It preserves context. When a panel expands, a card flips, or a new view slides in from the right, a short transition shows users where things came from and where they went. This spatial continuity keeps people oriented. Interfaces that snap instantly between states force the user to re-scan the screen each time and silently rebuild their mental model.

It confirms actions. Every click, toggle, and form submission is a small question: did that work? A quick visual response — a checkmark drawing itself, a row settling into place — answers it immediately. Without that feedback, users repeat actions, doubt the system, or assume something broke.

It shapes perceived performance. A thoughtful loading animation or skeleton screen makes a wait feel shorter and the product feel responsive, even when the underlying request takes exactly as long. Perception of speed is part of the experience, and motion is how you manage it.

These are the patterns that reliably help new users reach their first win.

Onboarding doesn't end after the first session. Most SaaS products keep shipping features, and each one faces its own adoption problem: users settle into a few familiar workflows and never discover the rest. Motion is how you reintroduce the product to people who already think they know it.

Contextual nudges that appear in motion — a tooltip that eases in near a newly relevant button, a gentle highlight on a feature the moment a user hits the limit it solves — are far more effective than a generic email. They arrive at the point of need and use movement to pull attention without hijacking the screen.

"What's new" reveals can announce an update with a small, well-timed animation rather than a modal that gets dismissed on reflex. A badge that animates in, or a panel that slides open on the first visit to a page, respects the user's flow while still being noticed.

Animated demonstrations carry the most weight for genuinely complex features. Showing a feature in motion — rather than describing it in text — lets users grasp a multi-step workflow in seconds. For intricate capabilities, this is where a short, purpose-made explainer earns its place, a point we return to in the next section.

CSS transitions and keyframe animations should be your default. They cover the large majority of onboarding motion — hovers, state changes, highlights, progress bars — and the browser can run them efficiently. Reach for JavaScript only when CSS genuinely can't express what you need.

The Web Animations API gives you CSS-level performance with programmatic control, which matters when animations depend on data or user input, or need to be sequenced and interrupted. SitePoint's guide to the Web Animations API is a solid introduction, and the broader web animation archive covers libraries like GSAP for orchestrating complex timelines.

Lottie is the bridge between designer-made animation and the web. Designers export vector animations as JSON, and a lightweight player renders them natively at small file sizes — ideal for polished illustrative moments like success states and animated empty states, without hand-coding every frame.

Pre-rendered video is the right call when a feature is genuinely involved and a looping CSS animation or a tooltip simply can't carry the explanation. A short, well-produced walkthrough can compress a multi-step workflow into something a user absorbs in under a minute, which is why many teams lean on a dedicated SaaS explainer video for their most complex features and onboarding moments. The trade-off is weight and production effort, so reserve video for the handful of places where it genuinely outperforms in-interface motion, lazy-load it, and never autoplay it with sound.

Motion that stutters does more damage than no motion at all, and motion that ignores accessibility can actively harm users. Two disciplines keep animation on the right side of that line.

For performance, animate the properties the browser can handle cheaply: transform and opacity. These can run on the compositor thread without triggering layout or paint, which is what lets animation hold a smooth 60fps. Animating properties like width, height, top, or left forces the browser to recalculate layout on every frame and is the usual cause of janky motion. Keep animations short — most interface transitions should land between 150 and 300 milliseconds — because motion that overstays its welcome makes a product feel slow.

Beyond that, never let an animation block interaction. If a user has to wait for a flourish to finish before they can click, the motion is working against them. Animation should support the task and then get out of the way.

Motion design is a product decision, so treat it like one and measure it. The metrics that matter map directly to the problems motion is meant to solve.

Treat animations as hypotheses and A/B test them. Ship the animated onboarding step to half of your new users and measure activation against the control. Motion should earn its place with data, not assumptions — and occasionally the data will tell you an animation is slowing people down, which is just as useful to know.

The best onboarding motion is the kind users never consciously notice. It simply makes the product feel clear, responsive, and easy to learn — guiding attention, confirming actions, and turning a complex interface into something approachable. That's the real payoff: not animation for its own sake, but fewer confused users, faster time to value, and features that actually get discovered.

Start small. Pick the single point in your onboarding where users most often stall, add one purposeful animation that removes the confusion, and measure what happens. Motion, used deliberately, is one of the most direct levers you have on activation and adoption — and like any good tool, it works best when it draws no attention to itself.

This is the least glamorous part of building with LLMs and it's where a lot of agent projects quietly fall over. The model is rarely the problem. The fetch is. In this article we'll look at why live web retrieval has become so unreliable for AI tools, what the numbers say about the shift, and how to build a small, well-behaved fetch layer in Python that fails loudly instead of feeding your model garbage.

A few years ago, a plain requests.get() worked on most of the public web. That's no longer true, and the reason is simple: there are now more bots than people.

According to the 2025 Imperva Bad Bot Report, automated traffic made up 51% of all web traffic in 2024. That's the first time in a decade it has passed human activity. Bad bots alone (scrapers, credential stuffers, scalpers) went from 32% to 37% of traffic in a single year.

Source: Imperva Bad Bot Report 2024 and 2025

From a site owner's point of view, your polite research agent and a credential-stuffing botnet look much the same at the network edge. Both are non-browser clients making fast requests from data-center IP ranges. So the defaults have tightened. In July 2025 Cloudflare began blocking known AI crawlers by default for new customers, and bot management products now look at TLS fingerprints, header order, IP reputation and JavaScript execution, not just the User-Agent string.

It helps to understand the publisher's side of the deal, because it explains why your agent is being treated with suspicion.

Cloudflare splits AI crawling into three purposes: training (collecting data to train models), search (building an index for AI search answers) and user action (fetching a page because a person just asked a question). Training dominates.

Training crawls take content and send nothing back. Cloudflare measures this with a crawl-to-referral ratio: how many pages a platform crawls for every visitor it sends to the site. In its crawl-to-click gap analysis, the ratios for some AI platforms ran into the tens of thousands, compared with single digits for traditional search.

If you ran a publication and saw those numbers, you'd block too. The catch is that blocking is usually done by user agent or IP range, so it hits user-action fetches as well. Those are the ones that happen when a person asks your agent to read a specific article, and Cloudflare's 2025 Year in Review found they grew more than 15x during the year. Your agent is caught in a fight it didn't start.

Although attribute selectors are flexible, they should not replace every class-based styling decision.

A selector such as [class*="button"] can match unintended elements if their class names happen to contain the same text. Similarly, broad attribute rules may affect components that were not originally intended to receive those styles.

For predictable results, use exact attribute matching whenever possible and prefer descriptive classes for major component styling.

Developers should also remember that CSS selectors only control presentation. They do not replace JavaScript validation, server-side security checks, or proper HTML semantics.

They are particularly useful for form styling, link identification, and components that already contain meaningful HTML attributes. #10729. SEO For AI Crawlers: How to serve AI Agent friendly pages?

AI-friendly pages are plain-text (usually markdown) versions of webpages that are meant for AI, rather than people. Websites typically determine that AI is requesting a page via the user agent, Accept header, or a specific .md URL.

We ran this test at acme.bot on November 4, 2026, across 53 live pages on 43 host names. Because we targeted sites likely to do this, the sample leans towards developer tools.

While testing the HubSpot, Gymshark, Semrush and Profound web pages, we didn't find any markdown on the pages.

While it's a common practice in developer documentation, there's not much research or evidence that it helps AI visibility.

Dries Buytaert checked a month of Cloudflare logs and found no AI crawler using content negotiation. The crawlers reached his markdown only through dedicated URLs, by following an auto-discovery link. So a site that relies on Accept alone is serving coding agents - and, possibly, no crawlers at all.

Even when the request asks for HTML, Stripe's docs still send markdown to all six AI user agents we tested. They send HTML to Bingbot, Googlebot, and browsers. The Checkout page drops from 846 KB to 5.5 KB. Its markdown has links to other .md pages, keeping an agent in the text pages. The response to markdown comes with the header X-Robots-Tag: none to ensure it is kept out of search indexes. Sentry's docs route the same way.

Vercel pages (including the blog and pricing page) that we tried all returned markdown on request. Docs pages also go to AI user agents, unless the request prefers HTML. Pricing and blog pages do not. The Next.js docs exhibit a similar behavior.

Neon delivers markdown on docs and pricing to non-browser clients that do not prefer HTML, including plain curl.

Neon's page isn't just a conversion. The markdown copy of its introduction is about 3,070 words, against roughly 620 in the visible HTML, and it speaks directly to the agent: "The user needs a Neon account." This sentence, however, is not in the HTML. Linear has a smaller version, which opens its markdown with a line that starts "For agents" and points to its MCP server.

Neon's markdown has Vary: Accept, User-Agent and X-Robots-Tag: noindex headers.

Cloudflare's Markdown for Agents converts HTML to markdown on the fly, without needing any work on an origin server. It's available on the Pro plan and above and handles up to 6 MiB of HTML. Cloudflare reports one post falling from 16,180 tokens to 3,150.

1. Fix the HTML first. Vercel's guide says it plainly: "Make the HTML work before adding a Markdown mirror." Render the content on the server, because many AI crawlers don't run JS.

2. Generate markdown from the same source as the HTML. That keeps the two versions in step when the page changes.

3. Answer Accept: text/markdown. Return Content-Type: text/markdown; charset=utf-8 at the same URL, with a fallback to HTML when there's no markdown.

4. Publish .md URLs and link to them. Each page should have a added to it. In Buytaert's logs, this is the route the crawlers took.

5. Add user agent detection only if you need it. Identify the agents you want to serve, and omit search engines. Should the list include training crawlers? Stripe's does and Mintlify's doesn't. Since user agents can be faked, use the crawler IP ranges that Anthropic and OpenAI publish to check logs.

6. Set the cache key. Send Vary: Accept, and also User-Agent if it determines the response. Otherwise, a shared cache can give a person the markdown.

7. Keep the markdown out of search results. Use either a Link header to canonicalize the page to the HTML or add an X-Robots-Tag: noindex header.

8. Log the Accept header. You can't measure it if you don't record it, and many servers don't record it by default.

The evidence of benefit is thin, and it measures crawling, not citations.

Google's John Mueller called converting pages "such a stupid idea". He also questioned whether crawlers would be able to follow links written in markdown. Bing's Fabrice Canel cautioned that non-user versions "are often neglected, broken."

Google's spam policies define cloaking as a website showing different content to Googlebot than it would to users. In our controlled test, all sites kept Googlebot on HTML. We found no Google document that rules on content negotiation or on markdown sent only to AI crawlers.

If your site is developer documentation, making you site agent friendly is cheap, and your readers' coding agents already ask for it. So, answer Accept: text/markdown requests and publish linked .md URLs. Keep both out of the index.

For a marketing site, the case is not that strong. No study yet shows more AI citations, and maintaining two copies of every page over time will be an overhead.

If citations are the goal, freshness looks like a stronger lever than format. In our study of 356 cited pages for getting cited by ChatGPT, typical article was two years old - but seven in ten had been edited in the last year.

Here is a question a product page should answer in about five seconds: “Will this magnetic phone mount hold my phone with the case still on?” When the answer is hard to find, shoppers tend to open another tab instead of contacting support.

Stores that sell both car accessories and home & kitchen products face an extra challenge: two very different catalogs share one site. A car accessories buyer cares about fit, mounting and compatibility. A home & kitchen buyer cares about size, capacity and how easy the product is to clean. A single generic product page frustrates both.

This guide covers seven practical e-commerce UX tips that designers and developers can apply right away, with code snippets and a checklist at the end.

A car mount and a vegetable peeler should not share the same spec table. Keep a single reusable product page component and change only the fields based on category. The layout stays consistent, while each shopper sees the details they need.

Shoppers look for deal-breaker information near the price and the purchase button: fit, size and compatibility. Do not bury it in an “Additional information” tab halfway down the page.

A short comparison table also works better than three paragraphs of marketing copy, because shoppers scan rather than read. Baymard Institute’s product page research consistently finds that unclear or hard-to-find specifications are a common reason shoppers hesitate.

A responsive layout is the starting point, not the finish line. People compare car accessories in a parking lot and browse kitchen gadgets while standing in the kitchen, often with one thumb.

A phone mount on a white background says nothing about how it attaches to a dashboard. Show it in a real car, from the driver’s point of view. For home & kitchen products, place the item next to something familiar, like a coffee mug, so people can judge its size.

Shoppers often search for a problem rather than a product. Few people type “seat gap filler” the first time; they type “stuff falling between car seats”. Your search, synonyms and category names should handle both.

Mixed catalogs show why this matters. Stores that cover both vehicle and household products, such as VNSPORT.org with its separate product collections, keep the two audiences apart so that someone looking for a dashboard mount never has to scroll past kitchen tools. What are the most important e-commerce UX tips for a car accessories store? Show compatibility and mounting details next to the price, use in-car photos, and make sure the mobile layout works with one hand.

How should product pages differ for car accessories and home & kitchen products? Use the same layout but different spec fields. Car accessories need fit, mounting and power details. Home & kitchen products need dimensions, capacity, materials and cleaning instructions.

How big should buttons be on a mobile product page? WCAG 2.2 sets a minimum target size of 24 × 24 CSS pixels. For primary actions such as “Add to cart”, around 44 pixels is more comfortable.

Do FAQ sections still help SEO? Mostly indirectly. FAQ rich results are now limited, but clear on-page answers can match long-tail searches, reduce returns and keep shoppers on the page.

How can I reduce cart abandonment? Show total costs early, offer guest checkout, display delivery times and return terms before payment, and keep the checkout form short.

Good e-commerce UX is not about clever animations. It is about answering the shopper’s question before they have to ask it. Clear specs, honest photos, thumb-friendly controls and real FAQs turn an ordinary product page into one that helps people buy, whether they are shopping for car accessories or home & kitchen essentials.

A one-hour YouTube talk contains roughly nine thousand words, and finding the one point you need can take longer than watching the whole thing. A transcript solves that. With text in hand you can search for a phrase, quote an expert accurately, turn a webinar into a blog post, create subtitles or feed a summary tool.

Plenty of tools now promise instant YouTube transcripts, but they work in different ways. Some read the captions YouTube already has. Others listen to the audio themselves and produce a fresh transcript. That difference affects accuracy, speed and cost more than any feature list does. This guide compares six options, explains how each works and points out where each one falls short. Plans and limits change often, so confirm current details on each vendor's site.

Caption extraction. The tool pulls the captions attached to a video, either written by the creator or generated automatically by YouTube. It is fast and usually free, but it is only as good as those captions. YouTube notes that its automatic captions come from machine learning, so quality may vary, and accents, background noise and overlapping speakers can cause errors. Captions may also be missing entirely for some videos.

Fresh speech recognition. The tool takes the audio and runs its own speech-to-text model. This can be more accurate on difficult recordings and works when no captions exist, but it takes longer and often costs money.

Lynote is built for exactly this job. Paste a YouTube link, search by keyword or add a playlist, and it returns a timestamped transcript with an AI summary. Batch mode accepts up to 10 videos at once. The tool lists transcription in more than 100 languages, translation into 25, auto-chapters, one-click copy and TXT download, with SRT export also mentioned. The page says transcripts are free, with no sign-up required.

Best for: Students, researchers and marketers who want a quick, readable transcript or summary from a public video without installing anything.

Limitations: It extracts YouTube captions, so videos without captions cannot be processed. If the original captions are poor, the transcript will be too. For critical accuracy, review the text or use a service with human verification.

Before you install anything, check what YouTube already gives you. Many videos show a transcript option in the description area, and creators can review and edit automatic captions in YouTube Studio under Subtitles. YouTube offers automatic captions in dozens of languages for long-form videos and Shorts.

Best for: Quick lookups on videos that already have decent captions.

Limitations: Captions may not generate for videos with poor sound, long silences, overlapping speakers, mixed languages or unsupported languages. Export options are minimal, and there is no summary or cleanup.

Best for: Professionals who handle a mix of recordings, meetings and YouTube videos in one place.

Limitations: The free minutes disappear quickly if you process long videos, and the strongest features sit behind paid plans.

Descript is an audio and video editor built around text. It transcribes recordings automatically, and deleting a word in the transcript removes the matching footage. You can import video, audio or a Zoom recording, and there is a free tier that does not require a credit card.

Best for: Creators who want to clean up their own videos and podcasts, not just read a transcript.

Limitations: The cost climbs for long videos, and the human option is slower than instant AI tools. Those accuracy figures are the vendor's own claims, so test your own audio.

Whisper is OpenAI's open-source speech recognition model, released under the MIT license. It handles multilingual transcription, translation into English and language identification. You install it with pip and need ffmpeg, and it comes in several model sizes from tiny to large, so you can trade speed against accuracy and hardware demands.

Best for: Developers building their own pipeline and teams with sensitive audio they prefer to keep on their own machines.

Limitations: You supply the audio, which means downloading it yourself and respecting the video owner's rights and YouTube's terms. Larger models need a capable GPU, and there is no built-in interface for non-technical users.

Start with the source. If the video already has good captions, a link-based tool is the fastest route. If the audio is difficult or the stakes are high, pick a tool that runs its own recognition or offers human review. If you are processing hundreds of videos or handling private recordings, look at Whisper or a paid platform with bulk features.

Whatever you choose, treat the transcript as a first draft. Check names, numbers and technical terms against the video before you publish or quote anything, and credit the original creator.

There is no single best transcript generator. Caption-based tools are quick and free, recognition-based tools are better when captions fall short, and human-verified services make sense when accuracy matters most. Try two or three on a video you know well, compare the results and keep a human in the loop for anything you publish.

Can I get a transcript if a video has no captions? Caption-extraction tools cannot. You need a tool that runs its own speech recognition on the audio, such as the options above that transcribe files.

Are AI transcripts accurate enough to publish? Often they are close, but not perfect. Accents, noise and technical terms cause mistakes, so review before publishing.

Is it legal to transcribe someone else's YouTube video? Transcribing for personal research or accessibility is common, but republishing the text may infringe copyright. Check the video's license and YouTube's terms, and get permission when in doubt.

What is the difference between a transcript and subtitles? A transcript is the full text, while subtitles are timed text shown on screen. Many tools can export both, often as SRT files.

Static website hosting is a service that stores and delivers pre-built website files, including HTML, CSS, JavaScript, images, and fonts.

Unlike traditional server-rendered websites, static sites don't need a server to generate each page when someone visits. The HTML is already prepared and can be delivered directly to the visitor's browser.

That doesn't mean static websites can't be interactive. JavaScript can still handle navigation, forms, filtering, and other features. Static sites can also communicate with external APIs or databases.

The main distinction is how pages are generated.

#10687. A Practical Introduction to the Twitch Helix API

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

Behind almost every Twitch tool you have ever seen, the stat trackers, the moderation bots, the stream overlays, the alert systems, sits one interface: the Twitch Helix API. Twitch is unusually generous with public data, and Helix is the door to it. If you have ever wondered how third-party sites know who is live, what a channel's viewership looks like, or details a Twitch profile page does not even display, this is the machinery.

This article is a practical overview of Helix for developers who have not used it yet: what it offers, how its authentication works, what people build with it, and where the sharp edges are.

Helix is Twitch's current REST API, served from api.twitch.tv/helix. It replaced the old "Kraken" API, which was fully retired in 2022, so any tutorial still showing Kraken endpoints is a sign to keep scrolling. Helix covers users, streams, channels, games and categories, clips, chat, moderation actions, subscriptions, and events, with JSON in and JSON out.

Two things distinguish it from many platform APIs. First, a large share of it works without any user's permission, because the data is public by Twitch's own definition. Second, it is paired with EventSub, a push system that notifies your application when things happen (a stream goes live, a user follows, a message arrives) instead of forcing you to poll. Most serious Twitch tools use both: Helix for lookups, EventSub for live events.

Authentication is where most newcomers stall, mainly because Twitch offers several OAuth flows and tutorials rarely say which one you actually need. The short version:

Every request needs a Client ID, which you get by registering an application in the Twitch developer console (two-factor authentication on your account is required first). Requests carry the Client ID and a bearer token together; forgetting the Client ID header is the classic mystery 401.

App access tokens come from the client credentials flow, a single server-to-server request with your ID and secret. They have no user attached and that is fine, because public reads, user profiles, live streams, top games, clips, need no user context. For most read-only tools, this is the only flow you ever touch.

Tokens expire, so real applications cache them with their reported lifetime and refresh on a 401 rather than assuming a token lives forever.

A tour of the endpoints that power most tools:

Users. Give the users endpoint a username and it returns the account's public record: display name, profile image, description, Affiliate or Partner status, and the account's exact creation timestamp. That last field is interesting because Twitch does not display it anywhere on a profile, yet it is one of the platform's most useful trust signals, moderators use account age to spot throwaway accounts, and streamers use it to filter giveaway entries. The creation date alone powers a small subcategory of tools: free account age checkers, such as Twitch account age checker, are essentially this one endpoint with a clean page around it, taking any public username and returning the creation date, age, avatar, and broadcaster status. It is a good illustration of how far a single well-chosen Helix field can go.

Streams. The stream's endpoint reports who is live right now, with titles, categories, thumbnail URLs, and viewer counts. This is the backbone of every "is my favorite streamer live" notifier and every directory-style site, and polling it over time is how stat trackers chart viewership history.

Games and categories. Top games, category search, and metadata. Combined with streams data, this is how analytics sites rank categories and how tools answer "how crowded is this category right now."

Clips and videos. Clip metadata, view counts, and creation tools. Highlight compilers and clip-sharing bots live here.

Helix enforces a points-based rate limit per Client ID, and every response tells you where you stand through Ratelimit-Limit, Ratelimit-Remaining, and Ratelimit-Reset headers. Well-behaved tools read those headers and back off before hitting zero; badly behaved ones retry 429s in a loop and wonder why things get worse. For lookup-style tools the practical fix is caching, data like account creation dates never changes, so there is no reason to ask twice.

A few more edges worth knowing before they bite:

Usernames change, IDs do not. Store user IDs, not logins, or your saved data will eventually point at the wrong person.

Missing users are not errors. Looking up a nonexistent username returns a successful response with an empty data array, so handle the empty case explicitly.

Batching exists. The users endpoint takes up to 100 logins in one call, which matters the moment your tool checks lists instead of individuals.

Secrets stay server-side. The client secret identifies your application; calling the token endpoint from browser code hands your app's identity to anyone who opens devtools. Public-facing tools put a small server between the browser and Twitch.

The reason Twitch's third-party ecosystem is so large is that the barrier is so low: one public API, generous free access, and a platform where small questions genuinely matter to people. That is why the space is full of narrow tools that each answer one thing well, when was this account created, is this channel live, how fast is this category growing, which clips popped this week. Most of them are a weekend of work on top of one or two endpoints, and some of them grow into real products.

If you want to start, the path is short: register an application at the developer console, request an app access token, and call the users or streams endpoint for something you actually care about. The official documentation at dev.twitch.tv is current and well maintained, which is not something every platform can claim. Pick one piece of data Twitch collects but does not display, put a clean interface on it, and you are following the same recipe as most of the Twitch tools you already use.

#10640. This new chip survives 1300°F (700°C) and could change AI forever

Modern electronics power everything from smartphones to satellites, but they all share a major limitation. Heat. Once temperatures climb above roughly 200 degrees Celsius, most devices begin to break down. For decades, this thermal barrier has been one of the toughest challenges in engineering.

Researchers at the University of Southern California now believe they have found a way past that limit.

In a study published on March 26, 2026 in Science, a team led by Joshua Yang, Arthur B. Freeman Chair Professor at the Ming Hsieh Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the USC Viterbi School of Engineering and the USC School of Advanced Computing, unveiled a new type of memory device that continues to operate at 700 degrees Celsius (~1300 degrees Fahrenheit). That temperature exceeds molten lava and goes far beyond anything previously achieved for this class of technology. The device showed no sign of failure. In fact, 700 degrees was simply the maximum their equipment could test.

"You may call it a revolution," Yang said. "It is the best high-temperature memory ever demonstrated."

The new device is known as a memristor, a nanoscale component that can both store data and perform computations. It is constructed like a microscopic layered structure, with two electrodes on either side and a thin ceramic layer in between.

Jian Zhao, the study's first author, built the device using tungsten for the top electrode, hafnium oxide ceramic in the middle, and graphene for the bottom layer. Tungsten has the highest melting point of any element, while graphene, a single-atom-thick sheet of carbon, is known for its exceptional strength and heat resistance.

This combination produced remarkable performance. The device retained data for more than 50 hours at 700 degrees without needing to be refreshed. It also endured over one billion switching cycles at that temperature and operated at just 1.5 volts with speeds measured in tens of nanoseconds.

The discovery was not part of the team's original plan. They were initially attempting to create a different graphene-based device, which did not work as intended. Along the way, they encountered something surprising.

"To be honest, it was by accident, as most discoveries are," Yang said. "If you can predict it, it's usually not surprising, and probably not significant enough."

Further investigation revealed why the device performed so well. In conventional electronics, heat causes metal atoms in the top electrode to slowly migrate through the ceramic layer. Eventually, they reach the bottom electrode, creating a permanent connection that short-circuits the device and leaves it stuck in the on state.

Graphene prevents this failure. Its interaction with tungsten is, as Yang described it, similar to oil and water. Tungsten atoms that approach the graphene surface cannot attach to it. Without a stable point to settle, they drift away instead of forming a conductive bridge. This prevents short circuits and preserves the device's function even under extreme heat.

The researchers confirmed this mechanism using advanced electron microscopy, spectroscopy, and quantum-level simulations. By understanding what happens at the atomic interface, they have turned an unexpected result into a principle that could guide future designs. Other materials with similar surface properties could be identified, which may help scale the technology for industrial production.

Electronics capable of operating above 500 degrees Celsius have long been a goal for space exploration. Venus, for example, has a surface temperature around that level, and every lander sent there has failed in part due to extreme heat. Current silicon-based chips cannot survive such conditions.

"We are now above 700 degrees, and we suspect it will go higher," Yang said.

The potential applications go far beyond space missions. Geothermal energy systems require electronics that can function deep underground, where surrounding rock can glow red-hot. Nuclear and fusion systems also expose equipment to intense heat. Even in everyday settings, durability improves significantly. A device rated for 700 degrees would be extremely robust at the roughly 125-degree temperatures often reached inside automotive electronics.

Why It Matters for Artificial Intelligence

In addition to storing data, the device offers a major advantage for artificial intelligence. Many AI systems rely heavily on matrix multiplication, a mathematical operation used in tasks like image recognition and language processing. Traditional computers perform these calculations step by step, consuming large amounts of energy.

Memristors approach the problem differently. By using Ohm's Law, where voltage times conductance equals current, the device performs calculations directly as electricity flows through it. The result is obtained instantly as the measured current.

"Over 92 percent of the computing in AI systems like ChatGPT is nothing but matrix multiplication," Yang said. "This type of device can perform that in the most efficient way, orders of magnitude faster and at lower energy."

Yang and three co-authors of the study (Qiangfei Xia, Miao Hu, and Ning Ge) have already co-founded a company called TetraMem to commercialize memristor-based AI chips at room temperature. Their lab is already using working chips from TetraMem for machine learning tasks. The high-temperature version described in this research could extend those capabilities to environments where traditional electronics cannot operate, allowing devices such as spacecraft or industrial sensors to process data directly on site.

From Lab Prototype to Real-World Technology

Despite the promising results, Yang emphasizes that practical applications are still some distance away. Memory is only one part of a complete computing system. High-temperature logic circuits will also need to be developed and integrated. In addition, the current devices were built manually at very small scales in a laboratory setting, so manufacturing at scale will take time.

"This is the first step," Yang said. "It's still a long way to go. But logically, you can see: now it makes it possible. The missing component has been made."

From a manufacturing perspective, two of the materials used in the device, tungsten and hafnium oxide, are already widely used in semiconductor production. Graphene is newer but is actively being developed by major companies such as TSMC and Samsung, and it has already been produced at wafer scale in research environments.

The work was conducted through the CONCRETE Center, short for Center of Neuromorphic Computing under Extreme Environments, a multi-university Center of Excellence led by USC and supported by the Air Force Office of Scientific Research and the Air Force Research Laboratory. Key experimental work was carried out in collaboration with Dr. Sabyasachi Ganguli's team at the AFRL Materials Lab in Dayton, Ohio, while theoretical analysis involved USC researchers and collaborators at Kumamoto University in Japan.

For Yang, publication in Science reflects more than a single achievement.

"Space exploration has never been so real, so close, and at such a large scale," he said. "This paper represents a critical leap into a much larger, more exciting frontier."

#10637. This new chip could slash data center energy waste

As data centers consume more energy to support growing digital demands, engineers at the University of California San Diego have introduced a new chip design that could make powering graphics processing units (GPUs) more efficient. The innovation focuses on a key function in electronics: converting high voltages into the lower levels required by computing hardware. In laboratory testing, a prototype chip successfully performed this type of voltage conversion with high efficiency under conditions similar to those found in modern data centers.

The findings, published in Nature Communications, suggest the potential for smaller and more energy-efficient systems in advanced computing environments.

Rethinking DC-DC Converters for Modern Electronics

At the center of the new design is an improved version of a widely used component known as a DC-DC step-down converter. These converters are found in nearly all electronic devices and serve as a critical link between power sources and sensitive circuits. Their job is to take a high incoming voltage and reduce it to the exact level needed for safe operation.

In data centers, electricity is often distributed at 48 volts, while GPU processors typically require much lower voltages, usually between 1 and 5 volts. Efficiently managing this large voltage drop has become increasingly challenging as computing systems grow more powerful and compact.

Limits of Traditional Power Conversion Technology

Conventional step-down converters often struggle when dealing with large differences between input and output voltage. As that gap increases, efficiency drops and it becomes harder to supply enough current. Most existing designs rely on magnetic components such as inductors. While these components have been refined over many years, they are approaching their practical limits and are becoming harder to improve further.

"We've gotten so good at designing inductive converters that there's not really much room left to improve them to meet future needs," said study senior author Patrick Mercier, professor in the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at the UC San Diego Jacobs School of Engineering.

Exploring Piezoelectric Resonators as an Alternative

To move beyond these limitations, Mercier and his team, including first author Jae-Young Ko, an electrical and computer engineering Ph.D. student at UC San Diego, investigated a different approach using piezoelectric resonators. These small devices store and transfer energy through mechanical vibrations rather than magnetic fields.

Converters based on piezoelectric components could offer several advantages. They have the potential to be smaller, more energy dense, more efficient, and easier to manufacture at scale. "They have a lot of room to grow and have the potential to deliver better performance than anything that's come before them," Mercier said.

However, earlier versions of piezoelectric converters have had difficulty maintaining efficiency and delivering sufficient power when handling large voltage differences.

Hybrid Design Achieves High Efficiency and Power Output

To overcome these issues, the researchers created a hybrid converter that combines a piezoelectric resonator with small, commercially available capacitors arranged in a carefully designed configuration. This setup enables the system to handle larger voltage conversions more effectively.

The team incorporated this design into a prototype chip and tested its performance. The device successfully converted 48 volts down to 4.8 volts -- a level commonly required in data centers -- with a peak efficiency of 96.2 percent. It also delivered roughly four times more output current than previous piezoelectric-based designs.

This hybrid approach offers several benefits. It creates multiple pathways for energy to move through the system, reduces wasted power, and lessens the strain on the resonator. Together, these improvements enhance both efficiency and power delivery while only slightly increasing the chip's size.

Challenges and Next Steps for Real-World Use

Although the technology shows strong promise, it is still in the early stages of development. The researchers view it as an important step toward overcoming the constraints of current power conversion systems. Future efforts will focus on refining materials, improving circuit designs, and developing better packaging methods.

One challenge is that piezoelectric resonators physically vibrate, which means they cannot be attached to circuit boards using standard soldering techniques. New integration strategies will be needed to incorporate them into electronic systems, Mercier explained.

"Piezoelectric-based converters aren't quite ready to replace existing power converter technologies yet," Mercier added. "But they offer a trajectory for improvement. We need to continue to improve on multiple areas -- materials, circuits and packaging -- to make this technology ready for data center applications."

#10627. This simple change stops robot swarms from getting stuck

Imagine a swarm of robots rushing to complete an urgent job, such as cleaning up an oil spill or assembling complex machinery. At first, adding more robots speeds things up. But after a certain point, the space becomes crowded, robots start interfering with one another, and overall progress slows.

This raises a simple but important question: in a limited area, how many robots can you deploy before efficiency starts to drop? Researchers at Harvard believe they have found a clear answer.

A new study from the lab of L. Mahadevan, the Lola England de Valpine Professor of Applied Mathematics, Organismic and Evolutionary Biology, and Physics, shows that adding a controlled amount of randomness to how robots move can reduce congestion and improve performance in crowded environments.

The work combines mathematical modeling, computer simulations, and real-world experiments. It demonstrates how basic local movement rules can lead to organized, efficient outcomes on a larger scale. The findings could influence how robotic fleets are designed and may even apply to human crowd management and traffic flow. The research was published in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences and led by applied mathematics Ph.D. student Lucy Liu, with guidance from SEAS Senior Research Fellow Justin Werfel.

Why Randomness Helps Predict Complex Behavior

Studying dense crowds is difficult because individuals can take countless possible paths and interact in unpredictable ways, Liu explained. To simplify the problem, the researchers treated each robot as a basic unit with a small, adjustable amount of variation in its movement.

"This might be counterintuitive, because how could randomness make things easier to work with?" said Liu. "But in this case, when you have a lot of randomness, it becomes possible to take averages -- average distances, average times, average behaviors. This makes it a lot easier to make predictions."

To explore this idea, the team created computer simulations of robot groups, referred to as agents. Each agent started at a random location and was assigned a random destination. Once it reached its target, it immediately received a new one, mimicking continuous task assignment in real-world systems.

Each agent moved toward its goal with a tunable amount of variation, described as "noise." With no noise, agents moved in straight lines. With high noise, their paths became erratic and inefficient. However, this wandering also helped them navigate around one another.

The simulations revealed a clear pattern. When agents moved in perfectly straight paths, they quickly formed dense clusters and traffic jams that halted progress. When movement became too random, congestion disappeared but efficiency dropped due to excessive wandering.

Between these extremes, the researchers identified a sweet spot. In this range, agents occasionally bumped into one another and formed short-lived clusters, but still managed to slip past and keep moving. This balance allowed the system to maintain a steady flow.

Using these insights, the team developed formulas to estimate "goal attainment rate," or how many destinations are reached over time. These equations made it possible to determine the ideal combination of crowd density and movement randomness to maximize performance.

To confirm their findings, Liu collaborated with physicist Federico Toschi at Eindhoven University of Technology in the Netherlands. Together, they set up experiments with small wheeled robots in a lab equipped with an overhead camera.

Each robot carried a QR code so its position could be tracked and updated with new destinations. Although the physical robots moved more slowly and less precisely than the simulated agents, they displayed the same overall patterns.

The experiments supported a key idea: highly complex coordination does not require advanced intelligence or centralized control. Instead, simple local rules can produce effective group behavior, at least within certain density limits.

"Understanding how active matter, whether it is a swarm of ants, a herd of animals, or a group of robots, become functional and execute tasks in crowded environments using the principles of self-organization, is relevant to many questions in behavioral ecology," Mahadevan said. "Our study suggests strategies that might well be much broader than the instantiation we have focused on."

Liu noted that she has long been interested in designing safer and more efficient crowded spaces. This research points toward a future where the movement of large groups, whether robots, vehicles, or people, could be predicted and optimized using mathematical tools.

The results suggest that introducing controlled variability into movement patterns may improve flow in many real-world systems, from factory floors to city streets.

These are everyday words people use to describe what goes on in the human mind. But when those same terms are applied to artificial intelligence, they can unintentionally make machines seem more human than they really are.

"We use mental verbs all the time in our daily lives, so it makes sense that we might also use them when we talk about machines -- it helps us relate to them," said Jo Mackiewicz, professor of English at Iowa State. Mackiewicz and Jeanine Aune, a teaching professor of English and director of the advanced communication program at Iowa State, are part of a research team that studied how writers describe AI using human-like language. The research team also included Matthew J. Baker, associate professor of linguistics at Brigham Young University, and Jordan Smith, assistant professor of English at the University of Northern Colorado. Both previously studied at Iowa State University.

Why Human-Like Language About AI Can Be Misleading

According to the researchers, using mental verbs to describe AI can create a false impression. There is also a broader concern. When AI is described as if it has intentions, it can distract from the humans behind it. Developers, engineers, and organizations are responsible for how these systems are built and used.

Friendly fraud prevention comes down to three jobs you can do in code: log the right checkout data, block risky payments before they settle, and automate your response when a dispute still lands. Get the first one wrong and you can't win the dispute later, no matter how good your case is.

Friendly fraud happens when a customer makes a real purchase, then disputes it with their card issuer. They might claim they didn't authorize it, that it never arrived, or that they canceled. Sometimes it's honest confusion. A spouse used the saved card, or the charge showed up under a name they didn't recognize. Sometimes it's a free refund with extra steps.

Either way, the money leaves your account. A dispute (also called a chargeback) reverses the payment, and your processor charges a dispute fee on top. Lose too many, and the card networks put your account into a monitoring program with higher fees.

#10610. Artificial neurons successfully communicate with living brain cells

Engineers at Northwestern University have created printed artificial neurons that go beyond imitation and can directly interact with real brain cells. These flexible, low-cost devices produce electrical signals that closely resemble those generated by living neurons, allowing them to activate biological brain tissue.

In experiments using slices of mouse brain, the artificial neurons successfully triggered responses in real neurons. This result shows a new level of compatibility between electronic devices and living neural systems.

Toward Brain Interfaces and Energy-Efficient AI

This advance moves researchers closer to electronics that can directly interface with the nervous system. Potential uses include brain-machine interfaces and neuroprosthetics, such as implants that could help restore hearing, vision, or movement.

The technology also points toward a new generation of computing systems inspired by the brain. By replicating how neurons communicate, future hardware could perform complex tasks using far less energy. The brain remains the most energy-efficient computing system known, and scientists hope to apply its principles to modern technology.

The study will be published on April 15 in the journal Nature Nanotechnology.

"The world we live in today is dominated by artificial intelligence (AI)," said Northwestern's Mark C. Hersam, who led the study. "The way you make AI smarter is by training it on more and more data. This data-intensive training leads to a massive power-consumption problem. Therefore, we have to come up with more efficient hardware to handle big data and AI. Because the brain is five orders of magnitude more energy efficient than a digital computer, it makes sense to look to the brain for inspiration for next-generation computing."

Hersam is an expert in brain-inspired computing and holds multiple roles at Northwestern University, including the Walter P. Murphy Professor of Materials Science and Engineering at the McCormick School of Engineering. He also is a professor of medicine at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine and a professor of chemistry at the Weinberg College of Arts and Sciences. In addition, he serves as chair of the department of materials science and engineering, director of the Materials Research Science and Engineering Center, and a member of the International Institute for Nanotechnology. He co-led the study with Vinod K. Sangwan, a research associate professor at McCormick.

Why the Brain Outperforms Traditional Silicon

Modern computers handle increasing workloads by packing billions of identical transistors onto rigid, two-dimensional silicon chips. Each component behaves the same way, and once manufactured, the system remains fixed.

The brain works very differently. It consists of many types of neurons, each with specialized roles, arranged in soft, three-dimensional networks. These networks are constantly changing, forming and adjusting connections as learning occurs.

"Silicon achieves complexity by having billions of identical devices," Hersam said. "Everything is the same, rigid and fixed once it's fabricated. The brain is the opposite. It's heterogeneous, dynamic and three-dimensional. To move in that direction, we need new materials and new ways to build electronics."

Although artificial neurons have been developed before, most produce overly simple signals. To achieve more complex behavior, engineers typically need large networks of devices, which increases energy use.

Printable Materials Enable Brain-Like Behavior

To better replicate real neural activity, Hersam's team built artificial neurons using soft, printable materials that more closely match the brain's structure. Their approach relies on electronic inks made from nanoscale flakes of molybdenum disulfide (MoS2), which acts as a semiconductor, and graphene, which serves as an electrical conductor. These materials were deposited onto flexible polymer surfaces using aerosol jet printing.

Previously, researchers treated the polymer in these inks as a flaw because it interfered with electrical performance. As a result, they removed it after printing. In this work, the team used that same feature to enhance the device.

"Instead of fully removing the polymer, we partially decompose it," he said. "Then, when we pass current through the device, we drive further decomposition of the polymer. This decomposition occurs in a spatially inhomogeneous manner, leading to formation of a conductive filament, such that all the current is constricted into a narrow region in space."

That narrow conductive path produces a sudden electrical response similar to a neuron firing. The resulting device can generate a wide variety of signals, including single spikes, continuous firing, and bursting patterns, closely resembling real neural communication.

Because each artificial neuron can produce more complex signals, fewer components are needed to perform advanced tasks. This could significantly improve computing efficiency.

Testing Artificial Neurons on Real Brain Tissue

To evaluate whether the artificial neurons could truly interact with living systems, the researchers partnered with Indira M. Raman, the Bill and Gayle Cook Professor of Neurobiology at Weinberg. Her team applied the artificial signals to slices of mouse cerebellum.

The results showed that the electrical spikes matched key biological properties, including their timing and duration. These signals reliably activated real neurons and triggered neural circuits in a way similar to natural brain activity.

"Other labs have tried to make artificial neurons with organic materials, and they spiked too slowly," Hersam said. "Or they used metal oxides, which are too fast. We are within a temporal range that was not previously demonstrated for artificial neurons. You can see the living neurons respond to our artificial neuron. So, we've demonstrated signals that are not only the right timescale but also the right spike shape to interact directly with living neurons."

Low-Cost, Sustainable Manufacturing and AI Implications

Beyond performance, the new approach offers environmental and practical advantages. The manufacturing process is simple and inexpensive, and the additive printing method places material only where it is needed, reducing waste.

Improving energy efficiency is especially important as artificial intelligence systems grow more demanding. Large data centers already consume vast amounts of power and require significant water for cooling.

"To meet the energy demands of AI, tech companies are building gigawatt data centers powered by dedicated nuclear power plants," Hersam said. "It is evident that this massive power consumption will limit further scaling of computing since it's hard to imagine a next-generation data center requiring 100 nuclear power plants. The other issue is that when you're dissipating gigawatts of power, there's a lot of heat. Because data centers are cooled with water, AI is putting severe stress on the water supply. However you look at it, we need to come up with more energy-efficient hardware for AI."

The study, "Multi-order complexity spiking neurons enabled by printed MoS2 memristive nanosheet networks," was supported by the National Science Foundation.

#10597. This AI knew the answers but didn’t understand the questions

Psychologists have long debated whether the human mind can be explained by a single, unified theory or if different functions such as attention and memory must be studied separately. Now, artificial intelligence (AI) is entering that debate, offering a new way to explore how the mind works.

In July 2025, a study published in Nature introduced an AI model called "Centaur." Built on standard large language models and refined using data from psychological experiments, Centaur was designed to simulate human cognitive behavior. It reportedly performed well across 160 tasks, including decision-making, executive control, and other mental processes. The results drew widespread attention and were seen as a possible step toward AI systems that could replicate human thinking more broadly.

A more recent study published in National Science Open challenges those claims. Researchers from Zhejiang University argue that Centaur's apparent success may come from overfitting. In other words, instead of understanding the tasks, the model may have learned to recognize patterns in the training data and reproduce expected answers.

To test this idea, the researchers created several new evaluation scenarios. In one example, they replaced the original multiple-choice prompts, which described specific psychological tasks, with the instruction "Please choose option A." If the model truly understood the task, it should have consistently selected option A. Instead, Centaur continued to choose the "correct answers" from the original dataset.

This behavior suggests that the model was not interpreting the meaning of the questions. Rather, it relied on learned statistical patterns to "guess" answers. The researchers compared this to a student who scores well by memorizing test formats without actually understanding the material.

The findings highlight the need for caution when assessing the abilities of large language models. While these systems can be highly effective at fitting data, their "black-box" nature makes it difficult to know how they arrive at their outputs. This can lead to issues such as hallucinations or misinterpretations. Careful and varied testing is essential to determine whether a model truly has the skills it appears to demonstrate.

Although Centaur was presented as a model capable of simulating cognition, its biggest limitation appears to be in language comprehension. #10591. AI lets chemists design molecules by simply describing them

Creating new molecules is one of the toughest tasks in chemistry. Whether the goal is a life-saving drug or a cutting-edge material, each compound must be built through a carefully planned series of reactions. Mapping out these steps requires deep expertise and strategic thinking, which is why chemists often spend years mastering the process.

A major hurdle is retrosynthesis. In this approach, chemists begin with the final molecule they want and work backward to figure out simpler starting materials and possible reaction routes. This involves many decisions, such as selecting the right building blocks, deciding when to form rings, and determining whether sensitive parts of the molecule need protection. While computers can scan enormous "chemical spaces," they still struggle to match the strategic judgment of experienced chemists.

Another challenge involves reaction mechanisms, which describe how reactions proceed step by step through the movement of electrons. Understanding these mechanisms allows scientists to predict new reactions, improve efficiency, and avoid costly trial and error. Although current computational tools can suggest many possible pathways, they often lack the intuition needed to pinpoint the most realistic ones.

Researchers led by Philippe Schwaller at EPFL have developed a new method that uses large language models (LLMs) as reasoning tools for chemistry. Rather than directly generating chemical structures, these models act as evaluators that guide existing computational systems.

The new framework, called Synthegy, combines traditional search algorithms with AI that can interpret chemical strategies written in natural language.

"When making tools for chemists, the user interface matters a lot, and previous tools relied on cumbersome filters and rules," says Andres M Bran, the first author of the Synthegy paper published in Matter. "With Synthegy, we're giving chemists the power to just talk, allowing them to iterate much faster and navigate more complex synthetic ideas."

How Synthegy Improves Retrosynthesis Planning

Synthegy starts with a target molecule and a simple instruction written in everyday language. For example, a chemist might request that a specific ring be formed early or that unnecessary protecting groups be avoided. Standard retrosynthesis software then generates many possible pathways.

Each of these pathways is converted into text and reviewed by a language model. Synthegy scores how well each option matches the chemist's instructions and explains its reasoning. This makes it easier to rank and filter the best routes. By guiding searches with natural language, chemists can quickly focus on strategies that align with their goals.

Understanding Reaction Mechanisms With AI

Synthegy applies a similar method to reaction mechanisms. It breaks reactions down into basic electron movements and explores different possibilities. The language model evaluates each step and steers the search toward pathways that make chemical sense.

The system can also incorporate additional details, such as reaction conditions or expert hypotheses, provided as text. This flexibility allows researchers to refine their analysis and explore more realistic scenarios.

In synthesis planning, Synthgey was able to identify pathways that matched complex strategic instructions. In a double-blind study, 36 chemists provided 368 valid evaluations, and their assessments agreed with the system's results 71.2% of the time on average.

The framework can flag unnecessary protecting steps, judge how feasible reactions are, and prioritize efficient solutions. It also demonstrates that LLMs can operate at multiple levels, from analyzing functional groups to evaluating entire synthetic routes. Larger models performed best, while smaller ones showed more limited abilities.

This research highlights a different way AI can support chemistry. Instead of replacing human decision-making, Synthegy positions language models as guides that help interpret and refine computational results. Chemists can describe their goals in plain language and receive solutions that reflect their strategy.

The approach could speed up drug discovery, improve reaction design, and make advanced tools more accessible to scientists.

"The connection between synthesis planning and mechanisms is very exciting: we usually use mechanisms to discover new reactions that enable us to synthesize new molecules," says Andres M Bran. "Our work is bridging that gap computationally through a unified natural language interface."

"The message is clear: speech timing is more than just a matter of style, it's a sensitive indicator of brain health," says Dr. Jed Meltzer, Senior Scientist at Baycrest's Rotman Research Institute and senior author on this study, titled "Natural Speech Analysis Can Reveal Individual Differences in Executive Function Across the Adult Lifespan."

AI Analysis Reveals Hidden Cognitive Clues

For the study, participants were shown detailed images and asked to describe them in their own words. They also completed established tests designed to measure executive function.

Researchers then used artificial intelligence to examine the speech recordings in depth. The AI system detected hundreds of subtle speech features, including the length and frequency of pauses, the use of filler words, and timing-related patterns in speech. These markers consistently predicted how well participants performed on cognitive tests, even after researchers adjusted for factors such as age, sex, and education.

Executive function naturally weakens with age and is often affected during the early stages of dementia. However, standard cognitive testing can be difficult to repeat frequently because it takes time and people often improve simply from becoming familiar with the tests.

The researchers say more long-term studies are needed to follow changes in speech over time and distinguish normal aging from the earliest signs of disease. #10581. How Text-to-Image AI Generators Work, and 5 Tools That Deliver Good Results

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

Type “a lighthouse on a cliff at dusk, oil painting, warm light” into a text box, wait ten seconds, and a picture that never existed appears. It feels like magic, but it is a well-understood pipeline of language models, neural networks and a great deal of math. Knowing how that pipeline works makes you a better prompter, helps you diagnose bad results, and makes it easier to choose the right tool for a job.

This guide explains how text-to-image generators turn words into pixels, why they fail in predictable ways, and how to write prompts that work with the model instead of against it. It then compares five tools that consistently produce good results, starting with Pollo AI, and finishes with a practical workflow, a troubleshooting table and an FAQ. Plans, credits and model versions change often, so confirm current details on each vendor’s site before you commit to a subscription.

Early image generators were mostly GANs, or generative adversarial networks. A GAN pits two networks against each other: a generator that produces images and a discriminator that tries to spot fakes. GANs could produce sharp faces and textures, but they were hard to train, often collapsed into repeating the same few outputs, and were poor at following free-form text.

A second line of research used autoregressive models, which generate an image piece by piece the way a language model generates words. These handled text prompts better but were slow at high resolution.

The shift came with diffusion models. Researchers showed that a network trained to reverse a gradual noising process could generate images with excellent quality and diversity, and that it was far more stable to train than a GAN. Combine that with text conditioning and a compressed image representation, and you get the systems behind most of today’s tools. Newer models increasingly swap the traditional convolutional network for transformers, but the core idea of iterative denoising is still the foundation.

Under the hood, mainstream generators combine three ideas: a way to understand text, a way to represent images compactly, and a way to generate them gradually. The sections below follow a prompt from keystroke to finished picture.

Neural networks cannot read words, so your prompt is first split into tokens, small chunks of text such as whole words or word fragments. A text encoder then converts those tokens into an embedding, a grid of numbers that captures meaning and context. Many systems use encoders trained jointly on images and their captions, so that the text “a red bicycle” and pictures of red bicycles end up close together in the same mathematical space. Some newer systems add a large language model to understand longer, more detailed instructions.

This is why wording matters. The encoder only sees what you wrote, and it has a limited capacity. A short, specific prompt produces a clear signal. A long, rambling one dilutes each instruction.

Instead of drawing on a blank canvas, the model begins with random static. The pattern of that static is controlled by a seed number. The same prompt with a different seed gives a different image, because it starts from different noise. If a tool lets you fix the seed, you can make results repeatable and change just one variable at a time.

During training, engineers take millions of real images and add noise to them in small steps until only static remains. The network is then trained to predict the noise that was added at each step, which teaches it to reverse the process. At generation time, the model applies that skill to pure noise, removing a little at each step. Because it has learned what real images look like, the “noise” it removes gradually reveals something that looks plausible.

Your text embedding is fed into the network through a mechanism called cross-attention. At each step, parts of the evolving image can “look at” relevant parts of the prompt and adjust accordingly. A region that will become a lighthouse attends to the word “lighthouse”. A region that will become the sky attends to “dusk”.

A setting called classifier-free guidance, often shown as guidance scale or prompt strength, controls how strongly each step is pulled toward the prompt. The model computes one prediction with the prompt and one without it, then exaggerates the difference. A low value gives the model freedom and often more natural, varied results. A high value follows the words more literally but can produce oversaturated, over-sharpened or distorted images.

Running diffusion directly on full-resolution pixels would be very expensive. The idea behind Stable Diffusion, known as latent diffusion, is to compress images into a much smaller latent representation using an autoencoder. Denoising happens in that compact space, and a decoder turns the final latent back into a full image. This is a major reason high-quality generation now runs in seconds on a cloud GPU instead of minutes.

The number of denoising steps and the scheduler, which decides how much noise to remove at each step, shape the speed and look of the result. More steps usually add detail up to a point, then give diminishing returns. Many tools hide these settings, but the trade-off is always the same: fewer steps are faster and cheaper, more steps can look more refined.

Most pipelines finish with an upscaler or refinement pass that sharpens detail and fixes small artifacts. Many tools also offer inpainting, which regenerates only a selected region, outpainting, which extends the canvas, and variations, which restart from a related image.

A generator can only produce what it has learned to represent. Models are trained on very large collections of images paired with captions, and the quality, diversity and licensing of that data affect both the output and the legal picture. Models trained on more photographs tend to produce convincing photorealism. Models tuned on illustration and concept art lean toward painterly looks.

This also explains a few behaviors. Common subjects look great because the model has seen thousands of examples. Rare or very specific subjects, such as an unusual machine part or a niche cultural item, often look generic or wrong. Biases in the data can show up as stereotypes, for example in how “a doctor” or “a CEO” is depicted, so it helps to describe the people and setting you actually want.

"Building on the legacy of previous space processors, this new multicore system is fault-tolerant, flexible, and extremely high-performing," said Eugene Schwanbeck, program element manager in NASA's Game Changing Development program at the agency's Langley Research Center, in Hampton, Virginia. "NASA's commitment to advancing spaceflight computing is a triumph of technical achievement and collaboration."

Radiation Hardened Processor Faces Extreme Testing

At the center of the project is a new radiation-hardened processor built to deliver up to 100 times the computing power of today's spaceflight computers while surviving the harsh environment of space. Engineers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) in Southern California are running a wide range of tests designed to simulate those conditions.

"We are putting these new chips through the wringer by carrying out radiation, thermal, and shock tests while also evaluating their performance through a rigorous functional test campaign," said Jim Butler, High Performance Space Computing project manager at JPL.

To qualify for spaceflight, the processor must withstand intense electromagnetic radiation and dramatic temperature fluctuations that can damage electronics. High-energy particles from the Sun and deep space can also trigger computer errors that force spacecraft into "safe mode," temporarily shutting down nonessential systems until engineers resolve the issue.

NASA is also testing how the chip handles the challenges of planetary landings.

"To simulate real-world performance, we are using high-fidelity landing scenarios from real NASA missions that would typically require power-intensive hardware to process huge volumes of landing-sensor data," said Butler. "This is an exciting time for us to be working on hardware that will enable NASA's next giant leaps."

Testing at JPL began in February and is expected to continue for several months. Early results have been highly encouraging. According to NASA, the processor is functioning as intended and has shown performance levels roughly 500 times greater than the radiation-hardened chips currently used in spacecraft.

The team also marked the beginning of testing with a symbolic moment by sending an email titled "Hello Universe," referencing the famous introductory messages used during the early days of computer programming.

AI Powered Spacecraft and Deep Space Missions

The processor is being developed jointly by JPL and Microchip Technology Inc., based in Chandler, Arizona. The company is working with NASA through a commercial partnership, and sample chips have already been shared with defense and commercial aerospace partners.

The technology is expected to play a major role in the future of autonomous spacecraft. With onboard artificial intelligence, spacecraft could respond to unexpected situations in real time when communication delays make human control impractical. The chip could also help deep space missions process, store, and transmit massive amounts of scientific data back to Earth more efficiently.

NASA says the processor may eventually support crewed missions to the Moon and Mars as well.

Small Processor With Massive Computing Power

The device is known as a system-on-a-chip (or SoC), meaning it combines the essential components of a computer into a single compact unit. The processor includes central processing units, computational offloads, advanced networking systems, memory, and input/output interfaces.

SoCs are widely used in smartphones and tablets because they are compact and energy efficient. However, NASA's version is designed to survive for years in deep space, potentially traveling millions (or even billions) of miles from Earth without maintenance or repairs.

Once the processor is certified for use in space, NASA plans to integrate it into a wide variety of missions, including Earth orbiters, planetary rovers, deep space probes, and crewed habitats.

The technology could also have benefits on Earth. Microchip plans to adapt the processor for industries such as aviation and automotive manufacturing.

The project is managed by the Space Technology Mission Directorate's Game Changing Development (GCD) program at NASA Langley. The GCD program and JPL, which is managed by Caltech in Pasadena, California, oversaw the development process from mission planning and industry studies through final delivery.

NASA JPL selected Microchip as a partner in 2022, and the company funded its own research and development work on the processor.

#10573. Building Reliable Product Emails Into Your Application

Stay up to date with AI tools, model releases, and developer workflows that matter.

A password reset email is part of your login flow. An invoice notification is part of your billing experience. Yet development teams often treat these messages as background tasks: render a template, call a sending API, and move on. That approach leaves an important part of the product experience untested until a user cannot complete the action they started.

Testing the application interface alone leaves a gap. A successful request to an email provider does not establish that the recipient can find the message, understand it, or use its links. Product emails need explicit success criteria, useful diagnostic records, and recovery options when something fails.

A reliable workflow combines application tests, controlled mailbox checks, and pre-send diagnostics for authentication and inbox placement. A comparison of available tools can help you identify which checks your existing setup covers and where additional testing would be useful. The goal is to investigate specific gaps rather than add another service without a clear purpose.

Start with the action the recipient needs to complete. A weekly activity summary can tolerate a delay that would make a sign-in code useless. An invoice needs accurate amounts and a clear payment destination. A security alert needs enough context for someone to recognize the event without exposing sensitive information.

For each message, document its trigger, expected timing, required data, and expiration behavior. Decide what the application should display if submission fails or the user requests another copy. These decisions give developers and testers concrete acceptance criteria.

For a password reset, test the complete journey: requesting the message, receiving it in a controlled mailbox, following the link, and setting a new password. Also check what happens when the link expires or has already been used.

Template tests should cover the data your application actually encounters. Try long names, missing optional fields, non-Latin characters, and large order totals. Escape user-provided values appropriately, and make sure conditional sections disappear cleanly when their data is absent.

Inspect both HTML and plain-text versions. Instructions should remain understandable when images are blocked, while descriptive links and readable text should make the next action clear. Check that every URL points to the correct environment, especially when staging and production share template code.

A development sandbox lets you inspect generated messages without contacting customers. Use it to check headers, attachments, and content. Keep a separate rendering check for the email clients your audience uses, because a sandbox preview cannot reproduce every client’s behavior.

A background queue can prevent a slow sending API from delaying a web request. It also creates another failure boundary: a worker may time out after the provider has accepted the email but before your application records the result.

If the job retries blindly, the recipient may receive two messages. Assign a stable identifier to each logical notification and record the provider’s message identifier when available. Where supported, reuse an idempotency key for retries of the same send operation, following the provider’s documented retention window.

Distinguish retries from intentional resends. A user asking for a new reset link is a different event from a worker repeating an uncertain request. Define which link remains valid when another is issued, and test that behavior explicitly.

Retries also need limits. Temporary service failures may justify another attempt with backoff. A permanently invalid address needs a correction or suppression workflow, rather than repeated sending.

Provider events describe different stages of the sending process. An acceptance response means the provider accepted the request. A delivery event generally means the receiving mail server accepted the message; it does not prove inbox placement or that a person read it.

Capture the events your provider exposes, including bounces, delays, and complaints. Associate them with the original notification so support staff can investigate a particular report. Confirm each event’s documented meaning instead of treating similar labels across providers as interchangeable.

Webhook handlers should verify incoming requests using the provider’s documented mechanism and tolerate duplicate notifications. Avoid assuming events always arrive in chronological order. Record enough history to explain what happened without allowing a late event to overwrite a more informative state.

Before releasing a significant template or infrastructure change, send controlled messages through the production sending configuration to test accounts at commonly used mailbox providers. Use synthetic data, and check placement, rendering, link behavior, and how long the message takes to arrive.

Inspect authentication results in the received headers. Published DNS records are only part of the configuration; the actual message must use the expected signing domain and sending infrastructure. If you change providers or domains, repeat these checks rather than assuming the previous results still apply.

Treat test mailbox results as samples. They can reveal problems, but they cannot guarantee identical placement for every recipient. Mailbox settings, filtering decisions, and recipient history can produce different outcomes.

Provide a resend option with sensible rate limits and explain when users should expect the message. Where appropriate, let signed-in users verify or correct their address. For public account-recovery forms, avoid responses that reveal whether a particular address has an account.

Give support staff a way to distinguish a notification that was never generated from a failed submission or a message accepted by the receiving server. Protect that diagnostic access, limit retention, and avoid logging reset tokens, private message bodies, or unnecessary personal information.

Reliable product email depends on the whole journey, from the triggering event to the recipient’s next action. Testing each stage and making failures observable helps teams resolve problems before users repeatedly click resend or abandon the task.

#10572. Forget electrons, this breakthrough uses light-matter particles to power AI

Eighty years after the creation of ENIAC, the world's first general-purpose electronic computer, researchers at the University of Pennsylvania are exploring a new way to power the future of computing. Instead of relying entirely on electrons, which have formed the backbone of computers since the 1940s, scientists are now turning to light.

ENIAC, developed by Penn researchers J. Presper Eckert and John Mauchly, helped launch the modern computing era by using streams of electrons to solve complex mathematical problems. That same electronic approach still powers today's computers, smartphones, and AI systems. But as artificial intelligence grows more demanding, the limits of electron-based hardware are becoming harder to ignore.

Electrons carry an electrical charge, which creates several challenges inside modern computer chips. As they move through materials, they generate heat and face resistance that wastes energy. Those problems become even more difficult as chips grow more complex and process enormous amounts of data for AI applications.

Researchers led by Penn physicist Bo Zhen in the School of Arts & Sciences believe photons, the particles that make up light, could help solve some of these issues.

"Because they are charge-neutral and have zero rest mass, photons can carry information quickly over long distances with minimal loss, dominating communications technology," explains Li He, co-first author of a paper published in Physical Review Letters and a former postdoctoral researcher in the Zhen Lab. "But that neutrality means they barely interact with their environment, making them bad at the sort of signal-switching logic that computers depend on."

In other words, light is excellent for carrying information quickly and efficiently, but it struggles with the switching operations needed for computing.

Combining Light and Matter for AI Computing

To overcome that problem, Zhen's team developed a special quasiparticle called an exciton-polariton. The particle forms when photons are strongly linked with electrons inside an atomically thin semiconductor material. This combination allows light to interact much more effectively, making it capable of performing the signal switching required for computing tasks.

The breakthrough could be especially important for artificial intelligence systems, which consume enormous amounts of power.

Many experimental photonic AI chips already use light to handle certain calculations at high speed. However, when these systems need to perform nonlinear activation steps, such as decision-making operations, they usually must convert light signals back into electronic ones. That conversion slows the process and increases energy use, reducing the benefits of photonic computing.

Using exciton-polaritons, the Penn researchers demonstrated all-light switching while using only about 4 quadrillionths of a joule of energy. That amount is extraordinarily small, far below the energy needed to briefly power a tiny LED light.

Toward Faster and More Efficient AI Chips

If the technology can be successfully scaled, it could lead to photonic chips capable of processing information directly from cameras without repeated conversions between light and electricity. The approach could also lower the massive energy demands of large AI systems and potentially support basic quantum computing functions on future chips.

#10567. Scientists are seriously asking if bees and ChatGPT are conscious

You might think a honey bee foraging in your garden and a browser window running ChatGPT have nothing in common. But recent scientific research has been seriously considering the possibility that either, or both, might be conscious.

There are many different ways of studying consciousness. One of the most common is to measure how an animal – or artificial intelligence (AI) – acts.

But two new papers on the possibility of consciousness in animals and AI suggest new theories for how to test this – one that strikes a middle ground between sensationalism and knee-jerk skepticism about whether humans are the only conscious beings on Earth.

Questions around consciousness have long sparked fierce debate.

That’s in part because conscious beings might matter morally in a way that unconscious things don’t. Expanding the sphere of consciousness means expanding our ethical horizons. Even if we can’t be sure something is conscious, we might err on the side of caution by assuming it is – what philosopher Jonathan Birch calls the precautionary principle for sentience.

The recent trend has been one of expansion.

For example, in April 2024 a group of 40 scientists at a conference in New York proposed the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness. Subsequently signed by over 500 scientists and philosophers, this declaration says consciousness is realistically possible in all vertebrates (including reptiles, amphibians and fishes) as well as many invertebrates, including cephalopods (octopus and squid), crustaceans (crabs and lobsters) and insects.

In parallel with this, the incredible rise of large language models, such as ChatGPT, has raised the serious possibility that machines may be conscious.

Five years ago, a seemingly ironclad test of whether something was conscious was to see if you could have a conversation with it. Philosopher Susan Schneider suggested if we had an AI that convincingly mused on the metaphysics of consciousness, it may well be conscious.

By those standards, today we would be surrounded by conscious machines. Many have gone so far as to apply the precautionary principle here too: the burgeoning field of AI welfare is devoted to figuring out if and when we must care about machines.

Yet all of these arguments depend, in large part, on surface-level behavior. But that behavior can be deceptive. What matters for consciousness is not what you do, but how you do it.

A new paper in Trends in Cognitive Sciences that one of us (Colin Klein) coauthored, drawing on previous work, looks to the machinery rather than the behavior of AI.

It also draws on the cognitive science tradition to identify a plausible list of indicators of consciousness based on the structure of information processing. This means one can draw up a useful list of indicators of consciousness without having to agree on which of the current cognitive theories of consciousness is correct.

Some indicators (such as the need to resolve trade-offs between competing goals in contextually appropriate ways) are shared by many theories. Most other indicators (such as the presence of informational feedback) are only required by one theory but indicative in others.

Importantly, the useful indicators are all structural. They all have to do with how brains and computers process and combine information.

The verdict? No existing AI system (including ChatGPT) is conscious. The appearance of consciousness in large language models is not achieved in a way that is sufficiently similar to us to warrant attribution of conscious states.

Yet at the same time, there is no bar to AI systems – perhaps ones with a very different architecture to today’s systems – becoming conscious.

The lesson? It’s possible for AI to behave as if conscious without being conscious.

Biologists are also turning to mechanisms – how brains work – to recognize consciousness in non-human animals.

In a new paper in Philosophical Transactions B, we propose a neural model for minimal consciousness in insects. This is a model that abstracts away from anatomical detail to focus on the core computations done by simple brains.

Our key insight is to identify the kind of computation our brains perform that gives rise to experience.

This computation solves ancient problems from our evolutionary history that arise from having a mobile, complex body with many senses and conflicting needs.

Importantly, we don’t identify the computation itself – there is science yet to be done. But we show that if you could identify it, you’d have a level playing field to compare humans, invertebrates, and computers.

The problem of consciousness in animals and in computers appear to pull in different directions.

For animals, the question is often how to interpret whether ambiguous behavior (like a crab tending its wounds) indicates consciousness.

For computers, we have to decide whether apparently unambiguous behavior (a chatbot musing with you on the purpose of existence) is a true indicator of consciousness or mere roleplay.

Yet as the fields of neuroscience and AI progress, both are converging on the same lesson: when making judgement about whether something is consciousness, how it works is proving more informative than what it does.
