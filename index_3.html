I have designed more than 100 websites and helped clients set up over 12 CRM and ERP systems. For years, my work revolved around WordPress, digital marketing, business automation, and helping companies choose the right technology.

But building a web application from scratch was a different challenge.

I wanted to move beyond configuring existing software and experience the entire product development process myself. I had an idea, a clear understanding of business workflows, and access to AI coding agents. What I did not want was another expensive subscription that would limit how I built, deployed, or maintained the application.

That decision led me to build a CRM using Claude, Next.js, Firebase Firestore, Tailwind CSS, Vercel, and Git.

The experience taught me something important: AI can reduce the cost of building software, but understanding what you are building still matters.

My background gave me a useful starting point. After working on more than 100 websites, I understood how users interact with interfaces, how businesses collect leads, and why a confusing workflow can make even good software difficult to use.

Setting up CRM and ERP systems for clients also helped me understand the operational side of software. I had seen how businesses manage contacts, sales pipelines, follow-ups, and reporting.

However, configuring an existing platform and building an application from scratch are two very different experiences.

I decided to use this project to learn those things through practical experience rather than limiting myself to website design.

Instead, I chose an AI-assisted coding workflow.

Claude acted as my coding partner. I described requirements, generated implementation drafts, reviewed the output, debugged problems, and refined features through repeated iterations.

This did not mean I could simply write a prompt and receive a finished application. I still had to make decisions, understand the generated code, test the results, and correct mistakes.

The financial advantage was meaningful. By avoiding paid app-builder subscriptions for this project, I saved more than $1,000 in potential tool costs and related expenses I would otherwise have budgeted for. That was my project-specific saving, not a claim that every custom application will be cheaper to build.

More importantly, I ended up with a codebase and development workflow I could control rather than depending entirely on a particular app-building interface.

I wanted a modern stack that supported fast iteration without sacrificing control over the application.

Claude helped me translate requirements into code, work through implementation problems, and iterate on features. I treated its output as a starting point, not something to trust without review.

Next.js provided the foundation for the web application. It gave me a structured way to build the interface and application logic while keeping the project organized as it grew.

Firestore handled the application's data storage. Its document-based model and integration with the Firebase ecosystem made it a practical choice for my development workflow.

Choosing a database is only part of the job, though. Data structure, access rules, query patterns, and the way records relate to users all require careful thought.

Tailwind CSS helped me build and refine the interface without maintaining a large collection of separate CSS rules. It also made iterative UI improvements easier.

Git gave me a history of code changes and a way to manage development safely. When an AI-generated change caused a problem, version control helped me inspect what had changed and recover when necessary.

Vercel provided the deployment environment for the application. Its integration with Git made it easier to move from code changes to deployed versions.

Together, these tools allowed me to build with AI assistance while retaining control over the underlying code and deployment process.

The biggest surprise was how quickly AI could produce a first version of a feature—and how much work could remain afterward.

A screen might look correct but behave incorrectly when connected to real data. An API integration could work in a simple test but fail when an external service returned an unexpected response. A feature could appear complete while overlooking permissions or edge cases.

Connecting different services required more than generating a request. I had to consider authentication, request and response formats, error handling, timeouts, and what the application should do when an external service became unavailable.

I learned to break integrations into smaller steps and verify each one before building additional functionality around it.

An application that stores customer information cannot rely on a good-looking login screen for security.

I had to pay attention to authentication, database access rules, permission boundaries, and the possibility of users accessing data they should not see. AI assistance made implementation faster, but security decisions still required careful review and testing.

These were not problems that could be solved by writing a better prompt alone. They required observation, testing, and repeated refinement.

The most valuable lesson was that vibe coding is not the same as handing over software development to an AI model.

My experience in WordPress and business systems helped me understand the workflows I wanted to support. AI coding tools helped me move from those requirements to a working implementation more quickly.

For others, a visual app builder is an excellent way to validate an idea before investing in custom development.

The right approach depends on the requirements, budget, technical constraints, and long-term plans.

For me, building from scratch with AI coding assistance was the right learning experience. It helped me avoid more than $1,000 in potential app-builder costs while giving me greater control over the application and teaching me skills I could not have gained by configuring an existing platform alone.

My biggest takeaway is simple: AI makes building software more accessible, but the ability to define problems, make sound technical decisions, and verify the results is what turns generated code into a useful application.

A useful pipeline has three responsibilities:

Choose storage separately from orchestration. A scheduler does not decide how facts should be modeled, and a transformation framework does not guarantee source completeness.

Before adopting a warehouse, measure query duration, concurrency, freshness requirements, and interference with application traffic. For some workloads, precomputed results are enough to improve reporting responsiveness; SitePoint’s materialized-view tutorial explains that approach in PostgreSQL. Refresh behavior and acceptable staleness still need an explicit policy.

A migration becomes easier to evaluate when the team can show a small working pipeline, documented metrics, representative queries, and known failure cases.

For teams evaluating data warehouse consulting services, those artifacts make a useful review brief. Ask for concrete decisions about source ordering, historical changes, reconciliation, access controls, and recovery procedures, together with the assumptions behind any cost estimate.

Keep the review focused on observable outcomes. For example:

●     Can a failed batch be replayed without changing correct totals?

●     How are conflicting source versions detected?

●     Which reports change when an old order is updated?

●     Can a dashboard total be traced back to source records?

●     Who owns freshness alerts and recovery?

These questions also help an internal team review its own design. The important deliverable is a system whose behavior can be explained and tested.

A dashboard total needs context about when its underlying data became usable.

Track the latest successful ingestion, transformation, and validation separately. A recent extraction timestamp does not prove that downstream models finished building.

Likewise, a quiet source can make an event timestamp look old even when ingestion is healthy. Combine source activity with pipeline execution metadata before deciding that the data is stale.

Define the serving behavior when checks fail. You might keep the last validated result available with a visible freshness warning rather than replace it with an unverified total. Store the successful version if that is the policy: a live view over changing raw data does not preserve a previous validated snapshot.

A trustworthy analytics pipeline begins with a precise question and a testable data model.

Define the reporting grain, preserve incoming records, resolve source versions before filtering, and verify that retries do not alter correct results. Then measure whether the workload needs a different storage platform.

Those foundations make a future warehouse migration easier to reason about and make the dashboard useful before that migration begins.

#11038. Check if eMudhra CA Is Still Trusted in Your Runtime

Eight eMudhra intermediate Certificate Authority private keys are trivially recoverable from their public certificates. Any attacker can mint trusted TLS certificates for arbitrary domains. Browsers have already blocked these compromised intermediates through revocation mechanisms, but most non-browser runtimes — curl, Python, Node.js, Go, and Rust — still silently trust them. This affects any team operating server-side infrastructure, CI pipelines, or CLI tooling that makes outbound TLS connections. If your stack trusts the eMudhra chain, an attacker can forge certificates your code will accept without complaint.

This guide provides a diagnostic sequence: test every runtime in the stack against the compromised certificates, remediate by updating trust stores, and verify the fix sticks. The primary source for this disclosure is Scott Helme's detailed writeup.

In RSA key generation, you derive the private exponent d via the modular inverse of the public exponent e modulo λ(n) (the Carmichael totient of the modulus), where λ(n) depends on the prime factors of n. RSA's security depends entirely on d being large — typically hundreds of digits — making it computationally infeasible to derive from the public key alone.

eMudhra generated eight intermediate CA certificates with single-byte d values. Specific recovered values include d=4, d=56, and d=115, as documented in Scott Helme's disclosure post. These are not large numbers. They are recoverable from the public certificate in milliseconds. Anyone with access to the public certificate (which is, by definition, public) can reconstruct the private key and sign certificates that chain up to eMudhra's trusted root. That means arbitrary TLS certificates for any domain, indistinguishable from legitimate ones to any client that trusts the eMudhra chain.

An attacker holding one of these recovered keys can issue a certificate for any hostname, and any TLS client trusting the eMudhra intermediate will accept it without complaint.

The practical consequence is total impersonation capability. An attacker holding one of these recovered keys can issue a certificate for any hostname, and any TLS client trusting the eMudhra intermediate will accept it without complaint.

Major browsers have deployed mitigations through their respective revocation infrastructure. The compromise was reported to Apple, Google, Microsoft, Mozilla, and eMudhra on Saturday, October 17, 2026. Chrome pushed a CRLSet update blocking the intermediates the same evening, Mozilla blocked them in Firefox via OneCRL within hours, Apple applied its platform block on October 19, and Microsoft rolled out a disallowed-certificate CTL update. Current browser versions already reject the compromised intermediates.

The problem lies everywhere else. Non-browser TLS clients skip revocation checks by default. curl does not check CRLs or OCSP unless explicitly configured to do so. Python's requests and urllib3 libraries rely on the certifi bundle and perform no revocation checking. Python's standard library urllib.request module uses the system SSL store, not certifi. Node.js's https module uses either a compiled-in root store or the system store depending on build configuration, with no active revocation. Go's crypto/tls loads system roots but does not perform revocation checks. Rust's rustls paired with webpki-roots uses a bundled Mozilla root set, while native-tls defers to the system store; neither checks revocation by default.

This creates a trust store gap. The system CA bundle on a Linux server, the certifi bundle shipped with Python, and the compiled-in roots in Node.js might all still contain the eMudhra intermediates or trust the root that chains to them. Without manual intervention, these runtimes remain vulnerable.

The incident's official test infrastructure, documented on the status page at emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com, provides twelve TLS endpoints: one for each of the six compromised TLS intermediates, served twice - once chaining to eMudhra’s emSign ECC root and once chaining to Sectigo’s cross-signing AAA Certificate Services root. Host names follow the pattern dv-g3b-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com (eMudhra chain) and dv-g3b-aaa-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com (Sectigo cross-signed chain), with dv/ov/ev and g3b/g3c variants covering all six TLS intermediates. The diagnostic logic is straightforward: if a runtime connects successfully, it still trusts the compromised chain and is vulnerable. If the TLS handshake fails with a certificate verification error, the runtime correctly distrusts the intermediate. The bare domain emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com serves only the status page itself over an ordinary Let’s Encrypt certificate, so connecting to it proves nothing - always test the per-intermediate subdomains. Test at least one -emudhra host and its -aaa-emudhra counterpart: a store can distrust one root path while still trusting the other.

Important: A network or DNS error will also produce a connection failure. Before concluding SAFE, confirm the endpoint is reachable by running curl -v --insecure https://dv-g3b-emudhra.we-have-a-problem.com/ and verify that any TLS error is certificate-related (e.g., "certificate verify failed"), not a network connectivity error.

Every frontend developer will eventually stare at a CORS error in the browser console, watching an API call fail for reasons that seem opaque. A CORS error fix requires understanding what the browser is actually telling you, matching that specific message to its root cause, and applying a targeted, security-conscious server configuration.

The scheme, host, and port together define an "origin." https://example.com and http://example.com are different origins (different schemes), as are https://example.com and https://api.example.com (different hosts). https://example.com and https://example.com:443 are the same origin because port 443 is the default for HTTPS, and browsers omit it in origin comparisons. The browser enforces the same-origin policy on client-side JavaScript reading responses, not on sending requests. This distinction trips up many developers: the server may still receive and fully process the request; the browser simply refuses to expose the response to the calling code. The request lands, the database row gets written, and the browser throws an error anyway. The policy exists to prevent a malicious page from silently reading sensitive data from another origin using the user's ambient credentials.

Cross-Origin Resource Sharing (CORS) is a controlled opt-in mechanism via HTTP response headers. The server signals to the browser that a specific foreign origin is permitted to read its responses. Four headers control the mechanism: Access-Control-Allow-Origin specifies which origin is allowed, and Access-Control-Allow-Methods declares which HTTP methods are permitted. Access-Control-Allow-Headers lists which custom or non-simple headers the client may send, while Access-Control-Allow-Credentials indicates whether the browser should expose the response to credentialed requests.

The browser does not treat all cross-origin requests equally. "Simple" requests use GET, HEAD, or POST with a Content-Type of application/x-www-form-urlencoded, multipart/form-data, or text/plain, and no custom headers. A POST with Content-Type: application/json is not a simple request and triggers a preflight.

Simple requests go directly to the server, and the browser evaluates the CORS headers on the response. Anything else triggers a preflight request: an OPTIONS call the browser sends automatically before the actual request, asking the server whether the real request would be acceptable. If the OPTIONS response lacks the proper CORS headers, the browser never sends the real request at all.

A typical Chrome DevTools error reads: Access to fetch at 'https://api.example.com/data' from origin 'https://app.example.com' has been blocked by CORS policy: No 'Access-Control-Allow-Origin' header is present on the requested resource.

Two origins appear in this message. The URL after "fetch at" is the target server, the resource the code tried to reach. The value after "from origin" is the page's own origin, the one making the request. Chrome is stating that the target server's response did not include an Access-Control-Allow-Origin header that matches the requesting origin, so the browser blocked the response from reaching JavaScript.

The most common variant is the missing header: No 'Access-Control-Allow-Origin' header is present on the requested resource. The server simply did not include the header at all.

When the console says Response to preflight request doesn't pass access control check, the server either did not respond to the OPTIONS request correctly or returned a non-2xx status code for it.

Redirect variant: Redirect is not allowed for a preflight request. Developers commonly misdiagnose this one. It occurs when the URL triggers a redirect before reaching the application handler. Typical culprits include HTTP-to-HTTPS upgrades, trailing-slash normalization (e.g., /api/data redirecting to /api/data/), or a load balancer rewriting URLs. The browser's preflight OPTIONS request follows the redirect, and the Fetch specification (§4.8) forbids following redirects on preflight requests, so the entire flow fails. Fix the URL so no redirect occurs.

Credential variant: The value of the 'Access-Control-Allow-Origin' header must not be the wildcard '*' when the request's credentials mode is 'include'. When fetch is called with credentials: 'include' or XMLHttpRequest sets withCredentials = true, the server must respond with the exact requesting origin, not *. The browser will block JavaScript access to the response if a wildcard is used with credentials.

A user fills out a form, requests a preview, and then changes their mind. They click “Clear,” the inputs become empty, and the interface appears ready for someone else.

A moment later, the previous preview returns.

The application cleared the fields, but an asynchronous request still held the old values and updated the screen when it finished. Similar problems occur when drafts remain in browser storage, validation messages retain personal details, or a component’s internal state survives a visual reset.

For developers building onboarding, expense, application, and document workflows, clearing a form is a state-management operation. It deserves the same attention as submission.

This article explains how to define that operation, prevent stale results, and inspect the outcome using practical browser and testing tools.

Start by listing the places where a draft exists. The visible inputs are only one location.

When reviewing a financial-data interface, a useful design exercise is to map which information belongs to the current draft and what should happen when the user starts again. The same exercise applies to expense submissions and customer onboarding; the implementation must be assessed separately from the visible interface.

Name actions according to their actual scope. “Clear this form” should not imply that previously submitted records have been deleted. If a draft has been saved remotely, distinguish clearing the current screen from deleting the saved draft.

For a broader introduction to form structure and validation, SitePoint’s guide to modern HTML forms provides useful background.

Autosave is convenient, but persisting the entire form after every keystroke is an architectural decision, not a harmless UI enhancement.

For a short workflow, keeping the draft in memory may be sufficient. For a longer workflow, decide which fields may be retained, how the user understands that behavior, and when the retained copy is removed.

Browser storage options have different lifetimes. According to MDN’s sessionStorage documentation, session storage is associated with a page session and survives reloads. It should not be treated as equivalent to an ordinary variable that disappears when the page reloads.

This reduces unnecessary copies. It does not provide secure memory erasure or protect a page from every script that can access its data.

#10956. AI Agents Need Idempotency: The Distributed Systems Problem Hiding Inside Agentic AI

Have 30-200 Employees? Make $50k-$500k selling your data for AI Training.

A customer wants a $480 refund for a faulty part of a shipment he received. After waiting for 30 seconds for the payment API to process the refund, the customer support AI sends another request. It ultimately is fulfilled. However, as it turns out, the first request had also been fulfilled. The only thing missing was the notification of the refund for the customer. So the customer received two refunds of $480 each instead of the one he was asking for.

The AI was acting appropriately within its given constraints; the surrounding system couldn’t tell if the initial action had already been taken and failed to deliver the result. A well-known problem in distributed systems is gaining more notoriety as AI-powered agents increasingly perform actions beyond simply generating text. Idempotence is the solution to this problem.

Idempotence means an action can be performed multiple times with the same result as performing it once. This concept is particularly important for an AI agent that issues refunds, creates tickets, sends emails, or updates business applications.

AI agents as distributed software components have simple processes.

Think → choose a tool → call an API → receive a result → decide what to do next.

As we have outlined, many of the agent actions will utilize services and networks that can have issues from time to time. Consider the simple action of asking a payment provider to issue a refund. There are three possible outcomes:

The payment service never gets the request.

The refund went through, and the agent got the answer.

The refund went through, but the agent never received it back.

The most severe case, however, is when the time to complete an operation exceeds the timeout, but the operation does complete successfully. Since there is no answer, the software agent never learns of the operation's successful completion and must attempt to complete the operation again.

Distributed systems have dealt with the issues of unreliable networks for a long time already (retries, duplicate requests, etc.). By introducing agents, we give the software more freedom to choose when and how to proceed.

Consider an agent with these tools: search_customer(), check_inventory(), refund_payment(), send_email(), create_order(), delete_account()

Calling search_customer() twice will usually not cause much trouble.

In other words, an agent runtime should be able to tell if a tool only gets data or makes changes to the outside world. In the second group are things like sending money, getting money back, placing orders, writing messages, making accounts, and changing infrastructure.

There are side effects to these treatments. It's risky to think that agents can just retry every failed tool call as they get access to more business systems.

One common way to handle the duplicate requests issue is to implement idempotency keys.

Idempotency-Key: refund-order-123-run-842

The service stores the idempotency key and the result of processing the associated request.

It allows the refund to be processed the first time the idempotency key is sent to the service by the agent. Thereafter, if the response to the refund is lost and the agent attempts to resubmit the refund with the same idempotency key, the service will recognize that the refund has already been processed and return the previous result to the agent instead of processing another refund.

Many of the world’s leading payment companies, including Stripe, PayPal, and Square, use idempotency keys to allow their customers to safely retry requests.

However, one important rule for agents is that the agent software or workflow system—not the AI model—creates the idempotency key, and it is up to date.

By the time the model retries the same step, the Operation ID will have remained the same. This means the system can recognize the request as another attempt to execute the same action as before and not a new action.

Things could be easy if we could tell the model to ‘remember if it has already issued one of these before issuing another.' But even in the production environment, this approach is not sufficient. The agent could crash. The context could go away. The workflow could be taken off to be executed on another machine. The problem is that the model lacks sufficient information about what has or has not happened in external systems. Instead, the system should keep a record of the execution state outside of the model. The model determines what should happen. The runtime determines safe execution.

Modern agent tools include a name, description, and input parameters for the production system. More importantly, the agent runtime also needs to know about the behavior of the individual tool. For example, ‘refund_order’ modifies the application outside this tool, so the operation must be re-executed with the same actionID used for the initial failure. Also, as an example, refunds larger than $5,000 require manual approval. On the other hand, a ‘search_customer’ action is only a read, so re-execution can occur without human intervention. Based on this information, the OS makes decisions about the danger of the individual tool call. Automatic re-execution is still possible, even if the search fails. However, refunds require a bit more caution to avoid double posting of money. Thus, in addition to the input models for the AI tools used in real life, so-called “execution rules” are also required. These rules state when re-execution, increased security, or human intervention is required.

Suppose an AI agent were to perform the following three actions: 1) Make whole the customer, 2) Send a confirmation email with the order update and a proof, and 3) Check the status of the order in process. We had described how stopping the AI from paying out twice was only half the problem; the system also needed to know where the work had stopped. To that end, we’d like to introduce the concept of durable execution. For durable execution, the agent must do more than simply remember the actions it has taken. We need to keep track of the progress of the actions of the AI. The refund action was marked complete, but the email action was not sent yet. Thus, when the agent is restarted, it continues from where it left off rather than starting over. To create reliable AI agents, we need a few things: stable operation IDs, irrevocable actions, durable state, controlled retries, and logging. There are also cases where one wants to perform a recovery or compensating action (e.g., the agent makes a reservation but then fails to perform the subsequent action). The recovery action would be to cancel the reservation that had been made.

While controls with a person in the loop are mostly suitable for fine-grained actions that cannot be undone, idempotence ensures that an action is not accidentally executed more than once, and human approval is required to decide whether the action should be executed in the first place. This point is illustrated by an example: An employee correctly approves a $10,000 refund. However, the payment request somehow times out and is automatically re-executed several times. Without adequate idempotency security, the customer might end up with even more refunds than intended. Note, however, that a person’s approval cannot prevent double killing in such high-risk agent processes. Therefore, additional decision controls and corresponding execution controls are usually required to ensure that all actions are executed safely and correctly.

There are 5 simple questions that need to be answered before allowing an AI agent to take real-world actions.

Improving the model alone will not make the agent reliable unless we can answer all of these questions.

Much work in AI today focuses on making agents smarter, better at planning, and better to use as tools. But running agents in production has its own problems. API calls time out, processes crash, networks fail, and responses get lost, and the smartest agent can try again and again until it works. These are not new problems. Engineers working on distributed systems have been struggling with these same issues for decades. So long as the AI agent is confined to a simple test environment, of course it can query payment systems, customer information, and corporate tools. But when it is turned loose on the production corporation’s infrastructure (with all the payment systems, customer information, and corporate tools that entails), it must also be reliable. A smart model can figure out that a customer is due a refund. But a strong system can only give that customer a refund once. So the important question to ask of an AI agent before turning loose on it is, "Some powerful new tool: what happens if the action worked?" The answer will tell you whether or not the AI agent is currently able to operate reliably in production.

AI image generation has become a normal part of many web projects. However, the image a model produces is rarely the image a browser should receive. A single large PNG straight from a generator can weigh several megabytes, ignore the visitor's screen size, and slow down your Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) before the page has a chance to impress anyone.

The good news is that the fix is not complicated. If you treat the generated image as a high-quality master file and run it through a small, repeatable pipeline, you can ship images that look sharp on every device and load quickly on slow connections.

This tutorial walks through that pipeline from start to finish: generating at the right size and aspect ratio, choosing modern formats, creating responsive variants with Node.js, writing srcset and sizes correctly, and avoiding the performance mistakes that show up most often in audits.

Image generators are built to produce visually impressive results, not optimized web assets. Their output is usually a single file at one resolution, often in PNG format, and sometimes with an aspect ratio that does not match your layout.

Sending that file directly to the browser creates three problems. First, mobile visitors download far more pixels than their screens can display. Second, PNG is a poor choice for photographic images because its lossless compression produces large files. Third, without width and height information, the browser cannot reserve space for the image, which causes layout shifts as the page loads.

A web pipeline solves all three. You generate one master image, export multiple sizes in modern formats, and let the browser choose the best version for each visitor.

Optimization starts before you export anything. The most efficient pipeline begins with an image that already fits your layout.

Look at where the image will appear and note its aspect ratio at each breakpoint. A full-width hero might use 16:9 on desktop, while a blog thumbnail might use 4:3 or 1:1. Then identify the largest size the image will ever display. For a full-width hero, that could be 1920 pixels wide on large screens, or more if you support high-density displays at wide viewports.

Your master image should be at least as large as that maximum rendered width. Upscaling a small image later almost always introduces blurriness or artifacts.

Modern image-generation models can follow detailed instructions, so describe the technical requirements in your prompt, not just the subject. Specify the aspect ratio, intended display size, visual style, and any areas that need to remain clear for HTML text. The exact controls available will depend on the tool you use, so check its official documentation before building a workflow around a particular feature.

A practical web-focused prompt might read: "hero image of a modern home office at sunrise, soft natural light, calm blue and warm wood tones, clear empty space on the left third for headline text, no text or logos in the image."

That last instruction matters. Avoid baking headlines or buttons into images. Text inside an image cannot be translated, resized for accessibility, or indexed reliably, and it often becomes unreadable at small sizes. Keep text in HTML and use the image for visuals only.

Once you have a master file, decide which formats to serve. For most photographic and illustrative AI images, a combination of AVIF, WebP, and JPEG covers every modern browser while keeping file sizes small.

AVIF typically offers the strongest compression for photographic content and is supported by all major modern browsers, including Safari 16 and later. WebP is slightly less efficient but encodes faster and has near-universal support. JPEG remains a reliable fallback for older browsers and email clients.

Use PNG only when you specifically need lossless quality, such as for sharp-edged graphics or screenshots. Both AVIF and WebP support transparency, so you rarely need PNG for images with transparent backgrounds anymore.

Creating a dozen versions of every image by hand is tedious and error-prone. Instead, script the process. The Sharp documentation explains how to resize images and export them in different formats.

Then create a script that exports several widths in each format:

const widths = [480, 768, 1080, 1440, 1920];

{ ext: 'webp', format: 'webp', options: { quality: 75 } },

Save the file as an ES module, such as build-images.mjs, and run it with Node. The withoutEnlargement option prevents sharp from upscaling the master when a target width is larger than the original.

The quality values above are reasonable starting points, not universal rules. AVIF and WebP quality scales do not map directly to JPEG quality, so compare outputs visually at the sizes your users will actually see. Smooth gradients, which are common in AI-generated skies and backgrounds, are especially prone to banding at aggressive settings.

The MDN responsive images guide explains how srcset and sizes work. The srcset attribute lists available image files and their intrinsic widths. The sizes attribute tells the browser how wide the image will be displayed in the layout. With both pieces of information, the browser can choose the smallest file that still looks sharp on the visitor's screen, including high-density displays.

Here is an example for an image inside an article column that is 720 pixels wide on larger screens and nearly full width on smaller ones:

alt="A modern home office at sunrise with a wooden desk and soft natural light"

The most common mistake is omitting sizes or leaving it at 100vw for images that are not full width. When that happens, the browser assumes the image fills the entire viewport and downloads a much larger file than necessary. Always match sizes to your actual CSS layout.

To offer AVIF and WebP with a JPEG fallback, wrap your image in a picture element. The browser checks each source in order and uses the first format it supports. See the MDN documentation for the picture element for the full syntax.

srcset="dist/hero-768.avif 768w, dist/hero-1440.avif 1440w, dist/hero-1920.avif 1920w"

srcset="dist/hero-768.webp 768w, dist/hero-1440.webp 1440w, dist/hero-1920.webp 1920w"

srcset="dist/hero-768.jpg 768w, dist/hero-1440.jpg 1440w, dist/hero-1920.jpg 1920w"

alt="A modern home office at sunrise with a wooden desk and soft natural light"

The img element is still required. It holds the fallback file, the dimensions, the alt text, and the loading attributes, while the source elements simply provide alternative formats.

If you need a different crop on mobile, such as a square version of a wide hero, add media attributes to your source elements. This technique is called art direction, and it is a good reason to generate or crop a separate master image for small screens rather than relying on CSS to hide important parts of a wide composition.

Always include width and height attributes on your img element. Modern browsers use them to calculate the image's aspect ratio and reserve the correct space before the file downloads, which helps prevent Cumulative Layout Shift. In CSS, set the image to width: 100% and height: auto so it scales responsively while keeping its proportions.

Use loading="lazy" for images below the fold, but never on your hero or other LCP image. Lazy loading your most important image delays it unnecessarily. Instead, add fetchpriority="high" to the LCP image so the browser downloads it early.

Write alt text that describes the image's purpose in context. If an image is purely decorative, use an empty alt attribute so screen readers skip it. Generated images do not come with meaningful descriptions, so this step always requires human judgment.

Use descriptive file names, such as home-office-sunrise-1440.avif, instead of generic names like image-final-v3.png. Clear names make assets easier to manage and give search engines a small amount of extra context.

Be aware of metadata. Some AI image providers, including OpenAI, embed C2PA provenance data that identifies an image as AI-generated. sharp strips most metadata by default, which reduces file size but also removes that provenance information. If transparency matters to your project or your client, recent versions of sharp offer a keepMetadata() method, and you can also disclose AI-generated imagery in captions or credits.

"Consciousness science is no longer a purely philosophical pursuit. "Understanding consciousness is one of the most substantial challenges of 21st-century science -- and it's now urgent due to advances in AI and other technologies.

"If we become able to create consciousness -- even accidentally -- it would raise immense ethical challenges and even existential risk," added Cleeremans, a European Research Council (ERC) grantee.

Consciousness, commonly described as awareness of both the world around us and ourselves, remains one of science's most difficult puzzles. Despite decades of research, scientists still lack agreement on how subjective experience emerges from biological processes.

Researchers have identified brain regions and neural activity linked to conscious experience, but major disagreements remain. Scientists continue to debate which brain systems are truly necessary for consciousness and how they interact to produce awareness. Some researchers even question whether this approach captures the problem correctly.

The new review examines the current state of consciousness science, future directions for the field, and the possible consequences if humans succeed in fully explaining or even creating consciousness. This includes the possibility of consciousness emerging in machines or in lab-grown brain-like systems known as "brain organoids."

The authors argue that developing evidence-based tests for consciousness could transform how awareness is identified across many contexts. Such tools could help detect consciousness in patients with brain injuries or dementia and determine when awareness arises in fetuses, animals, brain organoids, or even AI systems.

While this would represent a major scientific advance, the researchers caution that it would also create difficult ethical and legal questions. Determining that a system is conscious would force society to reconsider how that system should be treated.

"Progress in consciousness science will reshape how we see ourselves and our relationship to both artificial intelligence and the natural world," said co-author Prof Anil Seth from the University of Sussex and ERC grantee. "The question of consciousness is ancient -- but it's never been more urgent than now."

A deeper understanding of consciousness could have wide-ranging effects across society.

In medicine, it could improve care for patients who are unresponsive and assumed to be unconscious. Measurements inspired by integrated information theory and global workspace theory[1] have already detected signs of awareness in some individuals diagnosed with unresponsive wakefulness syndrome. Further progress could refine these tools to better assess consciousness in coma, advanced dementia, and anesthesia, and influence treatment decisions and end-of-life care.

Mental health treatment could also benefit. Understanding the biological basis of subjective experience may help researchers develop better therapies for conditions such as depression, anxiety, and schizophrenia by narrowing the gap between animal studies and human emotional experience.

Greater insight into consciousness could reshape how humans think about their moral responsibilities toward animals. Identifying which animals and systems are sentient could influence animal research practices, farming, food consumption, and conservation strategies. "Understanding the nature of consciousness in particular animals would transform how we treat them and emerging biological systems that are being synthetically generated by scientists," said co-author Prof Liad Mudrik from Tel Aviv University and ERC grantee.

At the same time, advances in AI, brain organoids, and brain-computer interfaces raise the possibility of creating or altering awareness beyond natural biological limits. While some researchers believe consciousness might arise through computation alone, others argue that biological factors play an essential role. "We need more team science to break theoretical silos and overcome existing biases and assumptions," said Mudrik. "This step has the potential to move the field forward."

The researchers also argue that scientific work should place greater emphasis on phenomenology (what consciousness feels like) alongside studies of function (what consciousness does).

"Cooperative efforts are essential to make progress -- and to ensure society is prepared for the ethical, medical, and technological consequences of understanding, and perhaps creating, consciousness," said Cleeremans.

#10904. A tiny light trap could unlock million qubit quantum computers

After years of slow progress, researchers may finally be seeing a clear path forward in the quest to build powerful quantum computers. These machines are expected to dramatically shorten the time required for certain calculations, turning problems that would take classical computers thousands of years into tasks that could be completed in hours.

A team led by physicists at Stanford University has developed a new kind of optical cavity that can efficiently capture single photons, the basic particles of light, emitted by individual atoms. Those atoms serve as the core components of a quantum computer because they store qubits, which are the quantum equivalent of the zeros and ones used in traditional computing. For the first time, this approach allows information to be collected from all qubits at once.

Optical Cavities Enable Faster Qubit Readout

In research published in Nature, the team describes a system made up of 40 optical cavities, each holding a single atom qubit, along with a larger prototype that contains more than 500 cavities. The results point to a realistic route toward building quantum computing networks that could one day include as many as a million qubits.

"If we want to make a quantum computer, we need to be able to read information out of the quantum bits very quickly," said Jon Simon, the study's senior author and associate professor of physics and of applied physics in Stanford's School of Humanities and Sciences. "Until now, there hasn't been a practical way to do that at scale because atoms just don't emit light fast enough, and on top of that, they spew it out in all directions. An optical cavity can efficiently guide emitted light toward a particular direction, and now we've found a way to equip each atom in a quantum computer within its own individual cavity."

An optical cavity works by trapping light between two or more reflective surfaces, causing it to bounce back and forth. The effect can be compared to standing between mirrors in a fun house, where reflections seem to stretch endlessly into the distance. In scientific settings, these cavities are far smaller and use repeated passes of a laser beam to extract information from atoms.

Although optical cavities have been studied for decades, they have been difficult to use with atoms because atoms are extremely small and nearly transparent. Getting light to interact with them strongly enough has been a persistent challenge.

Rather than relying on many repeated reflections, the Stanford team introduced microlenses inside each cavity to tightly focus light onto a single atom. Even with fewer light bounces, this method proved more effective at pulling quantum information from the atom.

"We have developed a new type of cavity architecture; it's not just two mirrors anymore," said Adam Shaw, a Stanford Science Fellow and first author on the study. "We hope this will enable us to build dramatically faster, distributed quantum computers that can talk to each other with much faster data rates."

Beyond the Binary Limits of Classical Computing

Conventional computers process information using bits that represent either zero or one. Quantum computers operate using qubits, which are based on the quantum states of tiny particles. A qubit can represent zero, one, or both states at the same time, allowing quantum systems to handle certain calculations far more efficiently than classical machines.

"A classical computer has to churn through possibilities one by one, looking for the correct answer," said Simon. "But a quantum computer acts like noise-canceling headphones that compare combinations of answers, amplifying the right ones while muffling the wrong ones."

Scientists estimate that quantum computers will need millions of qubits to outperform today's most powerful supercomputers. According to Simon, reaching that level will likely require connecting many quantum computers into large networks. The parallel light-based interface demonstrated in this study provides an efficient foundation for scaling up to those sizes.

The researchers showed a working 40-cavity array in the current study, along with a proof-of-concept system containing more than 500 cavities. Their next goal is to expand to tens of thousands. Looking further ahead, the team envisions quantum data centers in which individual quantum computers are linked through cavity-based network interfaces to form full-scale quantum supercomputers.

Broader Scientific and Technological Impact

Significant engineering hurdles remain, but the researchers believe the potential benefits are substantial. Large-scale quantum computers could lead to breakthroughs in materials design and chemical synthesis, including applications related to drug discovery, as well as advances in code breaking.

The ability to efficiently collect light also has implications beyond computing. Cavity arrays could improve biosensing and microscopy, supporting progress in medical and biological research. Quantum networks may even contribute to astronomy by enabling optical telescopes with enhanced resolution, potentially allowing scientists to directly observe planets orbiting stars beyond our solar system.

"As we understand more about how to manipulate light at a single particle level, I think it will transform our ability to see the world," Shaw said.

#10898. Scientists create smart synthetic skin that can hide images and change shape

Synthetic materials are widely used across science, engineering, and industry, but most are designed to perform only a narrow range of tasks. A research team at Penn State set out to change that. Led by Hongtao Sun, assistant professor of industrial and manufacturing engineering (IME), the group developed a new fabrication technique that can produce multifunctional "smart synthetic skin." These adaptable materials can be programmed to perform a wide variety of tasks, including hiding or revealing information, enabling adaptive camouflage, and supporting soft robotic systems.

Using this new approach, the researchers created a programmable smart skin made from hydrogel, a soft, water-rich material. Unlike conventional synthetic materials with fixed behaviors, this smart skin can be tuned to respond in multiple ways. Its appearance, mechanical behavior, surface texture, and ability to change shape can all be adjusted when the material is exposed to external triggers such as heat, solvents, or physical stress.

The findings were published in Nature Communications, where the study was also selected for Editors' Highlights.

Inspired by Octopus Skin and Living Systems

Sun, the project's principal investigator, said the concept was inspired by cephalopods such as octopuses, which can rapidly alter the look and texture of their skin. These animals use such changes to blend into their surroundings or communicate with one another.

"Cephalopods use a complex system of muscles and nerves to exhibit dynamic control over the appearance and texture of their skin," Sun said. "Inspired by these soft organisms, we developed a 4D-printing system to capture that idea in a synthetic, soft material."

Sun also holds affiliations in biomedical engineering, material science and engineering, and the Materials Research Institute at Penn State. He described the process as 4D printing because the printed objects are not static. Instead, they can actively change in response to environmental conditions.

Printing Digital Instructions Into Material

To achieve this adaptability, the team used a method called halftone-encoded printing. This technique converts image or texture data into binary ones and zeros and embeds that information directly into the material. The approach is similar to how dot patterns are used in newspapers or photographs to create images.

By encoding these digital patterns within the hydrogel, the researchers can program how the smart skin reacts to different stimuli. The printed patterns determine how various regions of the material respond. Some areas may swell, shrink, or soften more than others when exposed to temperature changes, liquids, or mechanical forces. By carefully designing these patterns, the team can control the material's overall behavior.

"In simple terms, we're printing instructions into the material," Sun explained. "Those instructions tell the skin how to react when something changes around it."

One of the most eye-catching demonstrations involved the material's ability to conceal and reveal visual information. Haoqing Yang, a doctoral candidate in IME and the paper's first author, said this capability highlights the potential of the smart skin.

To demonstrate the effect, the team encoded an image of the Mona Lisa into the hydrogel film. When the material was washed with ethanol, it appeared transparent and showed no visible image. The hidden image became clear only after the film was placed in ice water or gradually heated.

Yang noted that the Mona Lisa was used only as an example. The printing technique allows virtually any image to be encoded into the hydrogel.

"This behavior could be used for camouflage, where a surface blends into its environment, or for information encryption, where messages are hidden and only revealed under specific conditions," Yang said.

The researchers also showed that concealed patterns could be detected by gently stretching the material and analyzing how it deforms using digital image correlation analysis. This means information can be revealed not only visually, but also through mechanical interaction, adding an extra level of security.

The smart skin also demonstrated remarkable flexibility. According to Sun, the material can easily shift from a flat sheet into complex, bio-inspired shapes with detailed surface textures. Unlike many other shape-changing materials, this transformation does not require multiple layers or different substances.

Instead, the changes in shape and texture are controlled entirely by the digitally printed halftone patterns within a single sheet. This allows the material to replicate effects similar to those seen in cephalopod skin.

Building on this capability, the team showed that multiple functions can be programmed to work together. By carefully designing the halftone patterns, they encoded the Mona Lisa image into flat films that later transformed into three-dimensional forms. As the sheets curved into dome-like shapes, the hidden image slowly appeared, showing that changes in shape and visual appearance can be coordinated within one material.

"Similar to how cephalopods coordinate body shape and skin patterning, the synthetic smart skin can simultaneously control what it looks like and how it deforms, all within a single, soft material," Sun said.

Expanding the Potential of 4D-Printed Hydrogels

Sun said the new work builds on earlier research by the team on 4D-printed smart hydrogels, which was also published in Nature Communications. That earlier study focused on combining mechanical properties with programmable transitions from flat to three-dimensional forms. In the current research, the team expanded the approach by using halftone-encoded 4D printing to integrate even more functions into a single hydrogel film.

Looking ahead, the researchers aim to create a scalable and versatile platform that allows precise digital encoding of multiple functions within one adaptive material.

"This interdisciplinary research at the intersection of advanced manufacturing, intelligent materials and mechanics opens new opportunities with broad implications for stimulus-responsive systems, biomimetic engineering, advanced encryption technologies, biomedical devices and more," Sun said.

The study also included Penn State co-authors Haotian Li and Juchen Zhang, both doctoral candidates in IME, and Tengxiao Liu, a lecturer in biomedical engineering. H. Jerry Qi, professor of mechanical engineering at Georgia Institute of Technology, also collaborated on the project.

#10886. Brain inspired machines are better at math than expected

Computers designed to mimic the structure of the human brain are showing an unexpected strength. They can solve some of the demanding mathematical equations that lie at the heart of major scientific and engineering problems.

In a study published in Nature Machine Intelligence, Sandia National Laboratories computational neuroscientists Brad Theilman and Brad Aimone introduced a new algorithm that allows neuromorphic hardware to solve partial differential equations, or PDEs -- the mathematical foundation for modeling phenomena such as fluid dynamics, electromagnetic fields and structural mechanics.

The results demonstrate that neuromorphic systems can handle these equations efficiently. The advance could help open the door to the first neuromorphic supercomputer, offering a new path toward energy efficient computing for national security and other critical applications.

The research was funded by the Department of Energy's Office of Science through the Advanced Scientific Computing Research and Basic Energy Sciences programs, as well as the National Nuclear Security Administration's Advanced Simulation and Computing program.

Solving Partial Differential Equations With Brain Like Hardware

Partial differential equations are essential for simulating real world systems. They are used to forecast weather, analyze how materials respond to stress, and model complex physical processes. Traditionally, solving PDEs requires enormous computing power. Neuromorphic computers approach the problem differently by processing information in ways that resemble how the brain operates.

"We're just starting to have computational systems that can exhibit intelligent-like behavior. But they look nothing like the brain, and the amount of resources that they require is ridiculous, frankly," Theilman said.

For years, neuromorphic systems were mainly viewed as tools for pattern recognition or for speeding up artificial neural networks. Few expected them to manage mathematically rigorous problems such as PDEs, which are typically handled by large scale supercomputers.

Aimone and Theilman were not surprised by the outcome. They argue that the human brain routinely carries out highly complex calculations, even if people are unaware of it.

"Pick any sort of motor control task -- like hitting a tennis ball or swinging a bat at a baseball," Aimone said. "These are very sophisticated computations. They are exascale-level problems that our brains are capable of doing very cheaply."

Energy Efficient Computing for National Security

The findings could have major implications for the National Nuclear Security Administration, which is responsible for maintaining the nation's nuclear deterrent. Supercomputers used across the nuclear weapons complex consume vast amounts of electricity to simulate the physics of nuclear systems and other high stakes scenarios.

Neuromorphic computing may provide a way to significantly cut energy use while still delivering strong computational performance. By solving PDEs in a brain inspired manner, these systems suggest that large simulations could be run using far less power than conventional supercomputers require.

"You can solve real physics problems with brain-like computation," Aimone said. "That's something you wouldn't expect because people's intuition goes the opposite way. And in fact, that intuition is often wrong."

The team envisions neuromorphic supercomputers eventually becoming central to Sandia's mission of protecting national security.

What Neuromorphic Computing Reveals About the Brain

Beyond engineering advances, the research also touches on deeper questions about intelligence and how the brain performs calculations. The algorithm developed by Theilman and Aimone closely mirrors the structure and behavior of cortical networks.

"We based our circuit on a relatively well-known model in the computational neuroscience world," Theilman said. "We've shown the model has a natural but non-obvious link to PDEs, and that link hasn't been made until now -- 12 years after the model was introduced."

The researchers believe this work could help connect neuroscience with applied mathematics, offering new understanding of how the brain processes information.

"Diseases of the brain could be diseases of computation," Aimone said. "But we don't have a solid grasp on how the brain performs computations yet."

If that idea proves correct, neuromorphic computing might one day contribute to better understanding and treatment of neurological disorders such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's.

Building the Next Generation of Supercomputers

Neuromorphic computing remains an emerging field, but this work represents an important step forward. The Sandia team hopes their results will encourage collaboration among mathematicians, neuroscientists and engineers to expand what this technology can achieve.

"If we've already shown that we can import this relatively basic but fundamental applied math algorithm into neuromorphic -- is there a corresponding neuromorphic formulation for even more advanced applied math techniques?" Theilman said.

As development continues, the researchers are optimistic. "We have a foot in the door for understanding the scientific questions, but also we have something that solves a real problem," Theilman said.
