#6995. OpenAI Intros Dots, AI Agents that Continue Working Between User Conversations

OpenAI has introduced Dots, GPT-6 Astra-powered agents that run on their own cloud computers, using connected applications to carry on with assignments between conversations and incorporating users' feedback as they go.

OpenAI is also previewing specialist Dots for organizations, with separate identities and credentials for defined responsibilities. Those enterprise deployments are beginning with focused pilots. The company is working with Microsoft on integration with Agent 365's governance and security controls.

The broader ambition seems to be to make delegation last beyond a chat session. That change could reduce the need to repeatedly explain a project. It also makes supervision a continuing obligation: An instruction that was appropriate yesterday may no longer fit today's circumstances.

Users currently cannot inspect or directly edit individual Dot memories. Deleting a Dot deletes its own context, while files and conversations it created remain separately stored. Plugin permissions are shared across Dots, ChatGPT, ChatGPT Work, and Codex.

For an organization, that creates two related governance questions: what an agent can retrieve now, and what it already knows. Revoking a connection addresses the first without necessarily resolving the second.

OpenAI's safety account distinguishes authorized tasks that continue in the background from unsolicited "proactive research." The latter uses tools restricted to reading permitted sources. Those research tasks cannot directly send messages, change connected application content, or control a browser or desktop. Any subsequent action must pass the usual rules and checks.

A separate system, Auto-review, checks certain planned actions against instructions and safety requirements. OpenAI says the agent cannot modify the controls enforcing those checks.

"Dots can still make mistakes," the company cautions.

OpenAI's Dots system-card appendix acknowledges that persistence and multi-agent systems are not new. Its evaluations examine how continuing work affects authorization boundaries.

In one test involving successive related tasks, moderate scope violations increased from 8.6% to 19.7% when the number of intervening tasks doubled from five to 10. Examples included carrying information between unrelated tasks or editing a shared document beyond the intended scope. The company reported no severe breaches or exfiltration in that evaluation.

These are controlled test results, not measured failure rates in customer deployments. They nevertheless identify a problem that a successful demonstration cannot settle: whether an agent keeps the right boundaries as its workload accumulates.

According to OpenAI's DevDay recap, Dots are available to Pro and Business Premium users in eligible markets. Enterprise, Edu, and Healthcare access is an administrator-enabled beta, off by default.

The operational question is how organizations will keep delegated authority current. A continuing assignment needs a human owner who can revise its scope and decide when to stop it. Otherwise, the convenience of leaving work with an agent can outlast the reason it was authorized.

#6990. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

The company identifies penetration testing and exploit generation among activities that may trigger cybersecurity fallbacks. Source-code vulnerability scanning remains supported. Biology and reasoning-extraction safeguards can block requests outright, rather than route them elsewhere.

Organizations should distinguish those planned arrangements from access available today.

The broader enterprise issue is accountability. When an AI service changes models during an assignment, someone still needs to determine whether the resulting work is suitable. A visible handoff helps, but organizations must decide how employees should respond to it.

#6975. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6909. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

#6840. Stop Hunting for Pricey Antiques—Designers Say These 9 Humble Finds Give a Home So Much Character

They’re affordable, widely available, and stylish.

It’s any avid thrifter’s dream to stumble upon an extremely valuable piece of decor while browsing the shelves of their favorite antique store. But while you’re hunting for rare treasures, don’t discount the less-expensive finds—they can be the secret to a home that looks instantly filled with character.

“To me, a collected home is much more interesting when everything isn’t perfect or chosen for its monetary value,” designer Lauren Sullivan of Well by Design tells House Beautiful. “Often, it’s the less precious pieces that give a room its personality.”

Even as older generations are continuing to downsize and offload unwanted things to local thrift stores, it still might be hard to find the diamonds in the rough. Keep reading to find out what Sullivan, and a handful of other designers, say you should be on the lookout for on your next thrifting visit.

The most valuable antiques are the ones that have been passed down through generations. In Sullivan’s own bathroom, the designer keeps her great-grandmother’s vanity set on the countertop, its aged finish providing beautiful contrast with the more contemporary marble and finishes. In her bedroom, the same great-grandmother’s small bedside clock found a place on Sullivan’s nightstand.

“Neither is particularly valuable monetarily, but I love incorporating pieces that have been passed down through generations into a home filled with both old and new,” Sullivan says.

Designers love incorporating artwork into homes to add personality and dimension, but Sullivan, as well as designer Becca Meyer of BB Meyer Design, believe that antique shops are where you can really find some hidden gems.

“Some of my favorite pieces of art have come from flea markets or antique shops for very little money,” Meyer says. “I much prefer these to mass produced prints.” You’re more likely to find pieces with aged textures at these types of shops, and that patinaed quality creates an entirely unique look.

Sullivan explains that she’s actually particularly drawn to prints, as well as smaller paintings and silhouettes. She even has an inexpensive print of The Skating Minister in a vintage frame in one of her guest rooms, pictured above. “These smaller, or sometimes quirky, pieces can bring so much personality to a space without requiring a significant investment,” she adds. They may not be originals, but they’re definitely unique.

Old pottery and ceramics are widely available, and they are usually affordable as well. Designer Brooke Snyder of Design Hutch loves how they make any place feel more curated.

The imperfections these handmade pieces carry, from uneven shapes, variations in glaze, or evidence of years of use, are what help these items bring a warmth to a room, particularly when placed near more refined materials, according to Sullivan. They feel humble, maybe even utilitarian, especially when displayed in a kitchen. Or, better yet, used in a kitchen (as long as they’re safe).

“They're beautiful on their own but also so functional for holding utensils or branches or flowers,” Meyer adds. “They make any space feel a little more lived-in and loved and help tell a story—and they don't break the bank!”

“I love incorporating vintage plates, old copper pots, and woven baskets into a home,” explains designer Joe Waroquier. Though these are run-of-the-mill, everyday objects, they can take on and provide an entirely different life when displayed in the home as decor. Mismatched plates on a wall, a collection of copper pots hanging on a rod or resting on shelves in the kitchen, an old crock filled with branches—these items invite in warmth, texture, and more personality.

“I’m particularly drawn to pieces that show their age, whether it’s a little patina, a worn finish, or an imperfect glaze,” he adds. “I love mixing these finds with more contemporary furnishings because that contrast makes a room feel collected and personal rather than overly designed.”

Mirrors can be intriguing focal points of a room, or they can add dimension to a gallery wall. However you want to style them, Snyder loves finding these reflective pieces at antique malls and vintage shops.

“Purchasing something that you don’t see every day from a retailer allows this unique piece to stand out on its own, without feeling like something you see everywhere or feels too simple,” she explains.

As Snyder puts it, antique table lamps provide high visual impact with low investment. Adding one to your accent table or on a bookshelf is an easy way to make your space feel more curated while inviting in another level of ambience with the lighting.

These vintage lamps in the above space, for instance, were bought from a collector and make the primary bedroom feel much more charming and curated. And unlike antique sconces, there’s no need to hardwire these into the wall.

Besides pottery and lamps, designer Ryann Swan Hackett of Ryann Swan Design loves to find and collect other small tabletop decor for her projects. Think candleholders, vintage coffee table books, and trays—things that can layer a little history and individuality into a room or simple vignette.

“If you’re lucky,” Swan Hackett says of the vintage coffee table books, “you’ll find an old inscription inside, which gives the book a story of its own.”

In terms of midsize accent pieces, Meyer says that she has a soft spot for finding antique stools “because you can pop them into any space to add depth and dimension.” Tuck them beside an armchair in the living room, add them to an empty kitchen corner and place a plant on top, or stick one in a bathroom by the tub for somewhere to keep a drink while relaxing. And if you find an upholstered one that could use new fabric, switching it out for a pattern you love is probably worth it.

“These bring a natural warmth that can be difficult to replicate with something new, and their small size makes them fairly reasonable in price for a vintage furnishing piece,” Meyer says.

A larger piece of furniture is likely always going to be more expensive than a smaller one, but designer Yena Jung of By Yena Designs thinks vintage hutches are the versatile antiques people should pay more attention to. They come in many different shapes, styles, and sizes, and can easily make a home in any room of the house.

“A beautiful vintage hutch can add warmth, charm, and a sense of history to a space,” Jung says. “I especially love how their imperfections and natural patina make them feel collected over time.”

This is the woman who won an Academy Award for playing a fierce, unpredictable artist in Vicky Cristina Barcelona (making her the first and only Spanish actress to win an Oscar), but who also appears in Sex and the City 2 for literally one minute. It’s leathery and musky, but it has a good hit of cedar that combines to leans it into the world of amber. It also has a lot of floral notes as it dries on the skin to balance it all out. It’s a nicely layered fragrance. A purist might not call this amber, but we think it’s well within the sweet, resinous world of these other fragrances.

Not ready to commit to the full bottle? The unisex cologne also comes as a perfume oil in a compact rollerball perfect for a trial run or as a stocking stuffer.

As I noted in my intro, amber fragrances can take on a variety of scent profiles, all capturing the warmth and depth of true amber. Rich gourmand options like Horace’s Vintage Vanilla are a great pick if you love scents inspired by the culinary world, but don’t worry, it’s expertly balanced with a mix of woodiness and spice so you won’t end up smelling like you went overboard on your marshmallow-y Victoria’s Secret body mist.

Don’t even want to smell like an edible vanilla confection? No problem. The brand’s signature Oud Rose is another solid amber-forward scent that leans on floral notes to balance its intensity.

Diptyque is another one of those bougie fragrance houses everyone recognizes, yet for some reason I rarely reach for their scents over my tried-and-true favorites. But after smelling L’Eau Trois Eau de Toilette, I might have to rethink that stance. This one immediately transported me to being in Italy and stepping into a Duomo, where incense and myrrh linger in the air.

It takes a surprisingly verdant and green approach to the amber family, but those resinous top notes anchor everything with a spicy-smoky warmth. The result is a scent that feels refined, atmospheric, and just mysterious enough to keep you coming back for another sniff.

Sure, amber scents can be sweet and gourmand, but they can also be spicy and sophisticated. Giardini di Toscana’s Bianco Oro is a great example of the latter. At first spritz, you mostly get spicy pink and black pepper with a backdrop of amber, but as the scent lingers, the rich amber notes take center stage along with smooth musks and creamy woods. As it fades, it becomes a bit powdery, but the trio of spices, amber, and musks are still there. It’s the perfect scent to wear out to a nice dinner or evening event, but it works just as well in the daytime.

It’s a general rule that amber and woods go great together, since amber provides a warm richness that blends well with dry, crisp woods. That combination is at the foundation of Dolce & Gabbana’s The One for Men EDP, but before we get there, we actually get a touch of bright and juicy citrus from Tarocco orange and grapefruit as well as a natural, herbal hint of clary sage at the heart that adds a bit of earthiness to this fragrance. Amber and woods mingle at the base, grounding the lighter, more vibrant opening in something warm and rich.

Guerlain makes a handful of luxury amber fragrances, but Tobacco Honey is the one that stands out from the rest. It’s an intense blend of—you guessed it—tobacco leaves and golden honey, which creates a beautifully bold contrast of smoky and sweet. It emphasizes the sweet side of the ambery tobacco accord at the heart of this scent, enveloping the dry tobacco notes in vanilla, tonka bean, and sesame to create a rich, creamy heart that gets lifted up by vibrant, sweet honey. If you’ve ever wanted to try a tobacco fragrance but worried it would smell too smoky (or too much like cigarettes), this is the one that will change your mind.

For something a bit lighter and more airy than the mostly rich and deep amber scents on this list, try Golden Morning from Lafco’s new (and first-ever) personal fragrance collection. Like a cup of hot chamomile tea with honey, it’s soothing, warm, and a little sweet. Bright and citrusy at the top with bergamot and apple blossom, it then introduces chamomile and a floral heart of lavender, violet leaf, and rose. The dry down is smooth and sweet with honey, tonka bean, amber, and musks.

Okay, I’m not saying the other fragrances on this list can’t also be considered “bougie,” but Maison Francis Kurkdjian Paris’ Baccarat Rouge 540 takes luxury to an entirely new level. And yes, while the price tag is particularly eye-watering, I have to admit: this scent is absolutely worth it. A perfectly balanced blend of woody, floral, and ambery notes, this fragrance uses high-quality ingredients with a higher molecular weight, meaning they cling to the skin longer and create a richer scent profile with impressive staying power. In short, a little goes a very long way with this one…if that helps you justify the price tag.

Sitting somewhere between gourmand and citrus on the fragrance spectrum, Arquiste’s L’Or de Louis is one of those amber scents that just radiates richness. The 24K gold flakes are a little too on-the-nose, but the the story behind the fragrance does a better job of telling a story of opulence. It was inspired by the moments when King Louis XIV and his court strolled Versailles’ Grand Orangery, enjoying the flowering orange trees warmed by bonfires.

It also won The Fragrance Foundation’s Indie Fragrance of the Year Award in 2024, so the hoity-toity storytelling is backed by a scent that actually smells…well…good. The result is a golden aura of perfectly blended smokiness, fruitiness, and depth. A fragrance truly fit for a king.

If your nose is on the more sensitive side and you’re looking for a warm, enveloping scent that doesn’t overwhelm with heavy muskiness, D’Orsay’s Te dire oui. V.H. Eau de Parfum is a perfect choice. Built on leather, it avoids the overpowering muskiness found in many amber and leather fragrances, opting instead for a cleaner, sportier interpretation rounded out by a subtle saffron and rose finish.

This aligns perfectly with the brand’s philosophy, which favors fragrances that act as personal rituals—subtle companions that are seamlessly woven into everyday life—rather than scents that dominate every room you enter.

#6805. Samantha Morton Makes Every Minute Count

The captivating actress only needed one scene to make her mark on The Odyssey.

Samantha Morton begins each day by letting the light in. If she’s in her house in the English countryside, she’ll creep down the stairs to open the curtains and let sunshine stream onto the peacock feathers on her vintage sofa, making them glitter and gleam like jewels. If she’s in London, she’ll start her day outside, people-watching with coffee and a pastry from her favorite café. “I’m all about the light,” Morton explains. “I need to let the morning light into my heart and be in nature.”

It makes sense, then, that even when she’s inhabiting shadowy figures and dark subject matter, she can still find the light in the women she portrays. Take her much-talked-about role as Circe, the witch who transforms Odysseus’s men into pigs in Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey. Morton delivers a haunting performance; just try to take your eyes off her as she builds the intensity, humming as she shapes the men’s faces into beasts with her hands. After the epic drama’s massive opening this summer, in a cast full of A-listers like Matt Damon, Anne Hathaway, and Robert Pattinson, it was Morton that everyone was talking about. Though she appears in roughly just 10 minutes of the nearly three-hour film, her scene sticks with you long after the credits roll.

Yet Morton saw there was more to Circe than her hardened exterior. Her Circe delivers a powerful monologue to Odysseus (Damon) about his men’s primal instincts to rape and pillage, exposing their loss of humanity and animalistic greed, quite literally, and forcing him to confront the truth about what his men have become—the terrifying and marauding sea people they themselves have been warned about. “I read the script and I just felt an affinity with this woman,” Morton says, getting choked up. “It makes me quite emotional thinking about it, because I just understood her. I understood the words she was saying.” And when the time came to deliver the pivotal monologue, “I closed my eyes and [harnessed] all the truth I felt in my heart,” she recalls. “I allowed the winds to take me—I allowed magic to happen.”

Though Morton has been acting for most of her life, her role as Circe has brought newfound attention. “She’s baaack,” Morton says of the buzz around her now. “I’ve always been there—I work quietly and carefully and meticulously, but having the opportunity to be in a Christopher Nolan film has meant that there’s more visibility.” After starting her career in British TV, she charmed audiences with her portrayal of a mute woman in 1999’s Sweet and Lowdown without her ever saying a word, earning Morton her first Oscar nomination. Leading roles followed in Morvern Callar and Minority Report. In 2004, Morton picked up her second Academy Award nod for In America, playing the matriarch of an Irish immigrant family; with every questionable decision her character makes, you wish you could reach through the screen and hug her. She’s worked steadily—in The Walking Dead, Fantastic Beasts and Where to Find Them, and another brief but memorable arc in The Whale—but The Odyssey is her first performance in over two decades that has made her the subject of awards chatter again.

You could say Morton is where she is now because other people saw the light in her long before the industry did. She grew up in poverty in Nottingham, England, and had cycled through 12 foster families before age 7. (Her foster experience inspired her BAFTA-winning television film The Unloved, which she directed, wrote, and produced in 2009.) Morton began acting because of a mentor at school who got to know her. “There was a teacher, Mr. Thompson, who said, ‘You should do drama.’ And I was like, ‘What’s drama?’ He gave us pages and we did a play and I loved it.” At her teacher’s urging, she auditioned for a local drama club, and nearly missed her shot: “The letter that they sent didn’t get to me because I moved foster families. So I did get a recall, but I didn’t know.” The next year, a social worker jumped in to get Morton another audition, and she got in, an experience that she says “saved me.”
