#6965. It’s Not Too Late to Plant These 5 Perennials for a Gorgeous Fall Garden

Add cheer to gray days with these late-season planting picks.

Consistent wind and rain might not exactly be “gardening weather,” but you can still add color to your garden this autumn. If you have a bare patch to fill, some established perennials can be planted now—as long as the ground isn't waterlogged or frozen.

As autumn deepens and the last of the summer-flowering plants begin to fade, our gardens can start to look a little tired. Add the persistent drizzle and gray skies, and it's easy to feel that the color has gone for the year.

The good news is that there's still time to brighten things up. Established autumn-flowering perennials are a quick and reliable way to bring color and seasonal interest to your garden, and a mild, wet spell can help newly planted roots settle in.

The key is to check the conditions first: avoid planting directly into waterlogged soil, which can suffocate plants, or when the ground is frozen. If the soil is too wet to work, choose pots with good drainage and keep them somewhere sheltered from strong winds.

As Charlotte McGrattan, hardy nursery stock buyer at Hillier Garden Centers, adds, newly planted perennials “require watering well while they establish.” Rain can help with that, but check the soil rather than assuming your plants have had enough: containers, in particular, may still need watering.

Here, McGrattan shares five autumn-flowering perennials you can plant now.

Heleniums are tough, hardy, and easy to grow. If your soil is very wet or compacted, it's best to plant them in a pot positioned in a sheltered spot away from the wind.

“Heleniums add an abundance of color, bursting from mid-summer through into autumn. There are hundreds of varieties to choose from, from hot yellows and reds to vibrant oranges,” McGrattan says.

In these mild temperatures, it's also not too late to plant heliopsis, also known as “false sunflower.” “They not only make impressive, colorful features in borders and beds, attracting an array of pollinators but can also make for lovely cut flowers,” says McGrattan.

“They grow in clumps with branching stems that give a bushy habit, and the flowering period is between six and eight weeks. To encourage buds to form, deadhead any spent flowers.”

While spring is generally the best time to plant penstemons—which are available in shades of purple, pink, white, and blue—you can still plant them in pots now.

“With some varieties being bi-colors combining two shades, they bring welcome color to the garden,” says McGrattan. “Penstemon thrives in well-drained soil and is very tolerant to drought conditions.”

Crocosmias are a great way to bring both height and vibrant color to your autumn borders, as long as you plant established plants with roots instead of growing them from corms (bulbs). “Originating from South Africa, they require fertile, moist, but well-drained soil,” McGrattan adds.

You can also still plant potted Japanese anemones in late autumn, provided the ground is neither frozen nor waterlogged. “Japanese anemones boast cup-shaped pink or white flowers on the top of tall stems and can spread rapidly, so can be a great means of filling borders,” says McGrattan.

#6948. A Crash Course in Corduroy

Every product was carefully curated by an Esquire editor. We may earn a commission from these links. Here’s how we test products and why you should trust us.

It’s the perfect time of year for a tough, warm fabric with tons of texture. Here’s how to work it into your wardrobe.

According to a recent and deeply unscientific poll conducted by yours truly, some guys are confounded by corduroy. They like it. They want to wear it. But they’re not quite sure how it figures into their wardrobes. This is a pity, because corduroy is great. It’s warm, it’s hard-wearing, it delivers texture galore, and it can work beautifully in the context of all different types of personal style.

So in the interest of getting these guys—and you!—properly outfitted, I’m offering this crash course in corduroy. It’ll give you the key information you need in order to understand the fabric’s history and how you can wear it now. Let’s dive in.

There’s a great story about the name corduroy being derived from the French phrase “corde du roi,” or “cord of the king.” It conjures up all sorts of noble associations and sounds cool, like so much menswear lore does. The problem is, it’s likely apocryphal. No one really knows the root of the word for sure. (But if you want to bust out that tidbit at a dinner party and let your friends believe it, we wouldn’t blame you.)

What we do know is that corduroy traces its roots back to a category of fabric called fustian—basically heavy cloth, often used for menswear, that was usually woven from cotton (though there have been woolen and linen variations over the many centuries that fustian has existed). The name might come from the city of Fustat, near Cairo, which manufactured that sort of cloth. Or maybe it doesn’t. More menswear lore!

What sets corduroy apart from other fustian cloths like velveteen and moleskin is its distinctive ribbing. That innovation is newer; it was developed in Manchester around the middle of the 18th century, when the city became a center of production for cotton fabrics like velvet and the modern iteration of corduroy. The ribs are known as wales, and they’re created by weaving extra thread into the base fabric and then trimming them to created tufted, vertical ridges offset by channels between them.

This “modern” corduroy quickly became a workwear staple thanks to its durability and warmth. It was only later that it migrated to British country clothing and, eventually, to the rest of the globe. It slowly filtered into U.S. academia throughout the first half of the 20th century, eventually leading to the well-known stereotype of the college professor with the well-worn and elbow-patched corduroy jacket. It hit the counterculture in the ’60s. And it absolutely boomed in the ’70s, showing up on the era’s leading men both on and off the silver screen.

Since then, as with all menswear staples, its popularity has ebbed and flowed. But like denim, it never truly goes out of style. It’s all about how you wear it.

The width of the wales is measured by how many you can fit into an inch, so an 8-wale corduroy has thicker ridges than, say, a 12-wale corduroy. Most “standard” corduroy sits around 11 to 13 wales per inch. Chunkier stuff has a lower number, while “pinwale” or “needlecord” corduroy is higher, around 16 or above. As you might guess, the chunkier stuff reads more rugged and the finer stuff feels more refined.

Developed in and named after the town of New Bedford, Massachusetts, this fabric has distinctive vertical ridges, loads of durability, and roots in the world of workwear. It’s not quite corduroy, though. The ridges are created in the way the base fabric is woven, not by adding additional threads and trimming them. Still, it’s worth having on your radar if you like the look and feel of corduroy.

Just … wear it. I know that sounds like fake advice, but it’s really not worth overthinking it. Yes, you’ll want to think about the weather, because corduroy can wear warmer than other fabrics. And you might want to get yourself used to the idea of the occasional whoosh of the ribbed fabric rubbing against itself. (It’s not as distracting as the crinkle of a nylon shell suit, but it’s audible.) But corduroy is in its current form because it works. It wears in beautifully, fends off the cold, and adds some always welcome depth and texture to any outfit.

If you’re still nervous, start with something simple, like a corduroy jacket—your call on whether it’s a sport coat or something more utilitarian—or some five-pocket cord pants. You could rock it as an overshirt (go wide wale) or as a button-up. You could even channel everyone from Wes Anderson to Robert Redford with a full corduroy suit, though you might want to avoid the elbow patches if you don’t want to look too professorial.

As for what to wear it with? Well, nothing too techy. A super-stretchy golf shirt doesn’t exactly match the vibe; ditto for polyester joggers. And nothing too fine or fancy, either. Because of its more casual and countryish vibe, corduroy might not be the best complement to spit-polished oxford shoes or a particularly delicate worsted wool. The idea is to look for a sense of cohesion with your outfit, and anything that reads as deliberately futuristic or self-consciously dressy could detract from that.

Otherwise, options abound, especially when you start talking about other classic menswear fabrics and items. Corduroy looks great with beefy oxford-cloth button-downs. It plays well with denim and chino and flannel and hopsack. And don’t be afraid of going all in on texture. Bust out a shaggy Shetland or cable-knit sweater. Bring some tweed into the mix. Corduroy shouldn’t be confusing; it should be easy. So play around a little. You’re bound to have a (sorry) wale of a good time.

In 2022, the novel Corinne was published. At the story’s center is Corinne, a woman who walked away from family and her fundamentalist upbringing but finds herself unable to forget Enoch, her first love. Rebecca Morrow, the name on the book’s cover, was described as a “pseudonym for a New York Times bestselling author,” and though readers guessed at who that author might be, Rebecca Morrow’s identity remained a mystery until now.

Rebecca Morrow is Rainbow Rowell, the celebrated author of Cherry Baby and Eleanor & Park, among others. Corinne isn’t about her life, but it has parallels, and in 2022, publishing something so personal felt impossible. Now denying what she has come to consider her favorite book seems unthinkable. In July 2027, Corinne will be reissued with Rowell’s name on the cover, and in 2028 she will publish Catch Us the Foxes, a Corinne sequel.

Here Rowell talks about the decision to write Corinne—and deciding to finally put her name on it—in her own words.

In the darkest hours of the COVID-19 pandemic—when the future seemed uncertain, and the rest of the world felt far away—I did something very strange for me.

I wrote a book, knowing that I couldn’t let anyone read it.

“I just need to do this,” I told my literary agent. “And then I’ll write something we can sell.” It was a good time to sell. I’d just published three No. 1 bestsellers in a row. “I just need to get this out of me first.”

I wrote the first draft of the novel almost feverishly. It was about a woman who is cast out of her strict Christian church as a teenager and cut off by her family. She comes back home as an adult to reckon with the past—and with the man who was the catalyst for her exile.

Really, the book was about all the things I’d always been too scared to write about, or even to talk about….Faith. Shame. Sex. (More sex. Unclean sex. Forbidden sex. Sex in the same room as a Bible.) And, most thornily, how it feels to leave a fundamentalist church. The way you eventually realize that you can’t leave. That you’ll always take it with you, because you were made there. Baked there. Poured and set.

When I was done with the manuscript, it was my favorite thing I’d ever written. Maybe not my best book, but already the one I loved the most.

“But honestly,” I told my agent, “we can’t do anything with this. It’s too personal. And it’s the very last thing any of my readers would want.” I was writing queer YA fantasy. Not whatever this was.

My agent, being an agent, asked if he could read it anyway.

And he liked it. (He liked my beautiful baby!)

We very quickly began talking about a way to publish the book that might sidestep all my fears and anxieties….

There began my strange—and still hardly believable to me—journey as Rebecca Morrow, the pseudonymous author of Corinne.

It was exciting at first. It was different and fun. A pseudonym! A secret identity! A way to drop a novel into the world without having to talk about it, or sell it. Without having to worry about alienating or recruiting my existing readers.

The pseudonym satisfied that still feverish part of me that needed to let the story out, but was too terrified to stand by it.

Oh, I knew it was absurd to write a book about shame and secrets, and then to keep it a secret. (Don’t think this didn’t come up in therapy!) But at the time, it seemed like the only path forward for Corinne. It was this or let the characters die on my hard drive.

My editor and I agonized over how to handle the pseudonym. Traditionally, authors write fake biographies to go with their fake names. But I didn’t want to get caught in a lie if I was found out. And I didn’t want to seek out undeserved goodwill as a “debut author.”

The marketing department really wanted something they could say about Rebecca Morrow. They suggested “lives in Nebraska,” but even that felt like divulging too much. We settled on “Rebecca Morrow is a pseudonym for a New York Times bestselling author.”

My apologies to the bestselling, usually Mormon, authors who got blamed for this weird book. I didn’t see that coming.

I did expect readers to guess that I was the author. Even though I’d freed myself to write Corinne in a new voice and shaken off some of my more recognizable habits, the book ended up sounding more like me than ever. It was like my voice distilled.

Some people did guess. It didn’t bother me. I still didn’t have to talk about the book. I didn’t have to face it.

Corinne is my favorite of my books and beyond that, Enoch and Corinne, the man and woman at its center, are my all-time favorite characters. It’s hard to explain why…

I always write messy, flawed characters, but these two are my messiest and most flawed. Their love is not the stuff of romance. (This novel is not a romance; it will disappoint you if you come to it looking for reassurance or escape.)

Neither Corinne nor Enoch is based on me, though they carry many of my burdens. This is not a hidden memoir. But they feel so real to me. They make mistakes, and I want to forgive them. I want them to find grace. I think about them still, all the time. I worry about what’s become of them.

Ultimately, that’s why I’m stepping forward to claim the book now. At some point, “not having to talk about Corinne” turned into “not being able to talk about Corinne,” even when I desperately wanted to.

I love this book! I want to put it on my shelf, between Any Way the Wind Blows and Scattered Showers. I want readers who liked Slow Dance and Cherry Baby to discover it.

I loved the characters in Corinne so much that I wrote a sequel about them—Catch Us the Foxes—and I want that book to have a life, too. William Morrow is launching both books, properly—Corinne next July, and Catch Us the Foxes in March 2028—in beautiful hardcover editions.

I revised Corinne for the new edition—I went a little deeper on the church stuff this time, and sharpened the focus on some of the characters. It’s a little longer. And, I think, a little better. My favorite designer, Jim Tierney, designed the new matching covers.

I still get a surge of fear and excitement every time I see my name there front and center.

My therapist says that I’m very good at compartmentalizing—keeping some parts of myself shut away from other parts. I would argue that this is a very useful skill! But it does bad things to your soul, over time...being fractured like that.

But as the years have passed, it’s felt like I’ve left something half done. Like Corinne is a truth half shared. A story half told. I needed to get the book out of me, but I ended up cutting myself off from it. That was never the goal.

I’m grateful to “Rebecca Morrow” for making Corinne possible, but I’m finally ready to bring these characters home.

#6888. Better American Sunscreens Are Here—But We’re Still Way Behind the Best

Anyone who’s applied sunscreen only to be left with a chalky white cast, a greasy finish knows American sun care has long been a frustrating experience. The US is decades behind European and Asian counterparts, which have long been blessed with sunscreens that are lighter, more luxurious, and easier to wear every day. For skincare obsessives (this writer included) trips abroad are also opportunities to stock up on superior sunscreens they can’t find at home.

The root of the problem is regulation. The Food and Drug Administration classifies sunscreen as a drug rather than a cosmetic. Because of that distinction, more time and testing is required to get new sunscreens on the shelves, dramatically reducing innovation in American sun protection. But for the first time since the ‘90s, we’ve had some movement on that front. In June of this year, the FDA approved a new sunscreen filter called bemotrizinol—also known as Tinosorb S or BEMT—after years of lobbying in the states by dermatologists, skincare brands, and Congress members. It’s a meaningful step toward better sun care, but the US is still lagging behind.

Bemotrizinol is prized for its ability to provide both UVA and UVB protection while remaining stable in sunlight. That’s a significant advantage over some existing US-approved filters, which offer protection against one type of UV radiation more effectively than the other. BEMT also has the potential to make sunscreen more pleasant to wear: “And because it’s a large, oil-loving molecule, it mostly stays on the surface of your skin instead of soaking in, and it blends into lightweight, non-greasy formulas with no white cast,” says Dr. Joel Spitz, co-founder, chief medical officer, and board-certified dermatologist at Honeydew, a virtual dermatology platform.

Bemotrizinol might be new for the US market, but it’s already been used in sunscreen formulations abroad for 20 years, Spitz says. That long track record helps explain why consumers in the US have spent years coveting the more cosmetically elegant formulas available in countries like South Korea and France. While most consumers would love if major brands like Neutrogena, La Roche-Posay, or Beauty of Joseon brought their European or Asian formulations to the US, those SPFs still contain filters that the FDA hasn’t approved. As a result, many brands are developing entirely new BEMT-centric formulas for use stateside.

Australian label Ultra Violette was the first brand to release a BEMT sunscreen in the US with its Unblock Screen SPF 50 in August 2026. Neutrogena debuted its Ultra Sheer Daily Face Sunscreen SPF 70 in September. Earlier this month French drugstore staple La Roche-Posay put its Anthelios UVDefense Sunscreen SPF 50 up for sale online. Don’t mistake the relatively quick arrival of these products after the FDA’s approval for ease in which brands can develop new formulations. According to Emanuella Louis, La Roche-Posay’s public relations manager, the brand had been working on its new formula for more than two years.

That long lead time reflects the way sunscreen development works: Brands can research and test formulas in anticipation of regulatory changes, then move a product forward when a particular filter gets the green light. Louis says La Roche-Posay has access to filters the FDA may be considering for approval and is “actively working, playing, testing on what possibly those formulas would be.” When a filter is approved, she explains, a formula already in the testing pipeline can be moved up.

Some brands are still tinkering with BEMT formulas for the US. Round Lab, one of South Korea’s most popular sunscreen manufacturers, has sold a version of its SPF in the US since 2024, and is hard at work on a new version of those formulas that contains bemotrizinol.

“Bemotrizinol’s approval in the US is a meaningful change, but it does not mean a Korean formula can simply be brought to the US market as is,” says Koochul Kwon, Round Lab’s chief technology officer. Beyond incorporating the newly approved filter, brands must ensure the ingredients keep the UV filters stably dissolved and working alongside other components, including moisturizing ingredients. The finished product must then meet US requirements for SPF and broad-spectrum testing, product stability, and labeling. With those considerations in mind, Kwon expects Round Lab to release its own bemotrizinol sunscreen sometime in 2027.

A sunscreen is only as useful as a person’s willingness to wear it consistently. Bemotrizinol’s ability to protect against UV damage is important, but its potential to make formulas lighter, less greasy, and more comfortable could be just as consequential to get people to implement it in their everyday skincare routines.

“One of the strengths of Korean sun care is how naturally it has built sun protection into the everyday skincare experience,” Kwon says. “For people to apply enough sunscreen and reapply it when needed, the way it feels matters as much as how well it protects.”

But better filters alone won’t transform American sun care. The real breakthrough will be sunscreens that people actually enjoy wearing—and, as a result, are more likely to apply generously and reapply throughout the day, a key step to ensuring its effectiveness.

Charlotte Palermino, cofounder and chief brand officer of skincare brand Dieux, has been an advocate for easier sunscreen filter approvals ever since she tried her first Japanese sunscreen, Biore UV, a decade ago. She realized sunscreen could stave off skin issues after her dermatologist told her that her hyperpigmentation had been the result of failing to use sun protection. In the years since she has publicly pushed for regulatory reform. She’s hesitant to put an estimated release date on Dieux’s upcoming BEMT-centric sunscreen, simply because the brand is dialed in to ensure its able to deliver a quality, wearable product.

“We’ve gone through this with [Dieux’s] SkinMercy, which is an OTC colloidal oatmeal cream, but for sunscreen, the stability of your sunscreen is so fucking important,” she says. “If my sunscreen separates or if it’s not at that stated SPF 50, if that degrades at all with broad spectrum protection, I would be mortified.”

Palerimo cites brands like Korea’s Beauty of Joseon, which is more popular in the US than in its home country, as evidence that Americans have long been on the hunt for better sunscreen. And though BEMT is a step forward, we still have a way to go. Board-certified dermatologist Jane Yoo has been lobbying Congress to get new sunscreen filters approved for the US and is tracking the FDA’s progress in making those approvals. Currently there are seven other filters—bisoctrizole, drometrizole trisiloxane, octyl triazone, amiloxate, diethylhexyl butamido triazone iscotrizinol, ecamsule, and enzacamene—under the agency’s Time and Extent Application (TEA) pipeline. Those filters are part of the 2014 Sunscreen Innovation Act meant to speed up the process of determining if ingredients are Generally Recognized as Safe and Effective, or GRASE. But here we are 12 years later, and we’re still waiting for their approval.

“A good analogy is we’ve been making sunscreens with both of our hands tied behind our backs,” Palermino says. “We’ve now had one hand released. I would like both hands released.”

#6871. Nobody Wants to Have a Baby Boy Anymore

It began, innocently enough, with a cake. In 2008, American blogger Jenna Karvunidis threw a party to announce the sex of her unborn baby. The reveal came when she cut a slice and found pink inside. She wrote about the experience online, the post went viral, and a new ritual was born.

Nearly 20 years later, we all know the drill. If it isn’t a cake, it’s a balloon or a cannon. Friends and family gather. Someone counts down; something explodes in blue or pink; the parents-to-be hug; a grandparent enters the frame looking thrilled and slightly confused. And, of course, there’s a camera rolling, meaning my daughter could have in theory earned her first “like” long before she drew her first breath.

For the first time in a century, nearly less than half of parents are circumcising their newborns. New dad Kevin Lincoln investigates how America’s favorite elective surgery became as debated as childhood vaccines—and considers his own decision.

The gender reveal has evolved from a modest party trick into a full-blown genre of internet spectacle, complete with its own newborn industrial complex. Private ultrasound companies now sell “gender-reveal experiences,” with rooms that glow pink or blue and teddy bears containing recordings of an unborn baby’s heartbeat. Online, the reveals have become more elaborate: smoke bombs, pyrotechnics (one of which started a wildfire in South California in 2020), choreographed dances, skydivers and, in one particularly memorable case, a Louisiana alligator called Sally biting through a blue jelly watermelon.

But the spectacle has moved on. A new kind of gender-reveal video is becoming a genre of its own, one in which a particular reaction is the main event. A recent compilation racked up 33 million views and three million likes on TikTok. Expecting couples do not scream with delight when the color appears. They scream in horror. They collapse and cry. The reason is nearly always the same: it’s blue. They’re having a boy.

What looks like internet absurdity may actually point to something much bigger. Last year, The Economist published data showing that in mostly higher-income countries, the centuries-old preference for sons is starting to reverse. And in some countries, the data shows that opinion has fully flipped: Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Portugal and the Czech Republic are showing evidence of a preference for girls.

In the US, the same trend appears among adoptive parents. According to the National Bureau of Economic Research, prospective parents were willing to pay an average $16,000 more for a girl than a boy. Among couples having IVF in the US—where, unlike the UK, sex selection is legal— leading clinics have reported that 75–80% of patients want a girl.

This is a big demographic plot twist. On one hand, it’s what progress looks like. For most of human history, boys have been the prize. They inherited the family name and property. They were expected to earn money and carry the family line into the future. Girls, in turn, were generally expected to marry out or care for relatives.

In the worst cases, the preference for sons has had lethal consequences. In China, where a one-child policy enforced by the government lasted 35 years, and in India, sex-selective abortion and the killing or abandonment of female babies has led to generations with far more boys than girls. Misogynist discrimination has been so pervasive that in 1990, the Nobel Prize–winning economist Amartya Sen estimated that more than 100 million women were “missing” from world history. (Researchers have since questioned the precise figure and methodology, but the point still stands.)

But there is another, stranger side to the reversal. You don’t have to spend long reading the 20,000-plus comments beneath a viral gender-reveal video to realize that some of this isn’t simply about girls finally being valued properly. There is a growing and increasingly open distaste for boys—a sense that they are inherently problematic, undesirable by default.

Having a son, in certain corners of modern parenthood, has become unfashionable. A curious society might ask itself why this is—and where it might lead.

Ruth Whippman is the mother of three boys. She grew up in England but now lives in America, in what she describes as a “progressive area of the country [with] very progressive school districts”. As she writes in her 2024 book, BoyMom, friends and neighbors kept saying versions of the same thing: “I hope for your sake this one is a girl.”

“We were a year into the [first] Trump presidency,” she tells me. “There were incels on the news. There was just this huge backlash against toxic masculinity. So it wasn’t even just a sense of, ‘Oh, boys, their clothes aren’t as nice; you don’t get to play Barbies.’ It was this sense that boys are actively toxic and harmful.”

My son and I were calling to each other across a great distance, a distance that I was aware might never close.

When Whippman’s first son was due, she had the standard worries about raising a child of the opposite sex. Would they have enough in common? Would she know how to navigate experiences she hadn’t had? As a lifelong feminist, she tells me, the additional sense of cultural disapproval made everything worse. “It was really horrible, because nobody likes to be pitied,” she says. “Nobody likes to think that their children are second best. But I think it played into something in me as well, if I’m totally honest. I had my own fears and feelings about [having boys], and hearing everybody else’s reaction just made it much worse.”

Nearly a decade on from #MeToo, with Donald Trump back in the White House and male chauvinism metastasizing across the internet, it’s unusual to hear the word “masculinity” without the word “toxic” prefacing it. So parents can end up feeling—even if they’re guilty about it—that having a son is more complicated than it was for previous generations.

“When you hear about boys at the moment, at best, they’re this underachieving lunk who won’t go to college, is doing badly at school and will fail to launch,” says Whippman. “And at worst, he’s deep in the manosphere, commanding his AI girlfriend to let him choke her, or whatever. It gets to this wider consumerisation of parenthood. [The idea that] girls are better consumer products, in a way—which is a terrible, terrible way to talk about human beings.”

I’d be lying if I said it hadn’t crossed my mind, when I discovered I was having a daughter, that if I could raise her to be a confident, secure, kick-ass girl, I’d win the approval of the types of circle I move in. “Girl dads” who let daughters paint their nails are the subject of endless kind TikTok videos.

Parents of boys, not so much. Whippman, who has heard more and more fathers say they would prefer daughters, recognizes the problem. “Parenting a son is almost seen as a harm reduction project, rather than anything empowering,” she says. “That’s really at odds with our modern parenting culture, which is about encouraging [our kids] to reach for the stars and be happy and access all the world has to offer.”

And like everything else in 2026, the random, happy event of your unborn child’s sex can somehow thrust you into the us-versus-them culture war. “In the States and in the UK, there’s a sense that if you are on the progressive left, you are on the side of girls and women… any time you’re defending boys and men, it has this sniff of something right-wing, or being a bad actor,” says Whippman. “That’s a really hard thing to hold as a progressive, feminist parent of a son.”

There are other theories for why parents in Western countries, in particular, are increasingly downbeat about having boys. Millennials are having fewer children than any generation in history, and we’re doing it later in life. In other words, by the time it happens, we’re already exhausted. And boys are often perceived as more work, especially when they’re little: more physical, and harder to keep occupied and under control.

Lara Miller has been a midwife for more than 20 years. She uses social media to demystify pregnancy and childbirth. She thinks there is some truth to the idea that older parents may be wary of boys. “When you see older people having babies, they tend to be more controlling about everything to do with it,” she says. “I say this without judgment—I was an older mum myself—but you become used to doing things your own way and to your own routines.”

Of course, it would be silly to assume every girl will be content quietly coloring in while every boy spends his childhood trailing dog shit through the house. But according to Alex Trippier, a kids’ club leader who runs the social media account Be A Happier Parent, there can be real differences in behavior. “You get testosterone surges around age two, which I really see with the kids that I look after in my groups,” he says. “All of a sudden the boys come in and they’re going crazy. Parents who are on the quieter side can be overwhelmed by the sheer energy. Some people insist this is all nonsense, and [boys being more boisterous] is completely socialized. I think it’s a mixture of both. You’ve got that developmental layer going on, and you’ve got all the socialization on top of it.”

Trippier, who has two boys and a girl, thinks culture has shifted. Parents have always worried about teenage girls. But now, they have a whole new list of concerns for boys: porn, gaming addiction, exam results, algorithms. “There are all these fears we have for our sons, this kind of moral anxiety about the impossibility of raising good men,” he says.

In 2024, Zach Baron documented his long journey to fatherhood via IVF in GQ. Now, he reports on the mind-altering chaos (and occasional brilliance) of life with two walking, talking young boys.

Miller, who had similar feelings when she was pregnant but is now the happy mother of two sons, thinks there’s another factor: Schools aren’t always very good at accommodating boys. “We have changed as a society and we’re not as accepting of boys,” she says. “Boys don’t tend to fit the school model. You get worse reports. [Our school] gives the boys set days to be allowed to kick a football. Well, you don’t remember that at school, do you?”

She recalls being summoned by a headmaster after her son made a gun sign with his fingers. “The girls do better at school,” she says. “They’ve read 20 books; my boys have only read two. The teacher’s gone, ‘Well, if you buy a football magazine, do you think they’d read that?’ You’re like, ‘Oh, fuck off. Just fuck off.’”

There’s something else unusual about today’s parents, beyond grappling with the manosphere and Parkrun injuries. We’re the most psychologically literate generation to have ever lived. If we haven’t done therapy ourselves, we’re at least sufficiently fluent in the language of therapy to take a stab at self-reflection (or, as Boomer parents might call it, self-absorption). We know about childhood trauma. We know about attachment styles. We know that our parents’ behavior has consequences. We know that our childhoods don’t simply disappear when we become adults. It gives us an enormous number of things to worry about.

One of the most interesting conversations I’ve had on this subject is with a colleague who is expecting a son. He’s delighted now, but initially, the prospect of having a boy unnerved him—as it did me, before I knew the sex of my baby. We both agreed this probably came from remembering difficult experiences with our own fathers, and being afraid of repeating them. For me, it may have gone a step further. The idea of having a son scared me because I had a pretty broken relationship with myself.

Dr. Chrissy Jayarajah is a psychiatrist and the author of the world’s only academic paper on “gender disappointment.” She’s quick to point out that the term is not a clinical diagnosis, but what you might call a “known phenomenon” (particularly on TikTok, where thousands of videos are tagged with it).

“Something I always say is that becoming a parent will undoubtedly make you reflect on the way that you were parented,” she says. “Whether it was a good relationship, a bad relationship, an indifferent relationship or a non-existent relationship—they all play a part. An absent father is still very present in your mind. We are more psychologically minded and reflective compared with, maybe, the generation before. But I also think we don’t know what to do with that reflection.”

This can manifest in different ways, explains Jayarajah. “Working predominantly with mothers, I would say they fall into two camps,” she says. “They either want to emulate exactly how their mother was, from recreating those childhood memories, making those family favorite meals on a Sunday, and so on. Or they’re: ‘Oh my God, I’m turning into my mother.’”

That can produce powerful feelings about your baby’s sex. “We see that with women who have experienced abuse of some kind,” says Jayarajah. “Some absolutely do not want to have a girl, because they don’t want their daughter to experience what they went through. On the other hand, I have had women who say, ‘No, I definitely want a girl. I don’t want to have a son,’ because a son [would remind] them of the perpetrator.”

The sex of your baby, in other words, can become a vessel for all sorts of fears that existed long before the baby did. Then, there’s the demographic problem. There just aren’t enough babies. A rapid collapse in birth rates and fertility is playing out across the world. In 1990, global fertility was about 3.3 children per woman. Today it is around 2.2 to 2.3. More than half the countries in the world are already below “replacement level.”

The consequences will be huge. Labor shortages. Pressure on pensions. Rising social and healthcare costs. Less of the innovation and forward momentum traditionally associated with younger populations. In that context, favoring one sex over the other seems beyond counterproductive.

Maybe we are retreating into old, narrow assumptions. “We’ve never thought about gender more as a society than we do at this present day,” says Jayarajah. “Ideas about gender are in our face all day long. There was no such thing as a gender reveal party 20 years ago, let alone putting it online. What’s happening, in my opinion, is gender essentialism. This sexist idea of what it means to be a man, what it means to be a woman, is playing out in what we expect for our children.”

The following week, I saw this in action. I took my 19-month-old daughter to a soft-play morning, where she likes to roll around on the multicolored shapes and go down the slide on my lap. A few minutes after we arrived, I heard a scream. “Aaaargh!”

At one end of the ball pit, a little boy was staring at his own reflection in a mirror. “Aaaargh!” he went again, hammering his fists against the perspex and making the entire frame shake. A few feet away, my daughter watched him quietly. I made a mental note to keep them as far apart as possible.

The boy’s parents were nearby, deep in conversation. They seemed oblivious, or perhaps simply immune, to their son’s King Kong routine. A dozen judgmental thoughts zipped through my head. Why aren’t you keeping a closer eye on him? What kind of irresponsible shit are you pulling at home for him to do this? If he harms her, I swear to God…

I took a sip of my coffee, scrolled through my phone, then turned around, ready to rescue my sweet, innocent little girl. The boy who had been screaming a moment ago was now shyly handing her a dumper truck with a missing wheel. She closed her eyes, grinned, and took it with both hands.

#6848. Peptides Are About to Flood the Market. And Bro Science Is Coming for Us All

This past February, Health and Human Services secretary and MAHA Final Boss Robert F. Kennedy Jr. made it known during an interview with Joe Rogan that he intended to direct the FDA to lessen restrictions on a handful of peptides that had previously been banned under the Biden administration. The peptides in question, which included influencer favorites BPC-157 and TB-500 (collectively dubbed the “Wolverine stack” for their reported regenerative properties) had been placed on the FDA’s “do not compound” list, effectively barring pharmacies from providing them to patients, for reasons including serious safety concerns and a general lack of comprehensive human trials. No new research had emerged to prompt RFK Jr.’s announcement. Rather, the HHS head, a self-proclaimed “big fan” of peptides, believed the drugs should never have been banned in the first place—a decision that, in his view, was responsible for the subsequent explosion of a thriving black market for peptides online. So, for the past eight months, healthcare professionals have been waiting with baited breath for the other shoe to drop.

And then this past Wednesday hit with a thud.

In a seemingly unilateral decision—one that flies in the face of FDA scientists who warned earlier this year of the potential, largely unknown implications of effectively releasing scantily researched injectables into the wild—RFK announced that patients (or anyone with a prescription) would now have access to six of these previously outlawed peptides through a loophole-like policy called “enforcement discretion.”

Internet-famous peptides like BPC-157 and TB-500 might be the future, but right now they’re a shot in the dark.

To explain exactly what that means, I called up Edgar J. Asebey, a life sciences regulatory and transactional attorney at the law firm Frier Levitt. “I always tell people I speak three languages: English, Spanish, and FDA,” he says. “Enforcement discretion means that the FDA will not enforce a certain regulation. And in this case, it's the prohibition against compounding these six peptides. That's what it boils down to.”

Enforcement discretion is typically considered a break-in-case-of-emergency avenue for freeing up availability of certain drugs when warranted, Asebey says. “For example, when the GLP-1s were in shortage, pharmacies could lawfully compound exact copies of Ozempic and Wegovy.” In other words, the six peptides in question—BPC-157, TB-500, MOTS-c, Semax, KPV, and Epitalon—are still categorically unapproved by the FDA for human use, but Kennedy is going to allow pharmacies to dispense them as if they were, until further notice.

While this may seem outrageous, not everybody is outraged. For one, obviously, peptides are big business. (Market research firm Grand View Research valued the global peptide industry at over 140 billion in 2025, with North America accounting for more than 60% of that revenue.) Aside from that, however, one of Kennedy’s stated motivations is to reduce people’s incentive to procure peptides from regulation-skirting med spas and the dark corners of the internet, where substances like BPC-157 can be ordered to your door with the same ease as buying paper towels on Amazon. Since July, the Alliance for Safe Online Pharmacies (ASOP Global) has tracked nearly 21,000 peptide listings across more than 300 websites, including upwards of 8,800 listings for BPC-157 alone. As you might imagine, quality and safety present serious concerns to anyone choosing to go that route—that is, beyond the potential risks of using these peptides even in their purest forms—hence the burgeoning cottage industry of peptide purity trackers and testing services.

“My view is simple,” says Meena Verma, MD, MPH, a board-certified internist and nephrologist, and founder of Blue Coast Longevity in Newport Beach, CA. “People are already using these peptides, and there is an urgent need for harm reduction. Allowing licensed pharmacies to compound them under physician guidance means patients can now have an accountable channel with assurance of safety.”

But how this policy change plays out may not be quite that simple. Whereas purveyors of these six peptides—whether operating online or in person—were previously identifiable as unlawful simply by virtue of offering the unapproved drugs, Kennedy's mandate to usher these peptides into the broader health marketplace muddies the waters, making it harder for consumers to tell which providers are legit. "The online market is already flush with illegal actors who now can more easily hide amongst the masses with limited risk of getting caught,” reads a statement that ASOP Global released within hours of Kennedy’s announcement on Wednesday. “Without enhanced and consistent new enforcement of the market, today’s policy will allow criminals to hide amongst the newly compliant.”

Kennedy’s plan notes that compounding pharmacies will be subject to specific safety criteria, like requiring that the peptides are manufactured in an FDA-registered facility, but as The New York Times has pointed out, “the step basically only requires a manufacturer to notify the agency of its address.”

When you consider the scale of the peptide market alongside the reality of what the FDA is actually capable of policing with its post-DOGE workforce—following the laying off of 3,500 workers—it begs the question of whether the criteria laid out in Kennedy’s plan will even matter at all.

"For one, if you're going to have guardrails, you're going to need to enforce them,” says Leigh Verbois, PhD, ASOP's global board chair and the former director of the FDA’s Office of Drug Security, Integrity, and Response. “There needs to be resources to actually take action, full stop. And two, the organizations that are intended to utilize those guardrails may not even look at them. There are too many [compounding pharmacies] to be able to enforce against."

But I’d argue there’s something far more dangerous, and more consequential, than the possibility that this latest decision might inadvertently feed the back-alley peptide market rather than curtail it. After all, not everyone is out there looking for peptides. (I know I’m certainly not.) But we all need to go to the doctor at some point—something that could now conceivably land you on the receiving end of a prescription for a drug that has never even been researched in a human being.

“To classify this as safe would just be irresponsible,” says Motisola Lewis, MD, an Internal Medicine physician and the founder of Lewis Medical NY. “If you have a cancer patient—say, a woman who just had breast cancer—and she wants to fix her knee that just got injured, and she ends up getting an injection of BPC-157. This is known to promote tissue growth. This is known to promote vascularization. We don't know what effect it has on a patient who has had cancer. Does it promote cancer growth? We don't know.”

Peptides are blurring the line between supplements and prescription drugs.

The inescapable irony here is that not long ago it was the MAHA movement that was encouraging people to be skeptical of mainstream medical providers, to do your own research before just allowing your doctor to jab you with a COVID-19 vaccine. And now, by injecting these six potentially harmful peptides into mainstream medicine, where they will be readily available to physicians with varying degrees of familiarity with the substances themselves and the politics surrounding them, the administration has gone ahead and given the rest of us a very real reason to do our own research. Sure, this only applies to peptides at the moment, but a precedent has been set. And you can no longer afford not to pay attention. None of us can.

“When patients walk into their physician's office, or talk to a telehealth provider, they have an assumption that there will be a positive outcome—that the patient is seeking and that the physician is intending to meet,” says Dr. Verbois. “But without any of the quality, safety, or efficacy, it's completely the wild, wild west. If anyone in my family, in need of a drug for treatment, were to ask me if they should go down a path of utilizing a peptide of this sort, my response to them would be, ‘Never. Instead, seek out a treatment for that disease that's been shown to be safe and effective. These have not been, and you are putting yourself in harm's way.’”

#6834. Reckoning With Borat, 20 Years Later

Last month, I sat in a packed-to-the-gills movie theater to experience the latest release from a controversial comedian whose work regularly blurs the line between docu-reality and narrative fiction. I am, of course, referring to Ali G: Who Iz I?—the cinematic return of Sacha Baron Cohen’s once-and-still-somewhat-beloved durag-doffing titular character.

Before the film was unceremoniously dumped on Prime Video last week, I managed to snag a ticket to the only New York City screening that wasn’t totally sold-out, booking a seat—last row, tucked in an alcove of the theater—with the intention of isolated note-taking and (gasp!) the occasional phone-glance should the movie prove tedious. The recliners next to me were eventually claimed by a gentleman decked in a very Ali G-esque skull cap and wraparounds, who unleashed a deafening “BOOYAKASHA!” after Nicole Kidman’s AMC intro.

Cohen’s brand of confrontational cringe comedy continues to attract a level of interest, thanks in part to a certain mustached-and-mankini’d Kazakh journalist. The cultural explosion that was Borat! Cultural Learnings of America for Make Benefit Glorious Nation of Kazakhstan (has any movie title so padded the word count?) turns 20 years old this week, and it remains a curious artifact of the 2000s: impossibly dated and slightly better than you might remember, even as its creator’s talent has slowly and unmistakably eroded.

If you were just old enough to purchase a ticket to an R-rated movie in 2006, Borat felt like a truly out-of-nowhere phenomenon in terms of mass impact. But the character, armed with little more than a shabby suit and an aggressive cheer, emerged from Cohen’s cult favorite Da Ali G Show, which ran for one season on BBC’s Channel 4 in 2000 before making the leap to HBO in 2003. Alongside Ali G and the gay Austrian fashion correspondent Brüno, Borat Sagdiyev was just one of the many personalities Cohen regularly deployed in the thorny thickets of gotcha-mentary, trading in culturally oblivious and antisemitic observations supposedly inherited from his Kazakh homeland, often to the shock and dismay of his unsuspecting subjects.

These fool-me-once hijinks weren’t necessarily groundbreaking, even for HBO’s then-provocative late-night programming. Several years prior, The Chris Rock Show engaged in similar man-on-the-street shenanigans (once, he polled residents of NYC’s notoriously hate-crime-prone Howard Beach region about renaming a street after Tupac Shakur). But segments in which Ali G addressed Buzz Aldrin as Buzz Lightyear, or the one where Borat led a bar full of Arizonans in a rousing chorus of “Throw the Jew Down the Well,” were practically reverse-engineered for virality in the internet’s pre-social-media infancy, and the appeal of Cohen’s approach—how far will he go, and will ordinary folk follow him?—was easy to understand, regardless of the visceral discomfort it may have generated.

Straying from the sketch-based format of Da Ali G Show, Borat embraced a loose narrative structure, with its titular inquirer setting out for America to make a documentary on the country’s social peccadilloes with the intent of eventually romancing Pamela Anderson. If you want to get highfalutin about it, the plot itself situates Borat within the legacy of films about Europeans attempting to understand America—a small-but-fascinating subgenre that includes Paul Verhoeven’s fairytale-cum-nightmare Showgirls, the post-hippie pessimism of Michaelangelo Antonioni’s Zabriskie Point, and Kristoffer Borgli’s social-mores rom-dramedy The Drama.

Don’t get it twisted, though: Borat was and still is best enjoyed as a showcase of physical comedy and inflammatory non sequiturs. It’s no less funny in 2026 to watch a grown man wash his face in a toilet bowl, or gesture toward a tortoise while sincerely asking, “What type of dog is this?” The scene in which an etiquette coach attempts to guide Borat through the intricacies of comic timing is itself an excellent study in comic timing; the notorious full-frontal hotel chase sequence between Borat and his producer Azamat (portrayed by steadily-working character actor Ken Davitian) is a marvel for its sheer escalation alone. This stuff killed in a massive Northern New Jersey movie theater when I was 19, and it still kills from the confines of my couch 20 years on.

Unlike much of Cohen’s subsequent work, Borat is not an explicitly political film. Nonetheless, a good deal of its “GREAT SUCCESS!”—$262 million in box receipts off an $18 million budget, with a Best Actor Golden Globe for Cohen himself—is undoubtedly owed to a different sort of comic timing. The film came out in the throes of George W. Bush’s second presidential term, with the US mired in two wars abroad and a hell of its own making. Americans were thirsty for easy targets to shoulder the blame and, if possible, provide some cheap laughs in the process.

Their thirst was quenched in the form of Borat’s many unsuspecting subjects. Some of this was low-hanging fruit: Borat telling a group of feminists to smile more, or acting like a literal bull in a china shop amidst Confederate memorabilia. But the film’s more sensational material depicted rough corners of the American psyche long hidden in plain sight. Most memorably, Borat encounters a group of rowdy frat boys who huff about white pride and express dismay at the abolishment of slavery before engaging in a viewing of Anderson’s infamous leaked sex tape with ex-husband Tommy Lee.

Not everyone was displeased at their portrayal, though. Joe and Miriam Behar, the Jewish couple who offered Borat and Azamat room and board, were good-natured in a 2006 interview with the Boston Globe. For her part, Miriam shrugged off Borat’s naked antisemitism. “To me it's an anti-Muslim movie, not anti-Jewish, because Kazakhstan is mostly Muslim,” she offered.

Behar’s observation underlines the casual bigotry that otherwise stains Borat. The film’s portrayal of Kazakh culture is undoubtedly offensive, and provoked mixed reactions from the country’s government for years to come. For what it’s worth, Cohen pushes back—intentionally or not—on anti-Muslim sentiments during the film’s rodeo sequence, in which Borat tells a particularly racist attendee that he is indeed “not Muslim.”

But given the post-9/11 climate into which Borat was launched, the implications of a foreign correspondent from a majority-Muslim country espousing antisemitic and misogynistic viewpoints are crystal-clear, and Cohen himself seems too smart to sincerely profess a lack of awareness as to how this might be received. It doesn’t help that his subsequent projects resulted in exponential escalations of offense: 2012’s more narratively-focused The Dictator spared no quarter when it came to stereotypes of Arab culture, while the astoundingly homophobic Brüno, from 2009, was loaded with gags about anal sex and resulted in an out-of-court settlement with a Palestinian grocer who was falsely credited as a former terrorist in the film itself.

After 2016’s box-office bomb Grimsby, which is most notable for the allegations of on-set sexual harassment co-star Rebel Wilson later brought toward Cohen, his work went in a more explicitly political direction. There was his 2018 docu-satire series Who Is America?—two episodes of which featured writing and directing contributions from Nathan Fielder—and 2020’s Borat Subsequent Moviefilm, a second-verse-same-as-the-first sequel which earned several Oscar nominations and brought a truly bizarre “October surprise” to that year’s presidential election involving actress Maria Bakalova, Rudy Giuliani, and the former New York City Mayor’s pants zipper.

#6814. Why El Clasico Is the Best Grudge Match in All of Sports

Last Saturday, before the biggest grudge match in international soccer, Spanish police officers decked out in riot gear surrounded my Barcelona hotel. Blue lights bounced from the sun-stained stone of the alley as kids scrambled away in the distance. In the skirmish, one had lost a single ASICS sneaker that sat idly in the road until, sometime between night and the light of day, someone snatched it up for themselves. You often hear of the way heated football rivalries and cup finals bring out a kind of mob mentality among fans. The match, however, wasn’t set to kick off for another 24 hours.

Three days prior I’d landed in Barcelona to attend FC Barcelona versus Real Madrid, better known as El Clásico, for which Tyler the Creator’s GOLF brand had partnered with Spotify on Barcelona’s gameday kits. If you care about football in the slightest you know about El Clásico, though it’s worth knowing about even if you don’t.

Since 1902—the year of Real Madrid’s founding, and three years after that of FC Barcelona—the two biggest football clubs in Spain have clashed in a rivalry match dubbed El Clásico (the face-off now happens twice a season, plus various tournament play that varies in schedule year to year). Before last week’s installment, the match had been played 264 times over 122 years. In most high-stakes football rivalries, one club holds a historical edge: Arsenal has a handy 20 to 30 wins over Tottenham Hotspur in the North London Derby, depending on when you start keeping track, while Manchester United edges out Liverpool in total wins. But heading into the first El Clásico of the 2026-27 La Liga season, Barcelona and Real Madrid stood deadlocked with 106 wins each.

American sports rivalries are often the result of proximity, with most pitting divisional neighbors and crosstown rivals against one another, with the exception of teams that share a bitter history of championship bouts (like the Lakers and the Celtics). But if we’re being honest, not even Yankees-Red Sox holds a candle to El Clásico. Apart from both competing in the upper echelon of La Liga’s clubs, FC Barcelona and Real Madrid’s rivalry has stewed in over a century of political turmoil between the Catalan capital and the city that houses the Spanish throne. During the 20th century as Spain looked to enforce a uniform identity across the nation, Catalonia sought to maintain its independence from the crown and, crucially, its right to speak Catalan rather than Spanish. Francisco Franco’s dictatorship brought more enmity, as Madrid became the proxy club for a centralized Spain while Barcelona came to represent Catalonia’s Republican Left. During the Franco years, when both the language and the flag of Catalan were banned, Barcelona’s Camp Nou stadium became a safe haven for the free expression of Catalan identity. Meanwhile, Franco’s foreign minister Fernando Maria de Castiella declared Real Madrid “the best embassy we’ve ever had.” The wounds of that era have hardly subsided: while the war may be over, as recently as 2017 the Catalan people voted on whether or not they’d remain a part of Spain (the vote was declared illegal by the Spanish government, and there was police intervention at polling stations).

Today, the clubs are recognized as polar opposites in every sense, from politics to style of play: Real Madrid stands for conservatism and a unified Spain, while Barcelona remains the club of the rebellious Left. Madrid takes an Evil Empire approach to roster construction, buying up the most exciting free agents on the market for record-breaking fees. (This century alone they’ve rostered Cristiano Ronaldo, David Beckham, and Zinedine Zidane, and the club currently boasts a striker line that includes Jude Bellingham, Kylian Mbappé, and Vinicius Jr.) Barcelona, on the other hand, has the most acclaimed and successful youth academy in all of football. The school, La Masia, has promoted a singular theory of football for decades, prioritizing position play and a dominant possession game perfected by FC Barcelona veterans like Lionel Messi, Xavi, Pep Guardiola, and Andrés Iniesta. When Spain won the World Cup this summer, they did so with eight former students of La Masia on the pitch (one of whom, Marc Cucarella, currently plays for Real Madrid).

To this day, the rivalry is characterized by an unmistakable hostility. In my four days in Barcelona, I didn’t see one person wearing Real Madrid’s crest, though the souvenir shops lining La Rambla were well-stocked with the club’s bootleg gear. At one point, I walked past a wall scrawled with graffiti: “SPANISH GO HOME.” When we finally got to the match, most Madrid fans were sequestered to a supporter section in the upper deck surrounded by protective plexiglass.

“This is the biggest match in club football,” explained Joel Jaggar, a British transplant who’s lived in Barcelona for a decade. Jaggar runs Real Vintage Football, a rare jersey boutique in El Raval (just outside of the city’s Gothic Quarter) that was unsurprisingly overwhelmed with visitors all weekend. He’d stocked up on at least a hundred Messi jerseys, all of which were gone by the time they closed on Friday. “A lot of people come to Barcelona specifically for this game,” he shared. “Big fans, sure, but also people who want to experience the game for the sake of the experience.” Globally, he argued, the word “Barcelona” is as closely associated with the club as it is the city. “You can’t say the same for Madrid,” he quipped.

Jaggar and I met the morning after a swell of student-organized protests regarding the city’s cost-of-living crisis boiled over into vandalism and looting near the neighborhood that houses his store. There was even suspicion El Clásico would have to be postponed or cancelled altogether. Come morning though, those anxieties had faded–as is so often the case, football continued uninterrupted.

Even with capacity capped at around 62,000 during ongoing renovations, Camp Nou is something to behold. Ahead of kickoff, supporters lifted color-coordinated pieces of poster nestled into each seat,forming a massive, stadium-wide tifo in the colors of the club and, in our section, the Catalonian flag. Fireworks exploded, and El Clásico began.

But much of the match’s first half was mired in tension, as Barcelona dominated possession but failed to score. The crowd finally began to pop when Madrid nabbed their first goal, then properly exploded when Barca tied it up in the second half. When Mbappé scored his second goal of the evening at ‘87 minutes after a beautiful assist from Bellingham, he vaulted over to the gate of the Barcelona supporters’ section, taunting them as trash thrown from the crowd rained over him. Madrid held on, gaining the tiniest of advantages in the El Clásico win count: 107 to their rivals’ 106.

As we walked out of Camp Nou, which will hold a whopping 105,000 spectators when its renovation is complete, I thought about the sort of sporting events that tend to grace bucket lists: Super Bowls and World Cup Finals and World Series games. So often the point is the spectacle of a season or tournament ending—the big trophy, the championship ring, the confetti and fireworks. El Clásico is different. There’s no trophy at the end, no ultimate resolution. Like the ideological divide for which it’s become a proxy, El Clásico is in a constant state of ebb and flow. Each time Barcelona and Real Madrid take the pitch, there’s no decisive victory so much as another brick laid in the foundation of football’s greatest rivalry. These clubs have come to blows 265 times. But as long as there are football stadiums in Barcelona and Madrid, El Clásico will endure as a conduit for the enduring rancor between the country's two biggest cities.

#6796. What Writing a Book About JFK Jr. Taught Me About Personal Style

John F. Kennedy Jr. wasn’t the first guy to ride a bike wearing a suit. Likewise, the presidential scion and People’s 1988 Sexiest Man Alive didn’t invent the backward Kangol cap, the pinstriped suit, or wearing a waistcoat with jeans—he just did it better than pretty much anybody else. That’s a big part of why he was one of the most photographed men on the planet in the 1990s, and why he remains a menswear moodboard staple today, nearly 30 years after his untimely death. Still, no matter how hard you try, and no matter how closely you hew to the source material, all attempts to replicate JFK Jr.’s style are destined to leave you looking like a runner-up in a Washington Square Park lookalike contest.

Ask anyone who encountered John-John in his prime—which, in my experience, is virtually everyone who lived in Manhattan in the 1990s—and the first thing they’ll mention is his magnetism, his charm, and the fact that, against all odds, he was actually better-looking in person. I know this because, having recently written a book about JFK Jr.’s style, I’ve spent a good part of the last year poring over photos, reading biographies, speaking to people who knew him, and otherwise immersing myself in his world. Among the conclusions I’ve come to is that you probably won’t look as cool as JFK Jr. did wearing rollerblades, but there are a few style moves you can borrow from his playbook without resorting to full Kennedy cosplay.

JFK Jr.’s wardrobe was built on four pillars: tailoring, preppy basics, workwear, and sportswear. But it was the idiosyncratic way he combined them (i.e., a backward baseball cap with a suit, a polo neck sweater under a tweed blazer, hiking boots with tailored trousers) that resulted in some of his most memorable looks. Surprisingly, while the combination of straight-leg jeans, a button-up dress shirt, and a blazer has become shorthand for JFK Jr.’s style, I’ve never seen any evidence of him wearing it. In fact, he seldom wore jeans at all (some speculate his mother thought they looked shabby, while others told me that jeans didn’t complement his active lifestyle). That said, plenty of other ’90s style icons, from Ralph Lauren to Jerry Seinfeld, rocked this particular look, and it’s a pretty easy one to pull off. As with everything about JFK Jr.’s style, it’s not about lifting these combinations wholesale, but understanding the precise balance of contrast that makes them work.

One of the most consistent elements of JFK Jr.’s style was his love of discordant accessories, from his collection of flat caps to his ever-present wallet chain to his Oakley Eye Jacket shades. Some of these were practical—he was so prone to misplacing his wallet and keys that he needed to chain them to his pants—but others reflected his love of color (funky knit caps), his enthusiasm for skiing (fleece headbands), and his passion for travel (various hats picked up on trips to India and Thailand). Some accessories will suit you better than others, but if you have a personal connection to it, all the better. And most importantly, limit yourself to one standout add-on per outfit.

Speaking of accessories: JFK Jr. was a dedicated scarf guy, and he’d pair one with everything from joggers and sneakers to his best navy blue suit. Rather than draping a scarf loosely around his neck, he tended to tie it in either a dressy overhand or classic Parisian knot, both of which lend a more formal look. He also loved wearing a thick scarf as outerwear, throwing it over a sweatshirt on a chilly day instead of a coat, often paired with a knit cap. For best results, don’t pick a scarf that matches the rest of your outfit too closely.

Among the most surprising things I discovered about JFK Jr.’s wardrobe was his watch collection. I wasn’t able to uncover any evidence of him wearing the long-lost Rolex Daytona given to him by Aristotle Onassis (a watch that, if ever found, would give Paul Newman a run for his money), but I did identify an unusually wide range of other pieces in his collection, from a Victorinox Swiss Army field watch to a Swatch chronograph to a vintage Rolex Oyster Perpetual. This is of a piece with everything else we know about JFK Jr.: he wasn’t bound by any single style, brand, or price point, and got just as much enjoyment out of a cheap quartz watch as a luxury mechanical one.

As a rich kid raised on the Upper East Side, JFK Jr.’s closet included plenty of blazers, chinos, and polos. He was a gym rat who loved playing sports, so he also had a lot of sweats, tees, and sneakers. He was a downtown socialite, so he owned a lot of nice suits and tuxes, too. He also had an enviable collection of graphic tees from NYC institutions like the Russian & Turkish Baths in the East Village and the New York Athletic Club, as well as some commemorating events like the 1984 NYC Fresh Festival, a seminal moment in hip-hop history. What stands out to me—and what I believe makes his style so distinctive—is that JFK Jr.’s wardrobe was an authentic reflection of who he was, where he came from, and what he loved. This might be the most important style lesson any of us can learn from him: Great personal style is less about clothes than wearing them in a way that expresses your individuality.
