#6889. Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

The findings echo a recent report about management software and AI e-mail assistants. Research from Fortra and Barracuda showed how criminals are using legitimate administrative tools and hidden AI instructions to manipulate users and compromise systems.

#6886. Study: Sign-In Problems Are Primary Drivers of Help Desk Tickets

Sign-in problems account for up to 53% of help desk tickets at individual enterprises, but network issues can take significantly longer to resolve, according to a new study from ControlUp.

Analyzing 1,069,110 help desk tickets, the IT operations company identified password resets, account lockouts, and multifactor authentication (MFA) issues as major drivers of support demand.

At one large enterprise, account-related tickets took a median of 11 minutes to resolve, compared with 50 hours for network problems. Meanwhile, 87% of network and phone tickets were reassigned between teams, compared with 20% of account tickets.

MFA enrollment and re-enrollment problems appeared at 40% of organizations, with one in five linking these issues to employees replacing or upgrading their phones. Virtual desktop problems appeared at 55% of organizations analyzed.

Traditional phone support remained a major channel for IT assistance.

Among organizations that recorded how support requests were submitted, 57% reported that phone calls accounted for approximately two-thirds or more of contacts. At one organization, that figure reached 99%.

Machine-generated tickets from monitoring, security, backup, and patching systems appeared at half of the organizations studied. At 15%, automated tickets represented between 25% and 50% of all requests.

The research covered enterprises across retail, healthcare, financial services, manufacturing, construction, energy, and IT services.

ControlUp said the findings highlight the need to evaluate recurring problems and their operational impact rather than relying solely on ticket volume and resolution speed.

"IT organizations have traditionally measured support performance by how quickly they close tickets, but that doesn't tell the whole story," said Simon Townsend, senior vice president of marketing and office of the CTO at ControlUp.

"A password reset that takes a few minutes and an infrastructure failure that disrupts an entire location shouldn't carry the same operational weight."

#6885. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

#6881. Phishing Threats Totaled 7.3 Billion in Q3 2026

E-mail phishing threats reached 7.3 billion in Q3 2026, with September activity increasing 41% from August, according to Microsoft Threat Intelligence.

According to a report from Microsoft Threat Intelligence and the Microsoft Defender Security Research Team, phishing detections reached nearly 3 billion in September alone.

The findings also highlighted changes in attacker techniques, including business-service impersonation, credential theft, and social engineering through Microsoft Teams.

The Redmond-based firm recognized approximately 129 million malicious messages spoofing the sender domains of tracked business services during Q3.

DocuSign was the most frequently spoofed brand in September, accounting for 29% of the month's tracked spoofing activity. Adobe, SurveyMonkey, and Barracuda also experienced increases toward the end of the quarter.

The research found that some malicious messages were delivered despite failing e-mail authentication checks and being identified as threats.

In September, tenant- or user-level security overrides applied to 63% of these messages with a recorded security outcome — most involved tenant-level exceptions.

Microsoft warned that overly broad exceptions intended to support legitimate business communications can weaken protection against impersonation attacks.

The company emphasized that these findings do not establish whether recipients opened the messages or whether accounts were compromised.

Microsoft also recorded approximately 141,000 malicious Teams call attempts during Q3.

Attackers increasingly used ordinary personal names instead of explicitly impersonating IT support personnel. Such display names accounted for 46% of an observed sample in July, rising to 88% in September.

Meanwhile, QR code phishing declined 67% compared to Q2, totaling roughly 12.1 million attacks.

CAPTCHA-gated phishing activity increased toward the end of the quarter, reaching 3.1 million detected attacks in September. SVG attachments played a significant role in the increase.

Credential theft remained the primary objective of malicious attachment campaigns, with approximately 94% to 96% of payload-based attacks directing users to phishing sites or fraudulent sign-in pages.

Microsoft recommended reviewing Exchange Online Protection and Defender for Office 365 configurations, enabling Safe Links and Safe Attachments, and strengthening authentication protections.

The company also advised organizations to review security exceptions and verify the identities of external Teams contacts before responding to suspicious requests.

Nicola Brognano, the creative director of 7 for All Mankind, gave me the ultimate Y2K style treatment

On a recent Tuesday afternoon, I stopped by 7 for All Mankind’s SoHo store to meet Nicola Brognano, the brand’s newest creative director, for a one-on-one styling session. I felt a bit nervous, knowing I’d need to step outside of my sartorial safe space. For reference, my personal style tends to lean more minimal, a “classics with a twist” vibe. When I entered the store, I was wearing a striped button-down shirt, black jeans, and a trench coat—my typical uniform. Immediately, Brognano, wearing a leather jacket layered over a hoodie, was warm and kind. I felt my anxiety lessen, knowing I was going to be in good hands.

Brognano’s aesthetic couldn’t be further from my own. At Blumarine, where he served as creative director from 2020 to 2023, he transformed the brand away from bohemian, romantic whimsy and injected it with a jolt of Y2K energy (butterfly crop tops, slinkier dresses, and low-rise pants). In 2025, the designer took the creative helm of 7 for All Mankind and staged the brand’s inaugural runway show in February during New York Fashion Week. The appointment was a fitting choice for the American-born brand, which, when it launched in the 2000s, had every It girl wearing its low-rise jeans, from Britney Spears and Christina Aguilera to Nicole Richie and Paris Hilton. It was a time when Gossip Girl was the hottest show and Mary-Kate and Ashley Olsen were regularly spotted around the downtown New York scene with skinny scarves wrapped around their necks.

Brognano brought an exciting new energy to 7 for All Mankind that built upon the label’s roots. His collection had a nostalgic Y2K-era undertone, but polished up for 2026. Models walked the runway in shrunken black peacoats with exaggerated shoulders, skinny scarves, cropped leather jackets, and sheer, silky blouses.

Ultra-skinny jeans, wax-coated maxi skirts, and babydoll dresses were key hero pieces. New York’s coolest It girl, Chloë Sevigny, stars in the fall campaign; images from the shoot appear on digital screens throughout the store.

When he joined the brand, Brognano tells me, “I really wanted to bring that sexiness back.” In designing the collection, “I imagined a super cool, strong woman with an eccentric attitude,” he says. Could it be me?

After we met and assessed my sizing, Brognano went straight to pulling looks for me. The first look I tried on was a semisheer blouse with an exaggerated pussy bow at the neck, ultra-low-rise dual-panel waxed skinny jeans (a leather-like finish on the front and rigid denim on the back), and, to top it off, a shrunken black peacoat. For the finishing touch, I slid on a pair of sky-high platform heels. I felt like I just stepped out of the early aughts and was ready to head to the club (in a good way).

I rarely wear heels. (I’m more of a flats-and-loafers kind of girl.) I don’t think I’ve ever worn platform heels in my life. While walking in them posed its own challenge, I did feel an overwhelming sense of confidence and sexiness. “The most important thing for the collection, for me, was the attitude,” Brognano tells me about the inspiration, adding, “I started thinking about Mary-Kate and Ashley Olsen at the time. And a mix of girls like that—a little bit rockish, a little bit indie sleaze.” I felt this spirit wearing his clothes, a sensibility that I hadn’t experienced since my early 20s and one I thought I’d moved far away from. I am currently nine months postpartum. The outfit gave me a jolt of energy that felt exhilarating.

#6830. Nanofat Is Dermatologists’ Best-Kept Secret for Younger, Fresher Skin

It’s not an injectable filler, and it’s not fat transfer. It’s a secret third thing you’ll want to try.

The last few years have been unkind to our facial fat. We sliced out our buccal pads to create sunken-in, razor-sharp cheekbones. We zapped our skin with thermal devices that accidentally melted fat away in pursuit of boosting collagen. We injected our stomachs with fat-loss GLP-1 medications, leaving our faces looking hollow. Droopy. Structurally unsound. As it turns out, facial fat is critical infrastructure—not just for looking younger, but for your skin's overall health and quality. And while you can’t choose where you lose or gain fat, you can choose to have a doctor siphon it, remix it, and reinject it.

“Adipose tissue is a rich source of growth factors and progenitor cells, and re-injecting fat is probably one of the best ways to regenerate the full potential of healthy skin I've seen in the last decades,” says Patricia Ogilvie, a Munich-based dermatologist and member of the Dior Reverse Aging Board. “There are trends that are not really worth being a trend. I think the restaging of the importance of adipose tissue is very justified.”

You’re probably familiar with fat transfer, a procedure that’s been around for decades. In simple terms, fat transfer takes adipose tissue from one area of your body, like your flanks, liposuctions it, and moves it to another area, like your breasts or cheekbones. “With traditional fat transfer, it’s about restoring volume. And because it’s your own living tissue, it can integrate beautifully and give you very natural-looking results,” says dermatologist Shereene Idriss, medical director and founder of Idriss Dermatology in New York City. “But what’s interesting is that fat isn’t just fat. It also contains regenerative cells, including adipose-derived stem cells, and growth factors that may help improve the surrounding tissue over time. Nanofat is where it gets really exciting.”

Nanofat is part of a new class of regenerative aesthetics offered by dermatologists and plastic surgeons, treatments that don’t just make you look better, but rather address the signs of aging from the inside out by stimulating collagen or repairing tissue. Imagine taking that same fat from a traditional fat transfer and processing it into a fine, milky liquid rich in your own stem cells and growth factors, then injecting or microneedling it back into your face—that’s nanofat.

“Instead of using it to restore volume, you’re using it to potentially improve skin quality itself,” says Idriss. “These cells and growth factors communicate with surrounding tissue and can stimulate processes involved in collagen production, tissue repair, and blood vessel formation. So you’re not just trying to fill a wrinkle. You’re trying to improve the skin itself—its thickness, resilience, and texture—so that wrinkle potentially becomes less noticeable.”

The best areas for nanofat injections include under the eye, a notoriously risky area for hyaluronic acid fillers, and the cheekbones. “The ideal patient for nanofat is someone who's starting to notice thinner, crepier skin, fine lines, or changes in skin texture, especially around the eyes, neck, or chest, and wants to improve skin quality rather than add volume,” adds Idriss, who both has had the treatment done and offers it in her practice. Plus, Ogilvie notes, nanofat doesn’t attract water the way fillers do, so it won’t cause under-eye puffiness (a common post-filler complaint). But because nanofat doesn’t restore volume as much as it reinvigorates your skin, you can inject it almost anywhere you’re noticing signs of aging.

“We do it under tumescent local anesthesia, so you’re awake the entire time, and recovery is usually pretty quick,” says Idriss. “I personally had nanofat and fat transfer in June and was back at work the next day. The biggest downside is some soreness at the fat extraction site, which usually lasts a few days, and you’ll want to avoid strenuous exercise for about a week. Because there’s no general anesthesia, you’re also avoiding the grogginess and recovery associated with being put under.”

Like any cosmetic treatment, it has risks and downsides. Experts note that nanofat isn’t the right treatment for anyone looking to restore significant volume loss or seeking instant results. “Improvement in skin quality can vary from person to person, and it takes months to see the full results,” Idriss adds. “There are rare but serious risks, including infection, vascular complications, and even vision loss when injecting certain areas of the face.” But patients who have tried the procedure say the recovery is quick and easy, with results compounding over weeks, though they ultimately fade away faster than traditional injectables.

Nanofat injections sit somewhere between microfat and hyaluronic acid fillers. Microfat can be lumpy and unpredictable when injected, sometimes leading to lopsided results. Plus, in any fat transfer, only a small amount of fat survives. Fillers, on the other hand, offer long-lasting precision and volume restoration, but patients are tired of looking overfilled and unnaturally plump. Nanofat helps your skin look naturally healthier, fresher, and firmer, at least for a while. After all, it’s made from you.

“I look at this as something you can do periodically throughout your life, but what you do in between treatments is just as important. And that’s why I don’t believe in demonizing any one treatment, including filler,” says Idriss. “When used appropriately, for the right indication, these treatments can all work beautifully together. It’s about knowing what you need, when you need it, and when enough is enough.”

#6818. I Walked After Every Meal for Two Months, and My Digestion and Energy Levels Have Never Been Better

Experts weigh in on why sitting is the worst thing you can do after eating

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

It’s almost 9:00 p.m., and my work dinner in Manhattan’s SoHo neighborhood is finally coming to a close. As the people around me start to get up and call their Ubers, I head out the door to go home—on foot. After a lavish dinner, most people’s last thought is to get a brisk walk in, but for me, it’s crucial—not to hit a step count or burn calories, but as my version of a digestif, if you will.

My idea to walk after dinner came from a video I saw months ago from a TikTok nutritionist who touted a brisk 10-minute walk after meals as the key to boosting metabolism, regulating blood sugar levels, and helping your stomach digest food. My two favorite pastimes are eating and walking. I never thought the two would go hand in hand.

“Sitting briefly after eating isn’t inherently harmful, but prolonged, uninterrupted sitting can lead to a higher post-meal blood-sugar response compared with breaking it up with short walks,” says Elizabeth Sharp Edens, an internal medicine and obesity medicine physician, functional medicine practitioner, and founder of HMW Method. A 2022 meta-analysis in Sports Medicine found that light-intensity walking reduced post-meal glucose by almost 17 percent. The same study found that standing breaks after meals reduced glucose spikes by 9.5 percent on average. The TL;DR: Research says that any type of movement can improve glucose levels.

We know walking can do wonders—it can clear the mind, help you lean out, and even boost dopamine—but why is it a solution for feeling less full and avoiding afternoon crashes? “When you walk, your muscles use glucose for energy,” Edens explains. “Muscle contractions move glucose transporters called GLUT4 to the surface of muscle cells, helping draw glucose out of the bloodstream through a pathway that doesn’t depend on insulin.” Since your legs, the vehicle for walking, contain some of the biggest muscles in your body, it makes sense that walking effectively stimulates muscle contractions that move glucose transporters. “Walking breaks are a simple way to support blood sugar management while getting your steps in,” she adds.

While many rely on peptides, green powders, and “magic” supplements, walking is an easy, mindless (and free) way to improve digestion and prevent blood-sugar crashes. “Even two to five minutes after a meal can help, ideally before the glucose peak, typically 60 to 90 minutes after eating,” Kiran Krishnan, a research microbiologist, the founder and chief science officer of the Biome Learning Center, and Dose key opinion leader, adds.

I often joke after large meals that I will have to be rolled home. The good news: Walking can also reduce the overwhelming feeling of fullness. “Walking also helps the gut physically,” Krishnan says, citing a study that found that a slow walk after a meal significantly shortened the time it took the stomach to empty half its contents. “For people prone to bloating, mild [walking] cut intestinal gas retention from 45 percent to 24 percent and eased symptoms,” he adds. Plus, Krishnan says studies show reduced LDL cholesterol levels and improved liver function in those who walked after meals.

Then there’s good old gravity, says Caitlin Hosmer, a Maine-based registered dietitian and co-founder of MenoPantry. “Gravity helps too, which is part of why walking may help people who feel uncomfortably full or get reflux after meals,” she explains. “Gentle movement and staying upright help food move out of the stomach and along the digestive tract.”

Ten minutes isn’t long, but studies show it’s just as effective as a longer walk. A 2025 study published by PubMed Central found that a 10-minute walk lowered glucose measurements by 6 percent and a 30-minute walk lowered glucose levels by 5 percent, meaning just 10 minutes of walking is all you need to help reduce a post-meal glucose spike. If you can walk for 30 minutes, an hour, or even longer—that’s great. But just 10 minutes can work just as well.

What matters most is how quickly you get moving. “Glucose typically peaks 60 to 90 minutes after eating, and moving before that peak is what blunts the spike,” Hosmer says. “A walk puts your muscles to work just as glucose from the meal is flooding into the bloodstream.” At first, I questioned whether I had to walk after every meal. “Put your effort where it counts most: after your largest, most carbohydrate-rich meal,” Hosmer advises, adding that for most, that’s after dinner, but walking after all three meals is ideal.

My post-meal walks varied—some days I would walk for 30 minutes or even an hour, depending on how much time I had and where I was going next. But as I started incorporating them into my work schedule, especially on busy office days, I often had only 10 minutes to spare. When in a pinch, I would get up and walk around the office floor and, at home, do air squats, which engage the same large leg muscles as walking and can be just as effective as a post-meal walk.

It may feel strange to pencil in time to walk after every meal, but the benefits I have noticed surpass any inconvenience or awkwardness. I have fewer crashes after I eat, whereas I previously would feel lethargic after every meal. I’ve also noticed improved digestion. While this can be chalked up to a number of things (my beloved vibration plate, greens powders, or digestive enzymes), I always feel lighter and less full after a post-meal walk.

It’s also not meant to feel daunting or like a chore. “Your post-dinner walk isn’t a workout. It’s a way of helping your body handle the meal,” Hosmer says. “The one real ‘wrong way’ is skipping it because you think it has to be long or perfect. I often say something is better than nothing, and that’s especially true here. Even a few minutes of walking, started soon after you finish eating, can blunt the blood sugar rise, and it may help your digestion too.”

#6750. Your Expert-Backed Guide to Preparing for Top Surgery

From handling logistics ahead of time to a shopping list, experts share some of the best ways to make recovery from this major operation a little bit more manageable.

We may earn commission from links on this page, but we only recommend products we love. Promise.

Getting ready to go under the knife can be scary—even when it’s for a procedure you’ve wanted for as long as you can remember. If you’re preparing for top surgery (a gender-affirming chest surgery to remove or augment breast tissue) right now, chances are you’re probably trying to ease your pre-op anxiety by googling every question you can possibly think of. And that’s normal! But it can also lead you down some deep, dark Reddit threads that’ll do nothing but amplify your nerves.

If you’re wondering how to prepare for top surgery without all the panic-inducing anecdotes, you’ve come to the right place. I chatted with three experts and got their best advice on how to get ready for the procedure while preserving your peace of mind.

Before we get into the tips, keep in mind that the most important part of preparing for top surgery is choosing the right surgeon for you. In addition to finding an experienced and gender-affirming doctor, you’ll want to pick someone who can give you the results you’re looking for. “The techniques your surgeon will use can vary quite a lot depending on your breast size and shape, the texture and quality of your skin, and your goals for the procedure,” explains Eric Plemons, PhD, an associate professor of anthropology and cochair of the Transgender Studies Research Cluster at the University of Arizona.

To make sure you’ll be happy with your results, he suggests watching YouTube videos showcasing before-and-afters and looking at post-surgery images online to help you get an idea of what you want. Then be sure to find a surgeon who understands your goals—if they seem like they’re not listening to you, ditch them. Once you’ve made an appointment with a surgeon you trust, here’s everything else the experts recommend doing to prepare for top surgery.

This might seem like a no-brainer, but it’s worth mentioning. Before your surgery, your doctor’s office will normally provide you with instructions on what to do before and after the procedure. They typically include info on dietary guidelines, activity restrictions, wound care, and any medications you might need. Make sure that you read all of the information carefully, and keep it handy for the recovery process. “Following these instructions diligently significantly improves surgical outcomes and ensures a smoother recovery,” explains Scott Mosser, MD, FACS, a plastic surgeon who performs gender confirmation surgery and the founder of The Gender Confirmation Center of San Francisco.

Top surgery is a major surgery, and you’ll likely spend the first one to two weeks at home resting after the procedure. So it’s a good idea to prep your space accordingly. Make sure you’ve got a cozy couch or bed setup ready, and consider getting a mastectomy pillow to make lounging and sleeping more comfortable. The Trans Health Program at Fenway Health also recommends getting other items like a long phone charger, a grabber tool, and a back scratcher to make recovery go a bit smoother.

Also, keep in mind that you’re not supposed to lift your arms above your head in the days following top surgery. To make that easier to navigate, consider moving food and essentials to lower, easy-to-reach spots, suggests Plemons.

The last thing you want to do is spend your recovery period stressing about going back to your job. Be sure you request enough time off to allow yourself to truly rest and heal. Exactly how much time you’ll need to spend OOO varies from person to person, so it’s a good idea to chat it through with your surgeon at one of your pre-op appointments.

One of the main factors that determines how long you’ll need to take off work is how physically demanding your job is. You’ll have restricted movement in the weeks after your surgery, and you won’t be able to lift heavy objects, which could impact your responsibilities, depending on your line of work. “I generally ask patients to identify the most physically strenuous aspect of their job, and together we determine an appropriate leave period,” explains Dr. Mosser.

For your post-op wardrobe, you’ll want a stack of loose-fitting shirts that are easy to put on and take off without raising your arms. (Yup, t-shirts might be off the table for a bit.) Button-up and zip-up options will be your best friends during the recovery period. The loose-fitting aspect is also important because you may need space for surgical drain tubes under your shirt. Tops with interior pockets can also be helpful if you do end up with drains, says Dr. Mosser. And heads-up: Your shirts might get stained during the healing process, so Plemons recommends grabbing some options from the thrift store so that you’re not worried about ruining them.

In order to help your skin adjust post-surgery, you may want to get an emollient-infused moisturizer like Bio-Oil or Palmer’s Cocoa Butter Lotion. As long as it’s okay with your doctor, you can even start applying these one to two weeks before the procedure to start prepping your skin, if you’d like. After the surgery, Dr. Mosser recommends using one of these moisturizers on your chest every morning and evening—but be careful to avoid your incision sites. This will help with skin relaxation and reduce the appearance of stretch marks, if that’s something you’re concerned about, he explains.

Double-check with your surgeon, but after any scabs from the surgery have fallen off, you should also be able to start using silicone scar tape. Dr. Mosser suggests using it for three months to help optimize healing.

If you vape or smoke cigarettes or weed, you’ll need to quit ahead of your top surgery. The American College of Surgeons recommends quitting smoking four to six weeks before any major surgery and staying smoke-free for at least four weeks post-op to lower your risk of complications, and most doctors require a similar timeline for top surgery.

The stakes are high: According to the Gender Confirmation Center of San Francisco, smoking increases the risk of anesthesia complications and postoperative complications like losing a nipple graft, which can require additional surgery to fix. It also delays healing, which can prolong the recovery process and make it more uncomfortable. Dr. Mosser requires all of his patients to stop smoking for at least three weeks before and after top surgery—no exceptions.

You’ll need some help taking care of yourself and your basic needs in the days following top surgery, so talk to your friends and/or family in advance to put together a plan. “We’ve consistently seen better outcomes in patients who have a positive, supportive presence in their lives. The best way to ensure this support is to communicate openly with loved ones in advance, set clear expectations about what assistance might be needed, and cultivate an environment of encouragement and care,” Dr. Mosser says.

Decide who’s going to drive you home after the procedure (you won’t be allowed to drive yourself), and think about who might be a good fit to help you with activities like getting dressed and showering the first few days, suggests Plemons. You’ll probably also need someone to help you with tasks like meal prep, medication reminders, carrying groceries, and caring for any pets for a bit.

In addition to logistical support, you’ll likely need some emotional support during the top surgery process. The procedure is often something you’ve been anticipating for a long time, and it can be an emotional experience. “Such a big event comes with big feelings about your body, about yourself, and about your future,” says Plemons. So think about who you plan to lean on for support as you navigate your feelings both pre- and post-op. Staying connected to your community—whether that be friends, chosen family, or an online community—is really important. “Even small moments of support, like check-ins or affirming conversations, can make a huge difference in feeling cared for during recovery,” adds Jesse Kahn, LCSW-R, CST, director and sex therapist at the Gender & Sexuality Therapy Center in New York.

“Top surgery is a significant emotional and physical milestone, and a range of feelings—excitement, anxiety, or unexpected emotions—are completely normal,” explains Kahn. They recommend giving yourself permission to feel and process your emotions without judgment or criticism. #6715. Designers Say "Cook's Tables" Are About to Replace Kitchen Islands in 2027

Their versatility is just part of their appeal.

Pictured Above: A kitchen designed by Amy Sklar.

For years, oversized kitchen islands have been the be-all and end-all, but lately, designers and homeowners are gravitating toward something a bit smaller: a cook’s table.

Unlike built-in islands that can sometimes dominate a kitchen and cover most of the available floor space, cook’s tables bring a lot of flexibility to the room. Because they’re freestanding, you can move them around depending on your needs (especially if they’re on wheels), whether that’s for prepping meals, hanging out with your morning coffee, or helping kids with homework after school. They also tend to be narrower, making them the perfect solution for small kitchens.

The other benefit is the big charm factor they add. They’re often wooden, with traditional-looking design elements, like turned legs and curvy silhouettes. Cooks tables can also frequently be found in vintage and antique stores, so they’re a great way to work a bit of charm and patina into an otherwise utilitarian space.

As kitchens move away from ultra-sleek and minimal, pieces with warmth and character are becoming more and more popular, and a cook’s table is the perfect example of that. If you’re considering adding one to your own home, learn what to look for—and how to style it—below.

One of the biggest reasons cook’s tables are trending right now is that they soften the overall look and feel of a kitchen. “A traditional island can dominate a kitchen,” says designer Amy Sklar. Because cook’s tables don’t have a bulky built-in base, they feel visually lighter and less imposing, especially in kitchens where space is limited.

Designers also point to the emotional shift happening in homes post-pandemic. “The pandemic reshaped the way we live at home, placing greater value on spaces designed for connection and togetherness,” says designer Becky Ducsik of The Phinery. “Nowhere is this more apparent than in the kitchen, where homeowners are reimagining how they gather and entertain.”

Unlike traditional islands that are packed with appliances and storage, cook’s tables create a more communal focal point centered around gathering and spending time together. That relaxed atmosphere is part of what makes them feel so appealing. Designer Cynthia Lananh notes that cook’s tables strike a balance between practicality and personality, functioning as prep space, dining space, and entertaining hub all at once. “I love a kitchen island that looks like a piece of furniture,” she says, adding that the look feels more casual, approachable, and timeless than a standard built-in island.

While cook’s tables can absolutely make a statement in a large kitchen, designers say they’re especially effective in kitchens with tighter footprints or galley-style layouts. Because they take up less visual and physical space than a traditional island, they can help smaller kitchens feel more open and airy.

That said, larger kitchens can benefit from them too, just in a different way. “With the right vintage find or custom-crafted piece, it can anchor the room and become a striking centerpiece that adds both function and timeless appeal,” says Ducsik.

She recommends using cook’s tables as the focal point in kitchens where storage and appliances can be concentrated along the perimeter. Since seating typically wraps around all sides, she suggests leaving at least four to five feet of clearance around the table to maintain a comfortable flow.

For homeowners who want the look but don’t have space for a freestanding table in the center of the room, Ducsik also suggests integrating one as a peninsula extension at the end of cabinetry for a similar communal feel.

Unlike built-in islands, cook’s tables offer a level of flexibility that’s hard to beat. Since they’re freestanding, they can be moved or adapted more easily over time—a feature designers say is especially useful for entertaining.

Sklar points out that cook’s tables can create a flexible landing zone within a kitchen and are ideal for holidays or large gatherings when extra room is needed. “This is perfect for holidays and large gatherings where you need even more space,” Lananh says. She recommends adding casters if the table will double as both a prep and dining space, making it easier to move around as needed.

Scale is one of the most important things to get right: The table should feel proportional to the room while still leaving enough space to comfortably move around it. Height matters, too. Sklar notes that many vintage tables sit lower than standard counter height, so homeowners may need to modify them slightly with casters or leg extenders to make them more functional for prep work.

As for materials, wood remains the go-to choice for warmth and character. “Durable woods (like oak, maple, or walnut) are popular for their warmth, strength, and character,” says Lananh. “Reclaimed wood adds rustic charm, whereas metal or butcher block tops offer a more durable, industrial aesthetic.”

Ducsik echoes those sentiments and suggests echoing the table’s wood tones throughout the kitchen with details like open shelving, cutting boards, or serving pieces to create a cohesive look.

Lastly, focus on shape. Softer corners and curves can make the space feel more intimate, while rectangular tables can maximize seating and prep space. Ultimately, the goal is for the piece to feel like furniture, not cabinetry. And that’s exactly what makes cook’s tables so appealing in the first place.

It’s made my cook space so much more functional.

Ahead, here’s why I love having a pop-up outlet, what to consider before installing one, and a few other kitchen gadgets that you might want to consider adding to your next upgrade.

Like the name suggests, a pop-up outlet is basically an electrical outlet that hides inside your countertop until you need it. Most models have a top you press or pull, and the outlet rises up so you can plug in your blender, mixer, or whatever else you're using. When you're done, you push it back down and it's out of sight.

There are a few different versions, including ones that pop up manually and others that rise with the push of a button. Some, like the one we have, include USB ports or wireless charging built in. The outlet connects to power underneath the countertop, so it's something you'll want to plan for before your new counters go in.

Like pretty much any kitchen gadget, there are a range of options available from budget to high end. We opted for something mid-tier that still included the main functions we wanted like the additional USB ports and the charging capability on the top. If you’re a chronic “let your phone get down to one or two percent before finally charging it” person like me, I highly recommend this.

There also are some that will go nearly fully flush with the countertop, and can even have part of the countertop material on the top of it to look seamless. We did not go this route, but if you prefer the look of a flawless countertop, this might be the way to go.

We put ours in the corner of our kitchen where it’s almost entirely hidden by decor. That corner of the kitchen was otherwise without any spots to plug in appliances, so this fixed multiple problems by having electricity access while remaining discreet.

You can really put them anywhere you want, but keep in mind where you’ll need extra power accessibility and where it won’t be an eyesore, if that’s a concern. I’m also a big fan of in-drawer charging outlets.

#6695. 5 Ways Warmth Has Shaped Interior Design in 2026, According to Experts

Warmth is more than a palette—it’s the secret to a home you never want to leave.

After years of cool neutrals and minimalist interiors, there’s been a shift toward warmer, more inviting spaces that feel layered, personal, and lived-in.

“Warmth has become more than just a color palette or a particular material. It's about spaces that feel authentic, comfortable, and reflective of the people who live in them,” says Laura Gerber, founder of ODE Design.

At the end of the day, a warm home should feel inviting and effortless, with materials and details that make people want to spend time there. If you’re looking to add some warmth to your home, take inspiration from these designer-approved ideas.

Wood’s organic grain brings instant warmth to both modern and traditional homes, adding depth without making a space feel overly formal.

“We’re incorporating more white oak, walnut, and rift-cut cabinetry than we have in years, along with wood ceilings and paneled walls,” explains Rachel Simpson, senior interior designer at Revive Design and Renovation.

There’s been a shift from stark white countertops toward natural quartzite, travertine, and marble with warmer veining. These materials have natural variation and movement that bring character into a space.

“Honed finishes, in particular, feel softer and more organic than high-polish surfaces. They allow the beauty of the stone to speak for itself without feeling overly formal or glossy,” explains Simpson.

These days, people really want their homes to feel like a sanctuary.

“In a recent project, the client envisioned a grown-up treehouse nestled among the evergreens. We wanted to create a warm yet contemporary mountain retreat that felt luxurious but also comfortable and welcoming,” says Charles Greenfeld, founder and principal designer at Ethan Charles Design.

Rather than relying on one or two dramatic statement pieces, the interiors were designed to be experienced as a whole, with each detail contributing to that warm aesthetic.

Creating warmth is really about creating layers of thoughtfully curated design elements. This helps to create a home you want to enter and never leave.

“Well-proportioned rugs, textures on walls, loads of art, draperies, and lighting overhead and at eye level are just some of the things we can add to our spaces to make them feel cozy and accessible,” says Ashley Powell, cofounder and interior designer of Roseberry Allen.

Designers have lately been drawn to rooms where the colors are close to one another, but the materials do different things.

“A woven rug, a textured wall, and a piece of wood with a visible grain each catch the light differently,” says Jess Klein, founder and principal of Jess Klein Studio.

That variation adds visual depth and makes the room feel warmer and more inviting, even when the color palette is understated.

#6691. A Kitchen in Bloom: How Tiffany Howell and Miele Reimagine the Heart of the Home

Eschewing conventional design for an moody, romantic kitchen, this wildly glamorous space proves that high-tech convenience can foster intimacy, slowness, and the art of living well.

When Tiffany Howell of Night Palm Interiors designs a home, her vision transcends conventional norms, veering into an ethereal realm—an approach she calls “the poetry of place.” In Howell’s mind, film, fashion, and music are as essential as decorative art, and she uses these inspirations to create theatrical spaces that ignite all the senses—layering texture, sound, scent, light, and form to evoke deep emotion. When executed with this level of intention, a room becomes an environment you don't merely look at but fundamentally feel.

When Miele partnered with Howell to create a dream kitchen for an installation at Art Basel, she seized the opportunity to reimagine what a kitchen can be. The result is the interactive 360-degree rendering seen here—a wildly imaginative digital environment that inspired the brand’s upcoming physical installation at Miami’s premier art fair. “I wanted an undeniable sexiness, a beautiful, sensory experience,” she says. “I created the space with characters in mind that I’d invite to a dinner party in this kitchen.” After all, the kitchen is a home’s heartbeat, an interactive space with multiple purposes.

“I do think the kitchen is really one of the most tender rooms in the house,” Howell says. She leaned into that romance by installing black cabinetry with blossoming branches and birds in iridescent greens and pinks. The cabinets are an homage to her love of 1920s chinoiserie furniture and fashion, seen through a very glamorous, Los Angeles lens. That California focus also meant bringing the outdoors in. The grand conservatory space harnesses diffused, natural light with the promise of the scent of jasmine wafting into the room. Howell’s ideal playlist sets the tone with jazz greats and ambient synth-pop.

“I wanted to create a kitchen that’s a completely sensory experience,” Howell explains. “The sound of a crackling warm fireplace, the aroma of a meal cooked for loved ones, or of making coffee in the morning.” For Howell, these aren't incidental details; they are the sensory architecture that turns raw space into lived emotion. In Howell’s view, this kitchen is “also a place where you can take a nap, read a book, or write in your journal. Every moment in this room can become a ritualistic experience of living well.”

When it came to the practical needs of a kitchen, Howell used Miele as a muse, an invitation to eschew the pale minimalism so common in luxury kitchens. “The appliances are so elegant, they kind of act as the jewelry of the room,” she says. “And their functionality and ease of operation give you back time to live better, relax with a drink, hang out with family and friends, and create the stories that make up a life.”

Consider the Miele Built-In Coffee System and wine cooler in matching dark finishes which are nearly camouflaged so the technology reads more as furniture than appliance. That tension between heirloom design and high-tech styling becomes a unified part of a lived-in, well-collected home. Accent pieces, including a vintage Fortuny pendant lamp over the kitchen island, and upholstered chairs in the conservatory with detailing reminiscent of antique lingerie, create a moody environment well suited to Miele's black-finished lines that resemble rich obsidian.

The 36” Full Surface Induction Cooktop features a frameless, flush design that disappears into a dark-veined countertop. Transforming the entire glass surface into one expansive cooking area, it fits up to six pots or pans at once and with Intelligent Pan Recognition and SmartSelect White controls seamlessly transfer power settings wherever pans are moved. On the same wall is the 30” Handleless Combi-Steam Oven XXL, with DualSteam technology that emanates ASMR sounds of steam cooking, and a black glass façade that resembles a framed panel or window. From the cool, flush glide of the induction cooktop to the whisper-quiet hum of precision steam, the technology engages the senses rather than interrupting them, turning modern convenience into an intuitive, tactile ritual. Rather than breaking up the wall with a stack of conspicuous appliances, these seamless integrations keep the architecture artful. And for added convenience, Miele’s elegant touch controls are enhanced with Wi-Fi connectivity for an easier user experience.

Howell’s concealment of technology is exhibited in her use of the 42” French Door Refrigerator with its elongated custom handles and a dark lacquered paneled front that echoes the floral chinoiserie motifs—an unconventional solution that turns a mundane appliance into a piece of decorative art. Once the refrigerator doors are open, the Brilliant Light Pro illumination makes it easy to survey the food inside the large capacity interior.

“How do you nurture yourself and others in the kitchen?” Howell asks. Her answer: a design that creates a restorative space evoking intimacy and slowness, where technology can encourage a lightness and give us back more time. By composing every sight, surface, scent, and sound into a single harmonious narrative, Howell proves that a kitchen shouldn't just be an aesthetic statement—it should be something you feel in your bones. “What excites me is building a new world with more possibilities,” Howell says. “I don’t think I could design any other way.”
