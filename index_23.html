#6958. Sophos Launches Tool for Shadow AI Defense

Unauthorized artificial intelligence tools and agents are creating security risks for organizations as AI adoption increases, according to cybersecurity company Sophos. In a recent product announcement, the company highlighted the growing use of shadow AI, referring to AI tools and agents operating without formal organizational approval or oversight. The security firm cited research indicating that 69% of organizations suspect or have evidence of unauthorized AI use.

Separate research from Gartner and Cloud Security Alliance found that 82% of organizations discovered shadow AI agents in their environments during the previous year.

According to the security firm, organizations have struggled to identify which AI tools employees use, what information those tools access, and whether their use complies with security policies.

"Organizations are adopting AI faster than they can secure it, and most security teams can't answer three basic questions: which AI tools are running, what data is going into them, and whether that activity lines up with policy," said Kyle Falkenhagen, senior vice president of product management at Sophos.

The company also cited an IDC projection that the number of AI agents could reach 1.3 billion by 2028.

Sophos researchers reported observing attackers use AI agents within a customer's network to build and test nearly 80 malware modules.

The cybersecurity firm announced the general availability of Sophos AI Defense, a security offering designed to identify AI tools, monitor their activity, and enforce organizational security policies.

The product operates through Sophos Fusion, the company's cybersecurity platform, and uses information from devices, networks, identity systems, and browsers.

According to the company, AI Defense can inspect prompts, uploads, and other AI interactions to identify potentially risky activity and help prevent sensitive data exposure.

#6951. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

For more information, go to the Microsoft blog.

#6947. Inside a Layered Ski-in, Ski-out Retreat in the Mountains of Utah

An affectionate nickname inspired much of the design details.

Designers learn a lot of things about their clients when building or renovating their homes—it’s how they ensure the spaces actually feel like the homeowners. Their hobbies shape the purposes of distinct rooms, their tastes affect the color palette, and their experiences help to build out the decor. So, when designer McKenzie Matthews of Coven Haus (accidentally) learned that her client affectionately calls her husband The Wolf, she simply had to weave that part of their story into the design of their Park City, Utah, winter retreat.

“What started as a playful detail became a recurring theme, from a custom wolf head silhouette repeated along the secondary staircase to wolf hooks in the mudroom lockers,” Matthews says. The designer, who was part of the six-bedroom, seven-bathroom new-build from the start, wanted every aspect of the interior to feel like a luxurious and elevated boutique ski resort but with more personalized charm. “We carried the wolf motif into decorative pillows, artwork, and accessories, creating little moments of personality and meaning throughout the home.”

Outside of these smaller details, the rest of the ski-in, ski-out style home is layered with natural elements, like stone, reclaimed wood, and plaster, to complement the views outside of the Wasatch Mountain Range. The front door opens to a wood-clad foyer, topped with a vaulted ceiling accented with reclaimed wooden beams, which continues into the great room. Flanked by two double-sided stone fireplaces and looking out beyond the second floor of the deck into the woods, this gathering area invites any guest to escape the cold of the slopes and stay as long as they like.

Handmade and vintage pieces fill in any gaps, giving each room a collected feel. “One of my favorite examples is the primary bedroom bookcase, filled with unique objects and accessories we’ve gathered over the years,” Matthews says. “It’s those little details that give a home so much character.”

Spanning 11,758 square feet, it can be hard to make a house of this size truly feel homey, but Matthews’ dedication to the details ensured it was. “Every room has something special and unexpected, yet the entire home still feels cohesive,” she says. “I love that there's always something new to discover as you move through each space.”

The entry sets the tone for the rest of the home, with the wood-paneled walls, vaulted ceiling, limestone floor tile, and reclaimed beams serving as a warm, inviting escape from the cold outside. The variety of textures brings character and, along with the sculptural entry table, helps to soften the grand architectural details.

No detail is too small in this ski room. Marble knobs dot the top lockers, while a Calacatta Viola marble door jamb makes the mundane feel special. “With our clients being from Argentina, we wanted to do something a little unexpected and playful, starting with the textural floor tile,” Matthews says.

The fun continues to the ceiling, which is coated in the same red that lines the interior of the oak lockers, just in a matte finish. “We loved the idea of it feeling like a jewelry box,” the designer says of the painted locker interiors.

Fabric panels on the upper lockers soften the millwork and break up the oak, as does the painted boot wall which opens to the ski patio. “There are so many little details in this room, and I love every one of them!” Matthews adds.

Laying out the great room was a bit of a challenge. Flanked by two double-sided stone fireplaces, Matthews wanted to take advantage of the hearths without blocking or sacrificing the incredible view. “We opted for movable seating that could easily shift depending on how the space is used, while keeping the view as the star of the room,” she explains. The tumbleweed chandelier was the cherry on top, creating a bit of unexpected fun while bringing in an organic element.

To the right of the living room, beyond the fireplace, is a gaming corner, slightly blocked away from the main area in case any matches get a bit rowdy.

Downstairs in the living room, Matthews wanted plenty of seating for family and friends to gather, so they ordered a custom sectional with extra-deep seat cushions that comfortably seats at least eight people, if not more. “The reclaimed wood ceiling and exposed beams add so much warmth and character, while the soft furnishings keep the space feeling relaxed and lived in,” she adds.

If the family still has energy after a day skiing, they can always bond over a game of poker. Rich dark cocoa millwork and light-blocking drapery close off this space from the rest of the house, while the wall of books gives it character and ensures it still feels homey. If poker isn’t the game of choice, the wood top is removable so it doubles as a regular gaming or puzzle table.

“This might be my favorite room in the house,” Matthews says. “The ideas just kept flowing, and we reworked the bar layout a handful of times before landing here.” While the entire setup would make the most seasoned bartender jealous, it’s the details—like the patinated brass island, the tiled ceiling, the double bullnose countertop edge, and necklace-like sculptural chandelier—that makes the space a moment. “A little five-star resort experience without ever leaving home.”

Because Matthews’s clients wanted a dirty kitchen and a main one for entertaining, the designer could be a bit more creative here, choosing the finishes and features more for the way they look rather than how well they function. A calming blue and brown color palette makes it the most enticing spot for a quiet morning coffee or for setting out a grand buffet for party guests.

Since this is where all the cooking happens, functionality was Matthews’s biggest priority, from the layout to the actual appliances and finishes. “The kitchen layout changed several times throughout the process, and it certainly helped that our builder, Don Jones [of Craig Construction], was once a chef!” she explains. “We really prioritized flow and functionality first, then let the design follow.”

Blue cabinetry ties in with the stove while bronze hardware does the same with the custom patinated brass hood, which warms everything up and will only get better-looking with age, as will the quartz countertops. The vintage plates on the wall add personality and style to this workhorse space.

In the wine cellar and tasting room, the architectural elements were quite linear, so Matthews opted for an arched doorway to create some movement. “We loved the idea of the temperature controlled wine cellar having a cavernous feel, which is why we chose to bring natural stone into the interior,” she explains.

Another favorite room of Matthews’, she especially loves the bookcase since it gives the cozy room an almost historic, collected feel. To add height and balance the scale of the bookshelves, she hung a tapestry rug above the bed, creating a layered, timeless escape for the homeowners.

The wallpaper, paired with the ruched velvet bed frame, adds romantic appeal to this guest bedroom. Vintage artworks and furniture, like the trunk, lend a collected feel, bringing the space to life. “The vintage sketches were carefully framed and hung from brass chains, adding a nod to the past and giving the room a sense of history,” the designer explains.

With over a decade in the design industry, McKenzie Matthews firmly believes good design should feel effortless and evolve over time. #6946. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#6940. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. #6907. 5 Designer-Approved Ways to Make a Small Bathroom Feel Full of Personality

When decorating a small bathroom, it's tempting to stick to the tried-and-tested rules. White walls to make the space feel bigger, minimal accessories to avoid clutter, and perhaps a neutral tile or two. But where's the fun in that?

Just because your bathroom is short on square footage doesn't mean it has to be short on personality. In fact, smaller spaces can be the perfect place to experiment with colors, patterns, and decorating ideas you might hesitate to try elsewhere in your home.

Whether you're working with a compact family bathroom, a downstairs WC, or a tiny en suite, here are five ways to give your space a little more character.

White might be the traditional choice for small bathrooms, but it's certainly not the only option.

Bold colors can make a compact space feel intentional and full of personality, whether you opt for a rich, cocooning shade or something brighter and more playful.

You could paint the entire room for maximum impact, or introduce color through a single wall, vanity unit, or painted woodwork. For a more cohesive look, try picking out a shade from your tiles and repeating it elsewhere in the room.

If there's one room where you can afford to be a little adventurous with tiles, it's the bathroom.

Colorful square tiles, checkerboard patterns, and interesting layouts can all introduce character without taking up valuable floor space.

And you don't necessarily need to tile every wall either. A bold backsplash behind the basin, a tiled shower enclosure, or a contrasting border can make just as much impact.

For a more understated approach, consider using simple tiles in an unexpected color or experimenting with contrasting grout.

When space is limited, every surface counts—and your bathroom windowsill is the perfect place to introduce a little personality without cluttering your countertops. Pictured here, Annie Sloan's bathroom features a sweeping landscape painted right on the bath panel.

Rather than leaving it bare, use it to display a few carefully chosen accessories. A small vase of fresh flowers or a decorative ceramic ornament can make an otherwise functional space feel more considered.

You could even create a little display by grouping objects of different heights and textures, taking inspiration from the way you might style a shelf in your living room.

Just be careful not to overcrowd the space. A few thoughtfully chosen pieces will make much more impact than a windowsill packed with accessories, and you'll still want to leave enough room to open the window for ventilation.

Houseplants aren't just for living rooms and bedrooms. They can bring a welcome splash of color and life to a bathroom, softening hard surfaces and making even the smallest space feel more inviting.

Bathrooms can be particularly well suited to tropical plants that appreciate humidity, such as ferns, peace lilies, and orchids, provided they receive enough natural light.

If you're short on floor space, consider hanging a trailing plant from the ceiling or placing one on a wall-mounted shelf. A pothos cascading from a high shelf can look particularly striking against colorful tiles.

And don't forget about the pots themselves. Colorful ceramics and woven planters can be just as decorative as the greenery they hold.

Sometimes, it's the smallest details that make the biggest impact.

Swap plain towels for colorful or patterned designs, introduce a sculptural soap dispenser, or choose a mirror with an interesting frame.

A brightly colored bath mat can bring an otherwise neutral scheme to life, while coordinating toothbrush holders, storage jars, and trays can make everyday essentials feel more considered.

The trick is to choose a few accessories that complement one another, rather than filling every available surface.

And if you're decorating on a budget, this is one of the easiest ways to experiment with a new look without making any permanent changes.

#6906. Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

The findings echo a recent report about management software and AI e-mail assistants. Research from Fortra and Barracuda showed how criminals are using legitimate administrative tools and hidden AI instructions to manipulate users and compromise systems.

#6898. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

#6880. This St. Bart’s House Was Designed to Look Nothing Like Its Neighbors

Built for maximum family time, a converted boutique hotel puts the emphasis on entertaining, hanging out, and blending into the surroundings.

Few clients ask their designer to make the bedrooms in their vacation home smaller and simpler, but that was just the edit the homeowners made to plans for their house in St. Bart’s, says designer David Ries. “They didn’t want these humongous bedrooms with huge floating beds and massive bathrooms.” Instead, the couple wanted to be able to host as many people as possible, a request that didn’t surprise Ries, who’d already worked with the family on homes in Manhattan, Long Island, and Telluride. “It’s what this client has always asked for. They can’t have enough bedrooms.”

Situated on a steep hillside above one of St. Bart’s famously pristine white sand beaches, the house had initially been a boutique hotel. The plan was to turn it into a retreat for themselves and their adult children and grandchildren. The family are longtime visitors to the island—“the daughter told me it was her ‘happy place,’” says Ries, lead designer of the New York-based interior design firm Ries Hayes—and knew exactly what they wanted. Not the white box look popular with luxury new builds on the island, but instead something reminiscent of the “old St. Bart’s,” says East Hampton-based landscape designer Edwina von Gal, who, like Ries, has collaborated with the clients on other projects.

A Caribbean island is a challenging place to work under any circumstances. Everything has to be brought in from far away, including all the plants, which came by barge from Miami. St. Bart’s in particular requires anyone building to work with a local architect and engineer. In their initial proposal, this French team defaulted to that “slick and modernist” look, as von Gal describes it, with the oversized bedrooms and white color scheme that dominates much of the island’s luxury housing stock.

The design team pushed back—asking for a lot of bedrooms (eight) and generous living spaces. They also had strong opinions on the building materials. Given the island’s climate, the humid, salt-soaked air can be hard on everything. “If we did white paint, which is prominent throughout St. Bart's, it’s constant maintenance, constant painting,” says Ries. The design team insisted on cladding the buildings in Ipe, the durable tropical hardwood, which wears well. It mystified the local architects and set the complex apart from its neighbors. It also makes the buildings disappear into the greenery of the hillside.

But the architects quickly caught on to the new plans, and von Gal is eager to praise their technical accomplishments. Regulations require properties to conserve rainwater, so there are cisterns beneath the buildings, plus technology infrastructure and laundry facilities. “Working on the hill is so complex,” she says. “It's such a tight little package of technology and function. What they did was amazing.”

The team decided to reconfigure the property as a series of pavilions that face the view of the ocean and surrounding hills. The main entry is at the top of the steep hill, along with parking. The next level down is for family bedrooms; below that is a primary suite and study; and finally the fourth level is the main living space, including a playroom. “We made sure this playroom was front and center,” says Ries. “My client loves her grandkids.”

The playroom and study have built-in daybeds that can be used as spare guest rooms in a pinch. But the clients did get their eight bedrooms. “We know how to make compact bedrooms and make them very efficient, make them very livable, which allows us to fit in a lot of space for a lot of guests, and then that puts a lot of emphasis on having main entertaining spaces that are big and gracious,” says Ries. “The idea is that everybody hangs out together.”

Everything is united by generous outdoor spaces, which wind between the bedrooms. “Every room has an amazing view,” says Ries. “It feels almost like your own little treehouse in a way.” The bottom living area opens out to a pool and an expansive deck where the family eats most of their meals.

The house has an elevator, but stairs are the primary way the family moves between levels and finally down to the beach. “We hung on for a really great, gracious, fun set of stairs,” says von Gal. “They really introduce you to how amazing the different levels are and all the wonderful secret places.”

Durable fabrics and materials were chosen throughout and Ries made sure everything could be easily moved around and reconfigured. Outside on the main deck, three long dining tables from Sutherland were outfitted with casters so they could be maneuvered into a single long table or several smaller ones; 26 lightweight dining chairs can be pulled from different parts of the deck. If the infamous “Christmas winds” bring a downpour, the tables can even be moved indoors so a dinner party can proceed in inclement weather. Outdoor seating can be arranged as a 15-foot sofa or several love seats.

“It's so flexible,” says Ries. “We know this from working with a client that their needs for entertaining change every day, or sometimes a couple times a day.”

The kitchen is also efficient, designed to produce easy, clean meals. The family would rather spend time in the pool than over a stove. And no part of the living room is oriented around a TV. “They're readers,” says Ries. “There’s always stacks of new books when you go to their homes. So there’s all these cool little spots you can tuck into.”

Ultimately, the client and design team’s insistence on what they wanted paid off—but it wasn’t without a few moments of doubt. “When so many people are telling you ‘no, surely this is not what you want,’ it does start to erode your confidence a little,” says von Gal. “Then when it does come out well, there’s a real sense of triumph and satisfaction.”

It’s a small holiday-hosting dilemma with reasonable arguments on either side: The host would rather not have wet boots on the good rug; the guest would rather not spend a cocktail party in stocking feet.

So, is the request rude? Not inherently, according to the etiquette and entertaining experts we asked. But the occasion, the advance notice, and the room you leave for exceptions all matter. Here’s how to handle it before the doorbell rings.

For some households, taking shoes off is simply what you do when you come in, whether you’re stopping by for coffee or staying for Thanksgiving dinner. There’s a practical argument for keeping the rule during the holidays, too. Stacey Gustafson, Bona’s flooring and cleaning expert, points out that grit tracked in on outdoor shoes can scratch hardwood and wear down its finish over time.

But not every shoes-off request is a bid to preserve perfect floors. Removing outdoor footwear is an established cultural custom in Japan, for example, where the genkan, or entrance area, separates outside from in. What one guest considers an unexpected inconvenience may be an entirely ordinary expression of respect in another person’s home.

For David Jubé, founder of food and entertaining platform The Gourmet Host, the request itself is less of a problem than the way it’s delivered. “Asking guests to take their shoes off is not rude. Making them guess is,” he says. “The rudeness, when it happens, comes from springing the request at the door with no warning, so a guest finds out in front of everyone that they wore mismatched socks or that their boots take five minutes to unlace.”

“If you’ve invited guests to dress up for an elegant holiday dinner, asking them to remove their shoes upon arrival can undermine the very experience you’ve invited them to enjoy,” says Erika Gessin, founder and creative director of entertaining brand The Fête Edit. “Shoes are often an intentional part of an outfit.”

If shoes are coming off regardless, guests should know before they plan what to wear. For hosts willing to relax their usual rule, etiquette expert Nick Leighton suggests letting the occasion take precedence: “For larger or more formal gatherings, it’s ideal to just let everyone keep their shoes on and treat the cleaning you’ll have to do afterwards as part of the cost of entertaining.”

Comfort matters as much as appearance. Amber Dawn Schultheis, creator of hosting and recipe site The Host Notes, points out that some guests need the support of their shoes, especially if they’ll be standing for an extended period. Her preferred approach leaves the decision with them: “Shoes are optional. Do what makes you feel good.”

If you’re keeping the rule, make it easy to follow. Jubé suggests writing, “We’re a shoes-off house, so bring cozy socks or slippers if you like” on the invitation. At the door, give guests somewhere to sit while they unlace their boots, a rack or tray to put them on, and a basket of clean slippers or grippy socks. If someone needs to keep their shoes on for health, mobility, or safety reasons, let them do so without asking for an explanation.

And if you’re the guest? Take your cue from the shoes already by the door, not from what you do at home. If it isn’t clear, Gessin recommends asking quietly: “Would you prefer I take my shoes off?”

Jubé recommends a sturdy mat outside, a washable one inside, and a boot tray for wet footwear. Gessin suggests washable runners in high-traffic areas—the hall between the front door and the dining room is a good place to start. Those small adjustments let you give guests a choice without spending the party keeping tabs on the floor.

Once everyone is inside, resist the urge to monitor every footprint. “Guests remember how a gathering felt, not whether the floor stayed spotless, and a host fretting over every footprint makes everyone tense,” Jubé says.

“Floors can be cleaned tomorrow,” Gessin says. “How you made your guests feel is what they’ll remember.”

#6849. The 50 Best Sports Movies of All Time

We’re long overdue for a reassessment of the genre’s best of the best.

If Esquire’s list of the greatest sports movies of all time was accompanied by a training montage, it’d look something like this: the entertainment editors hunkered down at home, night after night, watching (and rewatching) our way through the great canon of this beloved genre. Caddyshack, still a blast. Talladega Nights, again? Twist my arm. A first watch of Breaking Away, a blind spot no more. Remember the Titans, for probably the seventeenth time.

Smash cut to us fighting about which films were truly worthy of our final top fifty. A long debate about whether or not The Color of Money is actually better than The Hustler. An editor’s brilliant suggestion that The Fast & the Furious: Tokyo Drift is actually a sports movie. A mutual decision to cut Friday Night Lights because, really, does it deserve to be here if the TV show is the one we remember most?

Final shot: the writing. Writing. Writing. Writing.

Now, this is the part where you take it all in, rewatch one (or four) of your favorites, decide what you agree with, what you simply cannot stand for, and let us know about it. Let’s walk through the tunnel together, shall we? These are the best fifty sports movies of all time.—Brady Langmann

Burt Reynolds stars in The Longest Yard as the incarcerated former quarterback, Paul "Wrecking" Crewe, who was kicked out of the NFL after admitting to point shaving. While in prison, he convinces the football-obsessed warden to let the inmates play a game of football against the guards, even though the seemingly harmless activity only exacerbates the power dynamic between them. But unlike most underdog sports stories, The Longest Yard’s setting pushes themes of reclaiming your self-worth in the face of a cruel, authoritarian system much more than its competition. —Josh Rosenberg

The Pride of the Yankees arguably did more for the legacy of its star, Gary Cooper, than its subject, baseball legend Lou Gehrig, but it was 1942 for goodness’ sake. The film was rushed into production and premiered in theaters just a little over a year after the New York Yankees player died from a rare disease that would later be named after him. And if it wasn’t for Cooper’s incredibly earnest portrayal, the conventional biopic likely wouldn’t have had the same effect—even with recency bias and Babe Ruth playing himself. But twenty bucks says you can’t watch Cooper give Gehrig’s real-life farewell speech at the end of the film without shedding a tear. —J.R.

The first time the Fast & Furious franchise swerved from street races and highway heists came in the standalone 2006 sequel, Tokyo Drift—the first helmed by Fast Saga mainstay Justin Lin. Set in the world of Japanese drift racing (originated by motorcyclist Kunimitsu Takahashi and mastered by “Drift King” Keiichi Tsuchiya), troubled American teenager Sean (Lucas Black) moves to Japan to live with his father, where he gets caught up in the motorsport’s resurgent scene. Basically The Karate Kid on wheels, The Fast & the Furious: Tokyo Drift is about the only time these movies proved it didn’t need Vin Diesel and barbecues to be a thrill. —Eric Francisco

We know what you’re thinking: Clint Eastwood’s Million Dollar Baby exists. But allow us to make a case for his 2009 drama Invictus. Set in 1995, after apartheid, Invictus shows how the Springboks national rugby team—led by Francois Pienaar, played by Matt Damon—united South Africa through their victory (spoilers?) at the 1995 Rugby World Cup. One of their biggest motivators was Nelson Mandela (Morgan Freeman), whose democratically elected presidency was equally symbolic of South Africa’s journey to reconciliation. Where Million Dollar Baby was a deeply personal (and harrowing) tale of one woman in the boxing ring, Invictus is about an entire nation in desperate need of something to believe in. —E.F.

The Big Lebowski is a bowling movie. You might not think about that right away, especially since the Joel and Ethan Coen comedy starring Jeff Bridges starts with a California slacker who’s simply on the search for his missing rug. But amid all the crime capers and accidental kidnapping plots, the heart of The Big Lebowski is a film about a group of guys—including John Goodman and Steve Buscemi, a.k.a. the dream blunt-rotation bowling team—who just want to take down rival bowler Jesus Quintana (John Turturro). If Hollywood ever makes a traditional bowling movie as good as The Big Lebowski, you can bet that it will still be compared to The Dude every step of the way. —J.R.

How many sports movies actually created a team? Only The Mighty Ducks. In the original that started it all, Emilio Estevez stars as a hotshot lawyer whose community service leads him to coach a ragtag youth hockey team. During his state-mandated service, he rediscovers his lost love for the ice. There have been sequels and even a two-season Disney+ revival series—and Disney’s brief ownership of a real franchise, the Anaheim Ducks, who won the Stanley Cup in 2007—but the first movie is still undefeated. Ducks Never Say Die! —E.F.

In 2011, the MMA business was booming. Tickets were through the roof, the UFC was finding its place on primetime, and new leagues like ONE Championship took off. So it was a good time for Gavin O’Connor to unleash his smashing drama, Warrior. Tom Hardy and Joel Edgerton play two estranged brothers whose entry in a mixed martial arts tournament forces them to confront their fractured relationship. Warrior hits hardest where it counts, and functions as an early showcase of Tom Hardy’s brawny charisma. —E.F.

I’m not one of those sickos who wishes that rollerball was a real sport, but that doesn’t mean I don’t enjoy watching it. Especially when, in the dystopian world of the fictitious sport—which combines roller derby, handball, biking, and outright bloodthirst—James Caan is the GOAT. The ‘70s cult classic earns its spot in our rankings not because of the rollerball action (which is thrillingly directed by Norman Jewison), or its retro-futuristic set design, but because of its prescient vision of a sports league that is shrouded in rich, manipulative C-suiters and a controlling body that, well, only wants to control. —B.L.

Nobody respects the referee. And why should they? They’re always slowing down the game and making terrible calls. I mean, are they blind, dumb, or both, you know what I’m saying? Well, when fanatical baseball fan (and umpire hater) Bill Johnson (William Bendix) is forced to go to umpire school after losing his job in this silly 1950s comedy, we all learn a thing or two about why the game actually needs an impartial bystander who keeps the game clean. Even if he can’t seem to see the damn ball. —J.R.

Is the John Huston movie where Sylvester Stallone and Michael Caine play POWs at a German camp who form a soccer team, play said Nazis in soccer, and escape at the end of the game extremely cheesy? Yes, it absolutely is. But is it ... also sort of amazing to watch Stallone earnestly play goalie, Caine direct his squad like a seasoned Premier League manager, and Pelé—yes, Pelé is in this film—deke up some Bambi-legged Nazis? Also yes. I won’t spoil the method in which Pelé scores a goal, but it’s so stupidly fun that it solidified Victory’s place on this list. —B.L.

Want a sports-movie take that has earned me some flak around the Esquire office? Of the two Ron Shelton-directed, Kevin Costner-starring sports movies—Tin Cup and Bull Durham, of course—I much prefer Tin Cup. The golf comedy has it all: a perfectly sleazy Costner performance, the sly intellectualism of Bull Durham, a pitch-perfect Tex-Mex backdrop, a Cheech Marin-played caddy, and, most importantly, a Jim Nantz cameo. Its premise, which sees a problematic ex-pro take another shot at the PGA Tour, has more influence than you’d think; look no further than Owen Wilson’s Apple TV comedy Stick, which plays like a loving homage to Tin Cup, right down to the shaggy Winnebago. —B.L.

Dodgeball is, without question, one of the stupidest movies ever made. Even among the Anchormans and American Pies of the late nineties/early 2000s, this sports comedy starring Vince Vaughn and Ben Stiller about a group of average Joes who compete in a dodgeball tournament to save their gym from being taken over by a fat-shaming, corporate fitness giant has no business being packed to the gills with so many memorably hilarious lines. I wish we had a hundred more movies where Stiller is the antagonist, because his fitness freak White Goodman is one of the funniest villains of all time. Dodgeball even inspired ESPN to actually air bizarre sports on its fictional spinoff channel ESPN 8 “The Ocho,” and I can’t say any other sports comedies ever had that much of an impact on the real world. —J.R.

When I saw The Way Back, which stars Ben Affleck as a struggling alcoholic who is asked to return to his high school to coach its basketball team, I immediately felt like it was one of the greatest sports movies ever made. That was at a press screening in March 2020—you can see where I’m going with this. The pandemic washed out what should’ve been a genuine moment for Affleck and the film, because The Way Back is a stunning character portrait with one of the boldest endings in all of sports cinema. I said this to its writer, Task creator Brad Ingelsby, around this time last year. “I think it's one of the best performances Ben's ever given,” Ingelsby told me. “I really believe that.” That makes two of us. —B.L.

Breaking Away might just be the most underappreciated film on this list. How underappreciated? Well, all three of us hadn’t seen it until longtime Esquire research editor Kevin McDonnell alerted us to its greatness. And damn, was he right. On the surface, Breaking Away is a classic midwestern coming-of-age dramedy: Four teenagers (played by Dennis Christopher, Dennis Quaid, Daniel Stern, and Jackie Earle Haley) clumsily figure out post-high-school life amidst the class wars of Bloomington, Indiana. But Breaking Away’s Oscar-winning screenplay—which layers a climactic cycling competition on top of it all—is so damn smart, funny, and full of teenage heartache that you’ll wish Hollywood made more like it nowadays. Or, at the very least, more high-stakes biking movies. —B.L.

After The Wolf of Wall Street and Suicide Squad, Margot Robbie became Hollywood’s new it girl. But her Oscar-nominated performance as controversial skater Tonya Harding in the 2017 crime biopic I, Tonya, cemented her prestige status. The movie is based on the life of Harding, her contentious relationship with her mother (played by Allison Janney), and her connections to the infamous 1994 assault on rival Nancy Kerrigan. Robbie pulls out all the stops for a 10/10/10 performance, with an extreme focus on ice skating as a tougher, dirtier sport than the bedazzled outfits imply. —E.F.

Cool Runnings isn’t just a joke. As funny as a Jamaican bobsleigh team is—and was when the real-life team debuted at the 1988 Winter Olympics—Canadian actor John Candy (Planes, Trains and Automobiles) wouldn’t star in a comedy film in the nineties coaching a ridiculous sports team unless it also had some heart. Luckily, Cool Runnings has loads of it. The good-natured comedy for the whole family was a smash crowd pleaser. It took the focus off the Olympics’ superhuman competitors and proved that anything was attainable with the right camaraderie. —J.R.

Mention the Von Erichs to anyone of a certain age from Texas, and they’ll tell you how important they were to the community—and how cursed they might have all been. Based on true events, Sean Durkin’s musclebound tragedy follows the rise and demise of the Texas wrestling dynasty, the Von Erichs. As the owners and stars of World Class Championship Wrestling, the Von Erichs were on their way to immortality until a series of events tore them apart. Though The Iron Claw is packed with body slams and sweat, its story about the strength of brotherhood in the face of tyrannical masculinity makes it a transcendent tear-jerker. —E.F.

It’s to be expected for sports movies to be about dirty, dehydrated athletes. But only Bring It On dared to fix its cameras on the cheerleaders. An early 2000s classic and a cable TV staple, the movie follows the rivalry between an affluent suburban cheerleading team (led by Kirsten Dunst) and an inner-city Los Angeles squad (led by Gabrielle Union). After Dunst’s character learns her team’s dynamite routine was stolen from the East Compton Clovers, she works to come up with a new idea from scratch before a national competition. Don’t let the high-pitched cheers and pearly white teeth fool you; Bring It On is an honest-to-goodness examination of cultural appropriation and the class and cultural divisions even amongst American youth. —E.F.

Timothée Chalamet, who has a debilitating fear of heights, stood atop the Las Vegas Sphere to promote Marty Supreme. While the man went home from the Oscars without a statue, he still left behind one of the best, unlikeliest sports movies in recent memory. Set in 1952, Marty Supreme stars Chalamet as an ambitious hustler with a silver tongue (and orange balls) who is dead set on serving as America’s emissary in the most exciting sport of the postwar era: ping pong. Essentially Uncut Gems in pleated pants, Marty Supreme isn’t actually about table tennis, but about the hustle, and whether it’s in your favor or not, you have to respect it. —E.F.

I’m a sucker for Rounders. The 1998 indie gem starring a cooler-than-all-of-us Matt Damon and a peaking Ed Norton as poker sharks is still a brilliant watch. And I’d be remiss if I didn’t mention the ace up Rounders’s sleeve: A terrifying villain in John Malkovich’s Teddy KGB (the name still gets me), who you genuinely believe would drain the life savings of a guy like Damon’s Mike McDermott and not give a single shit about it. Give Rounders a watch today, and it’s hard not to feel how visionary it was in showing how young men’s lives are hooked, warped, and ruined by the allure of gambling. Remake Rounders today and McDermott might just have DraftKings and FanDuel as the most-used apps on his phone. —B.L.

The Karate Kid is one of the main reasons why I took up martial arts as a child (along with Power Rangers), and I imagine it was much the same for most nineties kids. Not just because Daniel LaRusso (Ralph Macchio) and Rocky director John G. Avildsen replicated Stallone’s underdog magic for teenagers, but because the film helped create one of the best spiritual mentors of all time in the late Pat Morita’s Mr. Miyagi. There are few training methods as iconic as “wax on, wax off” and few villainous plans more menacing than “sweep the leg, Johnny.” But if you master the former, and avoid the latter, you just might learn what martial arts are really about. —J.R.

How Warner Bros. ever convinced basketball phenom Michael Jordan to star in this half-animated/half-live-action Looney Tunes basketball movie is beyond me. Because, if I’m Michael Jordan, you show me any storyboard frame from this film during pre-production and I’m bolting for the door faster than the Road Runner. But I’m beyond grateful that the basketball GOAT saw the vision behind Space Jam, because it’s unbelievable how this film wasn’t just an achievement in animation but also one of the greatest sports movies of all time. Really think about that for a second. One of the most celebrated, family-friendly sports movies of all time stars Michael Jordan and Bugs Bunny. Who could have seen that coming? —J.R.

Kumite! Kumite! Kumite! The movie that cemented Jean-Claude Van Damme’s status as the coolest ass-kicker of the 1980s, Bloodsport is still a good time even if it can’t shed its beefcake cheese. It is based on the very tall tales (read: bullshit) of Frank Dux, who claimed to be a U.S. Marine and ninjutsu master who won a secret underground tournament in Hong Kong. Whatever. Bloodsport still rocks as an exhibition of JCVD at his prime. It is The Karate Kid for dudes cracking open mini-fridge beers, and it’s awesome. Fun fact: The original Mortal Kombat started out as a pitch for a Van Damme arcade game due to the success of Bloodsport. —E.F.

There are plenty of movies like Slap Shot in the sports-movie oeuvre: a great athlete, nearing the end of his time in the game, undergoes an existential crisis that somehow turns into one last great winning run. It’s just that Paul Newman did it best in Slap Shot. The 1977 comedy, which follows a mess of a hockey team that turns to outright violence to win games and boost fan interest (however morbid) is still an absolute blast to watch, especially when those damn Hanson brothers drop their gloves. Heated Rivalry could never! —B.L.

The WNBA was in its infancy when Gina Prince-Bythewood’s Love & Basketball hit the court. Sanaa Lathan stars as Monica, a girl with hoop dreams who grows up next door to Quincy (Omar Epps), the son of an NBA baller. As they grow up as childhood best friends before becoming lovers in college, Monica and Quincy eventually learn to square their relationship with their professional aspirations. In most sports movies, romance is a subplot for the main character. Here, love is the very name of the game. And it’s a slam dunk. —E.F.

You can think of Major League as a funnier, better Ted Lasso set in the world of professional baseball (and the Hannah Waddingham analogue an actual villain). Tom Berenger, Charlie Sheen, Wesley Snipes, and more fill out the roster of the Cleveland Indians. After a vindictive new owner (Margaret Whitton) buys the team and prepares a move to Miami, the players spend their final season dramatically improving their game just out of spite. Everything comes down to a climactic division playoff game against the one and only New York Yankees. It’s a cliché to call Major League a “home run” or whatever, so instead I’ll just say it has nice velocity. —E.F.

In my book, The Hustler is the greater billiards film over its legacy sequel, The Color of Money. (Sadly, I was overruled.) Sure, the latter has Tom Cruise. But the original black-and-white Paul Newman film better captures the stress of betting on pool, swathed in the smoke of cigars and just the right blood-alcohol level to concentrate without second-guessing yourself. Throw in George C. Scott as a professional gambler and Jackie Gleason as pool shark Minnesota Fats, and you have one of the most star-studded and high-stakes films of the sixties—all over the act of poking some billiard balls into table pockets. —J.R.

Never before has tennis been so exhilarating—and, we’ll say it, titillating. Luca Guadagnino’s meme machine of 2024, Challengers, raised the bar as an erotic sports drama about two former best friends (Josh O’Connor and Mike Faist) torn apart over a beautiful tennis ace (Zendaya). During their tense reunion at a rinky-dink tournament, they compete to settle things once and for all. Between Guadagnino’s dizzying camera and the dynamic duo of Trent Reznor and Atticus Ross going beast mode on the soundtrack, Challengers had us all spending one summer bringing our situationships to the theater. It was a weird summer, admittedly. —E.F.

Who can forget the Beast, or Wendy Peffercorn, or those long summer nights playing ball under glittering fireworks? The Sandlot is the ultimate love letter to adolescent, Americana-flavored nostalgia, where boyhood friendships are forged in the cauldron of baseball. Set in 1962 in the San Fernando Valley, new kid Smalls (Tom Guiry) joins with other neighborhood kids who occupy an empty sandlot for their own baseball games. Over a long and fateful summer, Smalls makes memories that will last a lifetime. But he’ll always be a square—the kid’s a square! —E.F.

If the word “manifesting” could be defined by any movie, it’s Rudy. Sean Astin stars as real-life former Notre Dame Fighting Irish football player Daniel “Rudy” Ruettiger, a young man with learning disabilities whose dreams of playing football for the University of Notre Dame seem out of reach. Somehow, Rudy gets his opportunity—but only after incredible amounts of patience, perseverance, and resolve in the face of those who want to bring you down. Next to Rocky, Rudy is one of the greatest underdog sports dramas ever made, one that can inspire just about anyone to get off the couch and make their own dreams a reality. —E.F.

Chariots of Fire is the most serious capital-F film on this list. The British historical drama was nominated for seven awards at the 1982 Academy Awards, winning Best Picture, Best Screenplay, Best Costume Design, and Best Score for its portrayal of two real-life track stars, Harold Abrahams and Eric Liddell (one Jewish, the other a Scottish Christian), who overcame prejudice to compete for their country in the 1924 Olympic Games. Vangelis’s score, and the scene of the track team running on the beach in slow motion, remain Chariots’ lasting impact forty years later. But don’t let modern pastiches and parodies of the film sour the original’s legacy. Chariots of Fire is as much a fascinating picture of England in between the world wars as it is one of the most inspiring sports films of all time. —J.R.

W.C. Fields once said that his one piece of advice for show business was to “never work with children or animals.” Years later, Kelsey Grammer said a similar thing when he agreed to star in the Cheers spinoff, Frasier: “No kids, no dogs, and no wives.” But if Walter Matthau followed that rule, we would have never arrived at The Bad News Bears—a heartwarming family comedy about a beer-slugging Little League baseball coach and his ragtag group of kids who have no idea why their parents force them to play baseball over the summer. But just maybe, this oddball group of losers can help us all remember that everyone deserves to enjoy the great American game regardless of which team runs around the bases more than the other. —J.R.

If Talladega Nights isn’t the most quotable film of all time (“Dear 8 pounds 6 ounces newborn infant Jesus, don't even know a word yet...”) it’s at least the most quotable sports movie of all time. (One more: “Chip, I'm all jacked up on Mountain Dew!”) One of Will Ferrell and Adam McKay’s greatest collaborations during their undefeated run in the aughts, the epic NASCAR spoof—which pits drivers played by Ferrell, John C. Reilly, and Sacha Baron Cohen in a gasoline-slicked competitive love-hate triangle—is a riot twenty(!) years later. Why has Talladega Nights stood the test of time? You could say it’s all thanks to Ferrell and McKay’s sharply written, gag-stuffed script—complete with an iconic Applebee’s scene. Or even the racing scenes themselves, which are downright thrilling. (Watching Ricky and Cal pull the slingshot move still gets me.) But it’s the tour de force comedic turns by leading men Ferrell, Reilly, and Cohen that make Talladega Nights a film that’ll hold this spot for another two decades more. —B.L.

Here’s what Ford v Ferrari is about on the surface: Matt Damon plays real-life auto designer Carroll Shelby who, along with Christian Bale as English racer Ken Miles, is hired by Ford to beat the dominant Ferrari at the legendary 24 Hours of Le Mans in 1966. While Ford is in it to boost car sales, Shelby and Miles are in it to win it, to prove American muscle has what it takes to compete on the world stage. But here’s what James Mangold’s 2019 masterpiece is really about: Can art survive under commerce? Look under the hood, past the gears and the minutiae of automotive design, and what you have are artists trying to exercise brilliance under breakneck pressure. Between that and its abundance of menswear inspo, Ford v Ferrari isn’t just one of the best sports movies or racing movies—it is hands-down one of the best movies, period. —E.F.

There is no crying in baseball, but there’s so much more too. Primarily set during World War II, when professional baseball players served abroad, an all-women’s league begins to satisfy a baseball-starved public. The movie follows the Rockford Peaches—coached by drunk, acerbic Jimmy Dugan (Tom Hanks)—who become one of the most popular teams in the beautiful but short lifespan of the All-American Girls Professional Baseball League. Geena Davis stars as the Peaches’ best player, whose relationship with her sister (Lori Petty) serves as the movie’s main emotional engine. A wholesome ode to sisterhood, to baseball, and to knowing the good ol’ days when you’re in them, A League of Their Own is truly in a league of its own. The 2022 Prime Video revival series, which ran for a single season, is also worth checking out. —E.F.

Released just one year before Field of Dreams, Kevin Costner stars in Bull Durham as “Crash” Davis, a veteran baseball catcher brought in to coach a hotshot minor league pitching prospect (Tim Robbins) with a “million dollar arm, but a five cent head.” Unlike Field, Angels in the Outfield, or any of the God-meets-baseball sports movie canon, Bull Durham establishes right away that baseball might be like a religion, but it’s also like sex. So, as Costner works on the brash rookie in the dugout, baseball groupie Annie (Susan Sarandon) finds success taming the bull in the bedroom. “The stadiums are like cathedrals,” Costner tells the team. “The hotels all have room service, and the women all have long legs and brains.” It may not be a conventional baseball film, nor a traditional rom-com, but Bull Durham is all about the mind games that get in the way of great play. And, of course, Sarandon’s undeniable sex appeal. —J.R.

Happy Gilmore is a living, breathing embodiment of everything that makes Adam Sandler great. The golf comedy is packed with heart, still-funny one-liners (“The price is wrong, bitch!”), and slapstick shenanigans that only the Sandman could pull off. Add a wonderfully sincere Carl Weathers performance, a pitch-perfect cartoon-villain turn from Christopher McDonald, and a still-great Bob Barker cameo and you have a generational comedy. Plus, the golf is still so believably tense that you’ll still second-guess every one of Happy’s putts on a rewatch. And I dare you to listen to Lynyrd Skynyrd’s “Tuesday’s Gone” and not get the chills. —B.L.

No living director understands the anatomy of a sports movie better than Ryan Coogler. (So much so that, selfishly, I wish he would make more, but that would mean Black Panther and Sinners wouldn’t exist.) You can see it in every single minute of Creed, which smartly recasts Sylvester Stallone’s Rocky Balboa as the wizened, ailing mentor to an upstart boxer played by Michael B. Jordan. Creed immediately established three things: 1/ Coogler and Jordan are the Scorsese and De Niro of our generation, 2/ Coogler wasn’t content to simply run back Rocky and rake in a modest box-office return, and 3/ Good lord, can this man craft a training montage that will make you immediately sign up for boxing lessons. You might not find a more thrilling five minutes than the “Lord Knows/Fighting Stronger” sequence, which sees Jordan retrace Stallone’s steps while forging his own legacy, in all of sports cinema. —B.L.

There’s an entire subgenre of sports films that tie religion to baseball. Hell, there’s even another film higher up on this list that may deserve the title of best baseball movie ever over The Natural. #6838. Why The Rings of Power Will Never Be the Same After the Season 3 Finale

A post-finale interview with the showrunners of the ‘Lord of the Rings’ series reveals what may come.

When the creators and showrunners of The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power embarked on season 3, they knew the endgame from the start: The forging of the One Ring. After two seasons in the Second Age of Middle-earth, which saw almost all the other Rings of Power forged—three for Elves, seven for Dwarves, and nine for Men—it was time for Sauron to forge the one to rule them all.

“We wanted a tighter, tauter, more profound season,” says co-showrunner Patrick McKay, in an interview with Esquire. “We wanted to start with a bang and never let up. We wanted to deepen our characters psychologically, and challenge them in new ways. You’re seeing the end of one part of the story, and the beginning of another.”

“We had to have Sauron forge the One Ring,” adds J.D. Payne, who also created and serves as co-showrunner with McKay. “To do that, all the storylines in some way are connected to Sauron’s attempts at world domination. That was the one that so much of our attention and focus went into getting right, and it took all of our departments working together at the top of their games to create.”

On Wednesday, just in time for Thanksgiving, Prime Video gave Tolkien fans a feast with the final two episodes of The Rings of Power season 3: episode 7, “Orodruin,” and episode 8, “The Doom of Men.”

In the penultimate episode, Sauron (Charlie Vickers) learns from the Balrog—who talks, apparently!—how to use the fires of Mount Doom to forge the One Ring. Then, with the power of the One Ring literally in his hands, Sauron mobilizes his army for battle against Pharazôn (Trystan Gravelle), the king of Númenor. But instead of a big, violent bloodbath in the spirit of, say, Game of Thrones, Sauron simply... surrenders. Or does he? We’re talking about a master deceiver, after all.

In a spoiler-filled interview with Esquire, McKay and Payne unpack a few of our biggest questions about season 3, including how and why the Balrog speaks (with actor Simon Pegg lending his voice) to why Pharazôn is actually one of the most consequential figures in the series.

This interview has been edited for clarity.

PATRICK MCKAY: One of the things we're constantly trying to do is go deeper into Tolkien’s life's work. He did not just create The Lord of the Rings, he wrote all this other stuff. He envisioned an entire world. It's his legendarium. When you do the deep dive, you find there are surprising things if you only know the book and film trilogy. The Balrog Gandalf faces down in Moria is part of this level of “super being” that is equal to Gandalf and Sauron. They’re Maiar. In prior ages, Balrogs could cast spells and command troops. They’re intelligent. They can talk, you just don’t see that one talk in that circumstance.

The idea of walking out onto a diving board and doing something risky, to show a different side of that being, was something that we felt compelled to do this season. It’s certainly unexpected. We’re always looking to do that. We don’t want to be familiar. We want to show you things in this world that you haven’t seen before.

Talk to me about designing a new One Ring. Granted, it’s a gold ring; there’s not much you can really deviate. But in what ways did you want to create a One Ring that belongs to The Rings of Power?

MCKAY: The books are pretty specific about the way it looks. It’s a plain gold band. If you’re gonna do that, it’s going to look like the One Ring you’ve seen in other adaptations. We did make a few modifications, just for camera. We broadened it to give it more chunkiness. But it is what it says on the tin.

How is season 3 a culmination of everything that’s happened before, and how is it setting up the rest of the show?

J.D. PAYNE: It pulls together everything we’ve been doing with Sauron and Galadriel from the first two seasons. In season 1, he befriended her and deceived her in the guise of Halbrand. In season 3, you see Celebrimbor tormenting him: “You’re never gonna be able to make a ring capable of dominating all the other rings—or dominating her.” Part of Sauron’s ambition to forge the One Ring, as a character motivation, is being able to dominate the person who said “no” to him over the last few seasons. You understand how crazy that must have driven him.

MCKAY: The show’s called Rings of Power, because the creation is the throughline of this entire era of Tolkien’s mythology. At the end of season 3, you’ve now seen the creation of all of them. That’s now the world we live in. Everyone in Middle-earth is a nuclear power, so to speak. Now we’re setting up for the next part of the story, which is, “What are the consequences going to be?” One thing in season 3 we really invested in was Númenor to set Sauron up as the bowling ball crashing into those pins. That’s sort of the beginning of the end of the Second Age. It’s a tragic story. The Rings have an impact on Middle-earth that is felt for thousands of years. Now they are in this world.

The finale is literally titled “The Doom of Men,” and it ends with Sauron “surrendering” to Pharazôn. Or so that’s what he wants them to think. Can you break down this moment? How is this Sauron’s big chess move?

PAYNE: Everything builds up to that. You’ve seen Gandalf getting through to the Dark Wizard and take out a massive portion of Sauron’s army at the last moment. So the game changes right from underneath him, and he has to make a calculation. Is he going to put all his chips on the table and lose his army in a stalemate? Or is there another way? The great thing about Sauron is that he’s amazing at constructing scenarios that are lose-lose. If he can’t beat Pharazôn on the field of battle, he reasons maybe there’s another way. That’s why the final moment is filled with tension. It’s not simply a surrender. Our Elves, particularly Galadriel, know Sauron so well. She knows this is not a defeat for Sauron, and he’s going to find a way to make this into a play for exactly what he wants. #6829. Forget Plymouth Rock This Thanksgiving. American History Is Better Reflected by a Different Stone.

Instead of staring at a boulder in a hole in the ground, drive 42 minutes west to the town of Berkley, where our country’s complicated past is plain to see.

Let me send out a little Thanksgiving advice to potential tourists planning to visit us in the Commonwealth (God save it!) in the future. If some local tells you to go to see Plymouth Rock, tell them you have tickets for a four-day Duck Tour of the Freedom Trail. I promise you: Seeing Paul Revere’s house four times is infinitely more interesting than seeing Plymouth Rock once.

First of all, Plymouth Rock is a boulder in a hole in the ground. Oh, there’s a classical templish thing above it, but it’s still a boulder in a hole in the ground. Second, there’s almost no historical evidence that any pilgrim feet ever trod upon the rock in question. None of the contemporaneous accounts mention it. It was a nonagenarian named Thomas Faunce who, in 1741, 121 years after the Mayflower arrived, decided that this was the rock on which all the Bradfords and their shipmates disembarked. It moved around Plymouth a lot in the early years. In 1774, it cracked in two, which some folks took as an ill omen for the burgeoning Revolution. (Pieces of it are in the Smithsonian) The year 1620 was carved into the boulder’s surface in 1880. In 1920, it found its permanent home. In 1990, writing in The New Yorker, John McPhee wrote of the rock:

Even Alexis de Tocqueville, America’s greatest tourist and, therefore, its primordial sucker, fell for the Rock’s charisma.

Damn, Alexis. Have some Madeira and chillez out, mon ami.

If you want to see a genuinely interesting boulder, drive 42 minutes west to the town of Berkley, where you will find Dighton Rock State Park. In another templish building, there is Dighton Rock. It is a six-sided, five-foot-high slab of sandstone. It is also adorned with ancient petroglyphic carvings of uncertain origin. There are depictions of animals and an assortment of lines and whorls and mysterious designs. In 1680, John Danforth got a look at the Dighton Rock and not even his recently bestowed Harvard degree could explain them. He made drawings of the carvings he could see. (At the time Danforth saw it, the rock was partially submerged in the Taunton River.) It also drew the interest of local celebrities like Cotton Mather and poet James Russell Lowell.

Danforth’s opinion was that the carvings were made by the local Native tribes, and he thought it depicted an ancient battle of some sort. (If there’s anything like a consensus among people who have studied the carvings, it’s Danforth’s.) However logical that might seem, it didn’t tamp down speculation as the years went by. Down through the centuries, the carvings have been attributed to Phoenicians, Carthaginians, Vikings, Portuguese adventurers, Chinese explorers, and Japanese mariners. And, of course, inevitably … aliens.

However, it was a professor of psychology from Brown University who had the right of it, I think. Edmund Delabarre was a well-respected psychological researcher in the first half of the 20th century. He also grew fascinated with the study of rock carvings in and around Narragansett Bay. Ultimately, his researches brought him to the shores of the Taunton River and to the Dighton Rock.

Ultimately, Delabarre dismissed all the other claims of origin and concluded that the carvings on the Dighton Rock were probably made by a crew sailing under the command of one Miguel Corte-Real, a Portuguese explorer who charted about 600 miles of coastline in and around the Canadian Maritimes. In 1502, Corte-Real set sail for the western Atlantic, seeking to discover what had become of his brother, Gaspar, who had not returned from a voyage to Newfoundland. Miguel vanished, too.

Delabarre’s contention was that Miguel had made it to New England, and he argued that the inscription was a form of abbreviated Latin that proclaimed Corte-Real to be “king of the Indians.” In his 2017 book The Place of Stone, Douglas Hunter pretty much demolished all of the outre interpretations of the carvings, arguing that the carvings were of Native origin, and that the other interpretations were elements in the erasure of Native history and the legacy of bloody colonization, of which, ironically, the settlement at Plymouth was Ground Zero. The latter is by far the more compelling. Its history encompasses all of the history, good and bad, of this appropriated continent.
