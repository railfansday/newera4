#7060. This London Townhouse Was Designed for the Holidays

Miles Redd and David Kaihoi created the ultimate English Christmas retreat for longtime clients—cozy, festive, and swathed in reds and greens.

Above: In the living room, the seating is by Luther Quintana with Samuel & Sons fringe. The wallcovering is by Iksel, and the cocktail table has a Fornasetti top.

Some homes are for summers by the water, others for weekends on the slopes. When Krystyna Houser set about redecorating her family’s London townhouse, she was thinking of Christmas. “We spend every Christmas there,” she says. At this house, that meant more than just putting up a tree.

Located near Richmond Park, in southwestern London, the 19th-century townhouse has been in Houser’s family for more than 20 years. It was always a place to return to while Houser, founder of the nutritional startup mBIOTA Elemental, and her husband Dan, a co-founder of Rockstar Games, were raising their three children in New York City. Dan had grown up nearby, and the ample greenspace that gives the area its name made it an appealing place to visit with a young family. The interiors, however, had never quite received their due.

Houser turned to Miles Redd and David Kaihoi, the partners behind the New York–based firm Redd Kaihoi. She had known Redd since 2012, when she hired him to decorate a Brooklyn Heights townhouse, and the two became friends. They have since worked on multiple houses together. For London she trusted the designers to craft the warm and celebratory atmosphere she craved. “I wanted it to be equally elegant, cozy, and family-oriented,” she says.

Red and green were a natural starting point, as green already had the family’s allegiance. “It’s one of their favorite colors,” Redd says. “So we leaned into that a lot.” Verdant hues appear in velvet, felt, and marble throughout, while the entrance hall is dressed in crimson. These were holiday colors with no intention of coming down in January, and Redd was going for sophistication rather than holiday kitsch. “I wanted romantic 19th-century elegance, the feeling that they’re stepping into a Merchant Ivory movie.”

This approach suits a firm known for exuberant color and layered furnishings. Redd brings a sense of theater to traditional decorating, drawing on the work of such legendary designers as Billy Baldwin, Albert Hadley, and John Fowler and mixing lacquer, chintz, and bold geometric patterns. Before opening his practice in 1998, he trained with antiques dealer John Rosselli and decorator Bunny Williams. Kaihoi, a sculptor, joined the ELLE Decor A-List firm in 2008 and became a partner in 2019. Together the duo give grand rooms an ease and wit that keep formality in check.

Houser considers “interior designer” an insufficient description for Redd and Kaihoi. “I feel like they are set curators,” she says. She gave them considerable freedom, occasionally inviting them to surprise her with potential purchases. “I don’t think it’s my place to hinder creative expression, and, as a result, the rooms are just magical.”

The house’s existing architecture gave that freedom some useful structure. “It had great English bones,” Redd says. He retained the original living room mantelpiece, library doors, and front door, while upstairs he and Kaihoi worked to restore character lost to earlier alterations that had stripped away 19th-century details. An excavation created additional basement space for a screening room and a ping-pong area, giving the family more places to gather and play.

From first visit to completion, the project took the better part of an unhurried decade, with pauses for permits, the Covid pandemic, and other commitments along the way. All the while Redd and Kaihoi kept shopping at auctions, antiques stores, and fairs, which ensured that the rooms would have an accumulated character. The designers looked to the guards at Buckingham Palace for the entrance hall and main staircase, borrowing the red and gold of their uniforms for its matka silk walls trimmed with gold braiding. Beyond the hall, the green velvet–lined library opens into a living room papered to resemble a coromandel screen.

Upstairs, the primary bedroom and a sitting area occupy a long, narrow space. Green felt screen dividers give the bed its own area in one corner without a wall breaking up the suite. “It’s all sort of one big bedsit, which is a very English notion,” Redd says. A small painting of a parrot on one of the screens keeps Houser company at her bedside.

The couple wanted their suite to feel like a luxurious hotel—inviting places to read and work, a comfortable bed, and a generously scaled bathroom—without sacrificing a sense of home. “You’ve ruined hotels for us,” Redd recalls Houser telling him of their previous work.

Step Inside the Ultimate Christmas Retreat

But Houser’s favorite rooms are the ones that bring everyone together: the kitchen and the adjoining striped family room, which open onto the garden. It’s here where everyone gathers for meals and to relax. The connected spaces provide separate places to eat, play games, and watch television, allowing the family to spend time together even when everyone is doing something different.

“Their design helps bring us together,” Houser says. “And that’s what it is. It’s a family home.”

#7035. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. #7013. What to Eat for Healthier Skin and Slower Aging

A dietitian breaks down the foods, nutrients, and habits that may support skin from the inside out.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

On the quest to age smarter and slower, there’s a lot that your skin care can do. But perhaps one of the greatest contributions lies in the foods you consistently eat. Of course, diet and exercise are the pillars of good health, proper body function, and longevity, but a recent study done by scientists at the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases (DZNE) also reveals that 10 specific diets are linked to slower aging. Experts unpack the findings for ELLE and break down which foods you should incorporate or ditch from your diet to help improve skin health.

To understand the connection between diet and skin health, Caitlin Hosmer, MS, RD, LDN, a registered dietitian and co-founder of MenoPantry, says it’s important to define the notion of slowing down aging. She points out that this particular DZNE study didn’t look at wrinkles or how old people feel. “It looked at biological aging, measured with epigenetic clocks. These read chemical marks on our DNA (methylation) that shift as our cells age. In about 7,500 adults, the researchers found that following any of 10 healthy eating patterns more closely was associated with slower biological aging,” Hosmer explains. The 10 eating patterns or diets included in the study results are Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, MIND, and plant-based. Hosmer says that while the effect was modest, this kind of study shows an association, not causation—proof that diet is the reason for the positive outcome—so it is still a meaningful signal.

Additionally, Mark Gilbert, nutritionist from The 1:1 Diet by Cambridge Weight Plan, tells ELLE that no single food has been satisfactorily studied and shown to be likely, on its own, to slow aging. “Of all the predictors of a long, healthy life, two of the best, which can be improved with diet, are muscle mass and strength, but this leads to a controversial conclusion,” says Gilbert. “The best nutritional intervention to increase muscle and strength is eating a high-protein diet. However, many scientists argue that high-protein diets have the opposite effect on longevity. So, if we can’t even agree on that, you can see that science has a long way to go to try and figure this out.”

One stat Hosmer found particularly striking was that over 70 percent of the biological pathways affected were the same across all ten diets. “I interpret that to mean that no single superfood is doing the work, and it affirms that [a combination of] food, diet, and nutrition impacts us on the cellular level. The foods these patterns share are what matter,” says Hosmer, who, along with Gilbert, breaks down the standouts:

Alongside individual foods, Hosmer says certain diet components have the greatest impact on aging. For starters, protein is a must. “As we age, our muscles respond less efficiently to the protein we eat. This is called anabolic resistance, and in women, the drop in estrogen makes it worse,” she explains. Building and retaining muscle isn’t only about strength; Hosmer says it’s our largest site for handling blood sugar and a strong predictor of independence later in life. “Many women eat less protein just when they need more.”

Gilbert adds that while protein is an important part of a balanced, longevity diet, there are some considerations to keep in mind before adding it to your meal. “I believe that because muscle and strength are so strongly associated with longevity and that protein, particularly later in life, supports them,” he says. The caveat, however, is that there is a potential mechanism (which has to do with the amino acid methionine) whereby too much protein could have negative effects. “Many scientists argue that, because in insects and some other animals, less protein along with calorie restriction increases lifespan, humans would similarly benefit. Also, some cite the so-called ‘Blue Zones’, alleged to show that plant-based, low-protein diets are superior. In my opinion, all of these have been shown to be invalid,” says Gilbert.

Fiber is another essential nutrient that you’ll reap from plant foods and mushrooms. Meat, fish, dairy, and eggs contain no fiber, and because our enzymes can’t break them down, Hosmer says that fiber travels intact to the large intestine to assist. Once there, fermentable fibers become food for the gut microbiome; other fibers mainly add bulk and keep things moving. “As gut bacteria ferment fiber, they make short-chain fatty acids, which nourish the cells lining the gut, help keep that barrier strong, and keep inflammation in check. Fiber also steadies blood sugar and helps the body excrete estrogen the liver has already processed, supporting healthy hormone balance. Yet more than 90 percent of American women don’t reach the recommended 21 to 25 grams a day,” Hosmer explains.

Plant diversity matters, too, and not just quantity. Hosmer points to research from the American Gut Project that found people who ate 30 or more different kinds of plant foods a week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those who ate 10 or fewer. “That’s because each plant brings its own mix of fibers and polyphenols, and some bacteria have preferences, so a varied diet feeds a broader community of microbes. A broader community does more jobs. It makes short-chain fatty acids that protect the gut lining, helps regulate immunity and inflammation, turns plant compounds into forms the body can use, and even helps process estrogen.” The variety of plants Hosmer adds seems to matter more than any single “perfect” plant.

You’ve likely heard of healthy fats, meaning omega-3s from fish and the polyphenol-rich fat in olive oil. Adding these to your diet can support cell membranes and help settle inflammation.

Also on the list for Gilbert is vitamin D, which he says studies have shown to be inversely related to the risk of mortality. “This does not prove that low vitamin D is the cause, but it is compelling evidence. Although some results are controversial, low [vitamin D] levels have been linked to all-cause, cardiovascular, cancer, and infection-related mortality,” he adds. Vitamin D is intimately involved in immunity, too. It protects T-cell immune response.

Although Gilbert says, “The only thing all good nutritionists agree on is that there is no one ‘best’ diet or ‘best’ food,” he warns that for most people, minimizing the intake of sugar and refined, starchy grains and increasing protein intake is, in my opinion and experience, the best approach, but, again, different people can thrive on different diets. “As in the examples I gave, many of these foods contain phytochemicals—plant chemicals like polyphenols, flavanols, cyanidins, and others – which affect disease risk or support systems in the body that are likely to serve you well in old age.”

Hosmer notes several other red flags as contributing to unhealthy skin: Diets dominated by refined carbohydrates and added sugars, heavily processed meat, and regular alcohol. “Each of these drives the chronic, low-grade inflammation researchers call “inflammaging,” and blood-sugar instability.”

Having seen so many gaps in these important nutrients in her practice, and understanding how women’s bodies change in midlife, led Hosmer and her team to build Menomix at MenoPantry. “One serving adds meaningful protein along with ground flaxseed and psyllium fiber, plus a range of plant ingredients. It’s made to help close the protein, fiber, and plant-variety gaps that so many women are carrying,” she adds.

The information can be overwhelming, but instead of purging everything in your fridge, Hosmer suggests first focusing on adding good foods instead of restricting less healthful options. “In my experience, that’s more sustainable, both because it feels less like deprivation and because the “good” foods can displace the ones that speed aging up instead of slowing it down. You don’t need to pick the “right” diet and follow it perfectly.”

She highlights that the DZNE study found few people scored well on all ten diets at once, yet better adherence to any of them helped. What matters is the common ground. Mediterranean, Nordic, DASH, MIND, and plant-based diets vary on the plate, but they have a common core: more fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, and less red and processed meat, and added sugar. “That shared core is where I’d start,” says Hosmer, offering this meal planning guide:

Increase your familiarity with, and use of, plant-based proteins. Plant-based proteins offer an excellent package of beneficial nutrients, including fiber (not found in animal products). If you aren’t already familiar, learn how to include whole soy foods (tofu, tempeh, edamame) in your diet; add beans and peas and lentils to dishes here and there. Nuts and seeds and nut and seed butters count here also.

Build meals around vegetables and fruit. Fill half your plate with them, and choose deep colors like leafy greens, berries, beets, and cabbage-family vegetables. Those colors come from polyphenols, plant compounds that act as antioxidants and feed beneficial gut bacteria.

Make your grains whole and varied. Oats, rye, barley, brown rice, and whole-wheat bread are staples of the Nordic and DASH patterns, the two most consistently linked to slower aging in this study. Try a grain you haven’t eaten before—quinoa? Farro? Millet or teff? They are all cooked the same way—in boiling water, just with different grain-to-water ratios. Note: raise fiber gradually, with plenty of water, so your gut has time to adjust.

Count your plants, not your calories. Aim toward 30 different kinds of plant foods a week. Veggies, fruits, nuts, seeds, whole grains, beans, peas, herbs, and spices all count. A homemade trail mix, an interesting salad, or a smoothie with a handful of fruit and veggies can add three or four at once.

Choose your fats deliberately. Most of these patterns favor unsaturated fats over saturated ones. Use extra-virgin olive oil as your everyday fat, and eat oily fish two or three times a week.

Keep red and processed meat occasional, and keep added sugar low. Every one of these diets limits them.

Quality matters. Note that “plant-based” isn’t automatically healthy. A version of the plant-based diet built on refined grains, sweets, and sugary drinks tended to be associated with faster aging, as did a pro-inflammatory eating pattern overall. Food quality matters, not just food category.

Diet is the foundation the other efforts rest on. Collagen supplements and skin care can help, but they can’t make up for a body that lacks what it needs to build and protect tissue. According to Gilbert, collagen supplements contain very high levels of the amino acid glycine, which he says can balance the potential harms of proteins that are high in methionine.

Hosmer further explains that collagen peptides break down during digestion into amino acids and small peptides. Your body then decides where to use them. “To actually build collagen, it needs enough total protein, plus vitamin C. Vitamin C is a required helper for the enzymes that stabilize the collagen structure. It also needs minerals like zinc and copper. So a collagen supplement taken on top of a protein-poor, micronutrient-poor diet is not going to be optimized,” Hosmer says. “Collagen is also an incomplete protein: it’s low in leucine and lacks tryptophan entirely. It can add to your protein intake, but it isn’t a substitute for complete proteins when it comes to muscle.”

On the surface, what we eat affects the skin from the inside. Hosmer notes that polyphenol-rich foods and healthy fats support the skin’s own defenses against oxidative stress, and high blood sugar leads to glycation, which stiffens collagen fibers. The gut matters here, too. “Researchers call the connection the gut–skin axis. When the gut lining is healthy, and the microbiome is well fed, inflammation throughout the body tends to stay lower, and the skin reflects that. External products can only do so much if we aren’t supporting our skin from the inside,” Hosmer says.

Diet plays a major role in overall skin health and slower aging, but Hosmer argues that it’s not the full picture. To optimize more youthful-looking skin, consider other factors that may be equally as important as diet.

#7005. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

#6999. Why Am I Still Mad at My Partner After Makeup Sex?

There are some things even an orgasm can’t fix.

Arguments with your partner typically follow a general sequence of events: Something upsets you. You bring it up to your partner. You discuss (or argue). You go back and forth. You eventually work it out. You have wonderful makeup sex. And then—if the argument wasn’t super serious and you were able to hear each other out—you move on (give or take a few “I find it funny how...” monologues and a venting Facetime session with your best friend).

But what if after your conversation and orgasm, you find yourself still upset with your partner? What if, even after putting the argument in the “done” pile, you feel lingering emotions of pain and anger?

While I’m a firm believer that makeup sex is the best stamp of completion for every argument, this isn’t always the case for every relationship. Sometimes you don’t realize that the argument hurt you deeper than you realized—and even a hot and heavy session can’t mask it.

“While some couples experience sex as a bonding and often reparative experience, it may not solve the underlying process of disconnection or conflict in a relationship,” licensed marriage and family therapist Molly Mindel tells Cosmopolitan.

She explains that while sex can be an incredibly powerful tool for connectedness and regulation, it can’t repair bigger issues that may be at play—no matter how good the sex is.

You typically only have makeup sex... when you think you’re making up with your partner. And for many of us, we genuinely believe that’s the case when it happens. But sometimes, we get so caught up in the emotions and connectedness of the sex that we realize we actually don’t feel fully moved on from the argument.

Mandel explains that there are both physiological and psychological reasons sex can make us feel temporarily closer to our partner and less upset about whatever we were arguing about. .

“For one, sex temporarily changes our neurochemical and emotional states,” she says. She explains that oxytocin and dopamine are released during sex, which can make us momentarily feel really good and satisfied. But these chemicals don’t change our foundational relationship beliefs, unmet needs, or feelings about an argument.

“Sex can also regulate ‘attachment distress,’ meaning we get very close and feel very secure temporarily to our partner (maybe even in ways we don't with them outside of a sexual interaction),” she explains. “But after sex is finished and we get back to our relationship, that sense of feeling understood or cared for or connected to can fade, which can cause emotional distress and alter our behavior in the relationship, bringing us right back to our regular cycle of conflict.”

Think of post-nut clarity. While we get swept up in our horniness, makeup sex is simply a band-aid that will typically fall off once the sex is over. And even mind-blowing sex is not enough to keep the illusion going.

It’s not just about the makeup sex. The makeup sex represents the finishing of an argument. And because you already talked about it (and f*cked about it) you should be completely over it, right?

Well, not necessarily. You shouldn’t feel guilty about having unresolved feelings after an argument, especially if you didn’t feel like all of your concerns were addressed.

“Lingering anger, resentment, and hurt days or weeks after an argument with your partner can be a normal part of conflict, and aren't always things we need to rush to ‘solve,’” Mandel says.

Mandel explains that it’s common for people to want to leave conflicts with a sense of resolve—“almost as if we tied a nice bow on the conversation.” But our arguments, like our relationships, are never perfect. It’s less important that an argument is resolved in a certain time period and more that both you and your partner feel heard, respected, and valued—no matter how long it takes.

So if you feel mad even post-makeup sex, that’s totally normal.

“It's worth reflecting on whether the lingering hurt is discomfort from the argument and disconnection, or if there's genuine lack of resolve around a serious issue or emotion needing attention,” Mandel suggests.

If the argument represents a larger pattern in the relationship, this may be the reason you still feel distress even after you “talked it out.” You may have discussed the specific instance, but still need to address the bigger issue at hand. Even if the argument was about something singular, sometimes you just feel like you haven’t fully said your piece.

”In relationships, conflicts are messy, complicated, and cannot be solved in a matter of moments,” Mandel says. “Usually, there is something left unsaid, a feeling that was left unaddressed, or one that comes up later, which can be uncomfortable and sometimes painful.”

If your makeup sex didn’t seem to tie the argument together, you may be worried that nothing will. Don’t panic. Just because an argument leaves some lasting discomfort doesn’t mean that your relationship is doomed—it just means that you need to find a different method to approach the disagreement.

“Sometimes the powerful and healthy thing to do is to say, ‘We may not resolve this entirely’ and ‘Can we learn to be okay with that open ended-ness together for now?’”

And from there, you have to work with your partner to figure out how to move forward. Mandel suggests revisiting the conversation when both partners are calm and emotionally available. This way, she says, both parties will be able to approach the issue with curiosity rather than blame.

Like any argument, it’s going to take a lot of communication and then checking in after the fact. Just keep reminding yourself that there actually is no linear path to resolving conflict. And if both people prioritize each other during a discussion, there’s almost always a way through—even if it does take a few more conversations than expected.

The good news, though? Once the argument really is resolved, and you leave feeling heard and attended to, there’s always more makeup sex to look forward to.

#6975. This St. Bart’s House Was Designed to Look Nothing Like Its Neighbors

Built for maximum family time, a converted boutique hotel puts the emphasis on entertaining, hanging out, and blending into the surroundings.

They can’t have enough bedrooms.”

Situated on a steep hillside above one of St. Bart’s famously pristine white sand beaches, the house had initially been a boutique hotel. The plan was to turn it into a retreat for themselves and their adult children and grandchildren. The family are longtime visitors to the island—“the daughter told me it was her ‘happy place,’” says Ries, lead designer of the New York-based interior design firm Ries Hayes—and knew exactly what they wanted. Not the white box look popular with luxury new builds on the island, but instead something reminiscent of the “old St. Bart’s,” says East Hampton-based landscape designer Edwina von Gal, who, like Ries, has collaborated with the clients on other projects.

A Caribbean island is a challenging place to work under any circumstances. Everything has to be brought in from far away, including all the plants, which came by barge from Miami. St. Bart’s in particular requires anyone building to work with a local architect and engineer. In their initial proposal, this French team defaulted to that “slick and modernist” look, as von Gal describes it, with the oversized bedrooms and white color scheme that dominates much of the island’s luxury housing stock.

The design team pushed back—asking for a lot of bedrooms (eight) and generous living spaces. They also had strong opinions on the building materials. Given the island’s climate, the humid, salt-soaked air can be hard on everything. “If we did white paint, which is prominent throughout St. Bart's, it’s constant maintenance, constant painting,” says Ries. The design team insisted on cladding the buildings in Ipe, the durable tropical hardwood, which wears well. It mystified the local architects and set the complex apart from its neighbors. It also makes the buildings disappear into the greenery of the hillside.

But the architects quickly caught on to the new plans, and von Gal is eager to praise their technical accomplishments. Regulations require properties to conserve rainwater, so there are cisterns beneath the buildings, plus technology infrastructure and laundry facilities. “Working on the hill is so complex,” she says. “It's such a tight little package of technology and function. What they did was amazing.”

The team decided to reconfigure the property as a series of pavilions that face the view of the ocean and surrounding hills. The main entry is at the top of the steep hill, along with parking. The next level down is for family bedrooms; below that is a primary suite and study; and finally the fourth level is the main living space, including a playroom. “We made sure this playroom was front and center,” says Ries. “My client loves her grandkids.”

The playroom and study have built-in daybeds that can be used as spare guest rooms in a pinch. But the clients did get their eight bedrooms. “We know how to make compact bedrooms and make them very efficient, make them very livable, which allows us to fit in a lot of space for a lot of guests, and then that puts a lot of emphasis on having main entertaining spaces that are big and gracious,” says Ries. “The idea is that everybody hangs out together.”

Everything is united by generous outdoor spaces, which wind between the bedrooms. “Every room has an amazing view,” says Ries. “It feels almost like your own little treehouse in a way.” The bottom living area opens out to a pool and an expansive deck where the family eats most of their meals.

The house has an elevator, but stairs are the primary way the family moves between levels and finally down to the beach. “We hung on for a really great, gracious, fun set of stairs,” says von Gal. “They really introduce you to how amazing the different levels are and all the wonderful secret places.”

Durable fabrics and materials were chosen throughout and Ries made sure everything could be easily moved around and reconfigured. Outside on the main deck, three long dining tables from Sutherland were outfitted with casters so they could be maneuvered into a single long table or several smaller ones; 26 lightweight dining chairs can be pulled from different parts of the deck. If the infamous “Christmas winds” bring a downpour, the tables can even be moved indoors so a dinner party can proceed in inclement weather. Outdoor seating can be arranged as a 15-foot sofa or several love seats.

“It's so flexible,” says Ries. “We know this from working with a client that their needs for entertaining change every day, or sometimes a couple times a day.”

The kitchen is also efficient, designed to produce easy, clean meals. The family would rather spend time in the pool than over a stove. And no part of the living room is oriented around a TV. “They're readers,” says Ries. “There’s always stacks of new books when you go to their homes. So there’s all these cool little spots you can tuck into.”

Ultimately, the client and design team’s insistence on what they wanted paid off—but it wasn’t without a few moments of doubt. “When so many people are telling you ‘no, surely this is not what you want,’ it does start to erode your confidence a little,” says von Gal. “Then when it does come out well, there’s a real sense of triumph and satisfaction.”

Data centers are giant warehouses full of computer systems, complex computer systems requiring unprecedented amounts of RAM.

A favorite among traditionalists and avant-garde types alike is the fate of the suit. It’s been incorrectly declared dead enough times that the handwringing over its demise has largely shifted to questions of its evolving role in the modern guy’s wardrobe: Who wears a suit, and when, and why?

According to Alessandro Sartori, the artistic director of Zegna for a little more than a decade now, there’s another question we should all be asking ourselves: What exactly is a suit these days?

“Everything we do here is grounded in tailoring techniques and in tailoring fabrics,” Sartori tells me over Zoom from his home in Milan. “But I'm a big believer that a suit doesn't necessarily have to be a combination of the classic top and the classic bottom in the same fabric and color.” It can be comprised of those things, he stresses. But the “modern suit,” as he calls it, can also be an intentional pairing of a different kind of top—a chore coat, an overshirt—with a trouser with a different fabrication. The materials and colors should feel complementary and cohesive but needn’t be perfectly matched.

“This is not only a way to give a new life to suiting,” he explains, “but to dress men differently—and, at the same time, keep the quality, the craft, and the beautiful fit that a tailored piece has.”

The idea is to adapt classic techniques to the realities of getting dressed today. “Many fashion brands offer a nice quality, but is not the handmade quality that we do,” Sartori says. “And there are a lot of brands that are offering beautiful, beautiful quality, but they are quite classic.” Since he started in 2016, Sartori’s overarching project has been to populate the gulf between those two modes of production, making clothes that feel contemporary but are rooted in the top-tier fabrics and handwork that helped Zegna become the powerhouse of Italian fashion that it is today.

The latest addition to Sartori’s lineup is a new jacket, the Falcon. It pays homage to the sort of utilitarian jackets Zegna founder Ermenegildo Zegna might have worn while walking the grounds of what is now the Oasi Zegna nature reserve in northern Italy, at the base of the Biellese Alps. (According to company lore, Ermenegildo imagined those walks as being accompanied by the flight of a falcon.)

Sartori acknowledges that the use case, in 2026, might be a little different. “I don't think that people are necessarily using the jacket walking up in the mountains,” he says. The Falcon is more about embracing a “sporty-chic attitude,” whether you’re stomping around the country or the city, and working within the modular framework that the designer has created at Zegna. You can mix and match it with other pieces—both old and new—from the brand, and you can wear it as part of a “modern suit” or on its own.

The new design is cut from Zegna’s Oasi Cashmere for lightweight warmth, and Sartori calls it “a great addition to our vocabulary.” Buttoned patch pockets give you somewhere to stash your stuff, a bowling collar can be flipped up to shield against stiff winds, and an internal drawstring can be cinched at the midsection for a little extra protection against the cold. Ultimately, it’s a familiar-looking piece. But it’s exceptional in its construction and materials and its design—a streamlined take on the hunting jackets of yore—is as considered as how it’s made.

“Long-lasting life is a key factor for what we're doing, and I don't mean only quality-wise,” Sartori says. “I also mean design-wise.”

The designer, for his part, hopes that the Falcon—alongside other modern classics from the brand—will be the sort of thing that guys wear and reinterpret for years before passing it on to the next generation. And as the brand continues to grow and evolve alongside its customer base, one of his biggest aims is to ensure that each item he introduces remains relevant throughout its (ideally long) lifespan.

“It’s like writing a book, and every season I add a chapter,” he says. “Because the story is going on, is moving with our clients, our community of people, our brand, our family. So many layers are … I don’t want to say ready, but cooking. I see a bright future for the brand, following this principle of walking with our customers and building an indelible aesthetic. I like this approach.”

#6882. Take Note: This Is How Cool People Are Decorating for Thanksgiving This Year

These tastemakers are bringing their personal style to the holiday.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Pumpkin centerpieces, rustic garlands, dried-fruit wreaths, and cornucopias are classic Thanksgiving decorations. They’re timeless and beloved, but they can also feel predictable. Traditional decor has its place, of course, but Thanksgiving tablescapes—and dinner parties in general—are evolving, much like interiors, to feel more personal, lived-in, and layered. So we asked nine tastemakers and experts how they’re decorating for this family-centric holiday.

From traditional touches to wholly unexpected looks, read on to see how they’re making their homes feel festive this autumn, and maybe you’ll be inspired to try something new this year.

Ever since designer Mikel Welch moved to sunny California, his family has never hesitated to go to his for Thanksgiving to escape the chilly Michigan weather. It’s become a tradition to gather at his house, and similarly to how he grew up, Welch likes to keep his decorations for the holiday understated and warm.

“I love getting the exterior of my house into full fall mode with mums, pumpkins, and seasonal greenery,” Welch tells House Beautiful. “The beauty of fall decorating is that it can carry you from October straight through Thanksgiving without having to change much.”

The designer’s house is filled with dark, moody colors and earthy textures year-round, so he focuses mainly on bringing natural elements inside to do the work for him. “I also have a little tradition with my dad,” he adds. “For the past couple of years, we've made trips to the nursery together to pick out flowers, greenery, and sometimes bales of hay for the front of the house. It's become something I really enjoy doing with him.”

“Thanksgiving has always been a big family affair,” real estate expert and maximalism extraordinaire Harvin Greene says. She’s happy to keep that tradition going as an adult, just in her own way. In the past, Greene’s aunt and uncle would have everyone at their house for the big meal, but last year, they passed the hosting baton to Greene for the first time. “I may have gotten a little carried away...in the best possible way.”

Getting to decorate two tables—one for the “adults,” another for the “kids,” even though they’re technically all grown-ups, too—Greene used this opportunity to design two completely different tablescapes. “I grew up surrounded by women who understood that a beautifully set table and a genuinely good time should go hand in hand,” she adds.

The one in the formal dining space (i.e., the “adults’” table) was theatrical and dramatic, and nearly everything on it had some sort of familial connection, from her parents’ engraved goblets to her husband’s grandmother’s sterling flatware. The kids’ table, on the other hand, was whimsical and vibrant, complete with a yellow patterned tablecloth, colorful glassware, and playful animal candlesticks. “I don't feel the need to follow the traditional Thanksgiving color palette,” Greene says. “Who decided Thanksgiving had to be orange and brown? Yellow, pink, turquoise, and green can be just as festive.”

The holiday isn’t over until they bring out the Bingo cards, a spirited Thanksgiving tradition that Greene’s Aunt Jean started—which involves cash prizes. “It's one of those family traditions that makes the holiday feel like ours, no matter whose house we're celebrating in.”

Talia Hubble is no stranger to a good dinner party—much of her content actually centers around her love of hosting. But Thanksgiving is a bit extra special for the Rhode Islander, especially since it’s being held at her cottage this year. “Growing up, it was just me and my mom, and she was too busy to decorate,” Hubble says. “I think that’s part of why I’ve fallen in love with seasonal decorating as an adult, I get to bring all my cozy childhood decorating dreams to life!”

As soon as the calendar switches from October to November, Hubble starts decorating for fall, opting for an eclectic mix of subtle Christmas decor (think bows, garlands, and wreaths), an excessive amount of taper candles, and a traditional Thanksgiving palette of oranges, browns, greens, and reds. Every year looks a little bit different, but there are pieces in her collection that are simply too cute not to bring out every holiday season.

“Half the fun of pulling everything out for Thanksgiving is remembering where it came from,” she says. “I think the best decor is a culmination of pieces you’ve been collecting for years, and the memories behind those pieces are what make the table feel special and uniquely yours.”

By the time she’s done decorating, her home feels like a warm, welcoming escape, somewhere her guests can exhale. “My philosophy is that you can’t go wrong when you aim for cozy, colorful, and embrace a little organized mess,” Hubble adds. “My favorite Thanksgiving tables are the ones that look even better halfway through dinner, when the napkins are rumpled and everyone’s reaching for seconds.”

Chef and food stylist Romilly Newman can’t always count on being in the same place as her family for Thanksgiving since everyone’s scattered about, but she always makes a point to throw an autumnal dinner with friends around the holiday time, complete with beautiful fall decor.

Her mom never decorated their home while she was growing up with specific Thanksgiving decorations—instead, she would decorate with deep orange and red flowers to capture the fall vibe. Newman’s style as an adult is similar. “I am fall-obsessed, so as soon as the temperature drops from summer, I'm setting up branches, candles, and darker accents,” she says. “I love the atmosphere inside and outside around Thanksgiving time, and I try my best to bottle that up in my home.”

Natural thicker linen, pops of silver, dark florals, and moody lighting envelop her space as the chef relies mostly on candlelight and organic elements—like figs, sage, chestnuts, and mandarins—as the decorative accents.

“Thanksgiving is great because it brings family together and offers time to enjoy food, friends, and being at home,” Newman adds. “Whatever your style is, try to create an environment that allows for great conversation and a festive evening. You want everyone to be comfortable and feel the holiday spirit.”

When writer Olivia Noceda was a child, she would stay up late the night before Thanksgiving and design custom menus for everyone on Microsoft Paint, which she would then print out, frame, and place on each table setting in preparation for the big meal. The holiday was a big deal for Noceda’s mother, who would always put on the most beautiful Thanksgiving gatherings. “I think that's where my love for hosting and setting a beautiful table really started,” she explains.

However, for the last six years, Noceda has been having Thanksgiving with her husband’s side of the family, so when she does host around the holiday, it’s typically for Friendsgiving. “I don't necessarily pick a theme, but I do like everything to feel cohesive,” she explains of her decorating process. “I usually start incorporating little autumnal touches sometime in October and let them carry me through Thanksgiving.”

The writer and recipe developer lets the season guide her, building her tablescape and decor around a beautiful branch from the farmers’ market, a bowl of pears or persimmons, a collection of little pumpkins and squash, or a few taper candles on the dining room table. Ingredients play a big part in her decor—she can even take a fresh loaf of bread and make it feel special on her table.

For the rest of her decor, she likes to incorporate vintage serving pieces, everyday elements, and elevated details like antique silver and linen napkins with things she already owns to lean into that lived-in, warm feeling. The last thing she wants is her home to feel like a department store display. “Nothing needs to match perfectly, and I actually prefer when it doesn't,” Noceda adds.

What entrepreneur and Real Housewives of New York star Erin Lichy remembers best about Thanksgivings growing up is the smell. “We always spent Thanksgiving out east, and the house was full of pumpkins, local hay, and lanterns,” she explains. The scent of that fresh produce, mixed with her grandmother’s homemade turkey t’bit, has shaped the way she decorates for and hosts Thanksgiving. “That coveted and highly personal (and incredibly delicious) recipe is in my cookbook, She’s a Host, and it’s part of every Thanksgiving I host.”

Decorating starts in the kitchen for her family. Lichy wants the home to smell like fall, so she focuses on that sense, making seasonal recipes with squash and pumpkin and incorporating autumnal flowers in her interiors. The melding of those scents gives her townhouse a warm feeling, especially when mixed with layered fall colors and the crackling of the fireplace.

Ever since Lichy and her family have moved into their townhouse, she’s been obsessed with decorating her stoop too. “The stoop is the first thing everyone sees, so this year I decided to bring in Simon Wallace Design to make it really stand out!” Lichy adds. “They completely transformed it with layers of seasonal color and texture, and it feels so warm and welcoming.” She specifically loves using the faux garlands and pinecones, as well as lanterns, from Balsam Hill and Frontgate because they’re so beautifully made that they look real, and she’s able to reuse them year after year.

No matter what she does to decorate though, she just wants her home to feel warm and lived-in, and this is the season and holiday to really lean into that.

Decorating for the holidays is Gabby Amato’s version of a self-care day. “It's truly one of my favorite days of the year,” she says. “I carve out a weekend afternoon when my husband and son are out of the house, light my favorite fall candles, turn on some music, and have the house entirely to myself.”

Amato, the founder of Table Two Studio, a paper plate brand which specializes in beautiful, artistic designs that you definitely won’t want to throw away, credits her love of Thanksgiving and the fall season in general to her mother. While growing up in the ‘90s, she saw her mom put so much care and effort into the holiday decorations, always making their home feel warm, nostalgic, and full of spirit. “For Thanksgiving, she decorated our mantel with gourds, greenery, and ceramic pilgrim statues that were a gift from my grandmother,” Amato says. “We had oversized cornstalks and pumpkins at the front door. Nothing ever felt overly styled or precious.”

It’s this feeling that has stuck with her throughout her life, and it’s what she hopes she captures in her own home every year. She begins decorating for the fall early, right after Labor Day, pulling out old pieces and unboxing anything new and arranging it all in fresh ways so nothing feels stale. “I love beautiful decor, of course, but I'm much more interested in creating an atmosphere than achieving a particular aesthetic,” she adds. “I want my home to feel warm, lived-in, a little nostalgic, and festive.”

Donna Lennard’s family was never one for holiday dressing—most of the attention at Thanksgiving fell on the delicious food shared with family. As a restauranteur and cofounder of beloved NYC establishment il Buco, it makes sense why the meal remains to be the most important thing to Lennard even today.

“Clearly, the table is always festive, whether it’s the tablecloth, the napkins, or linens that reflect the season in burnt orange and the like,” she explains. “I love to decorate a table with assorted colorful gourds and seasonal florals, beautiful candlelight (our beeswax candles already have an orange glow), orange ranunculus, and the occasional dried floral.” Colorful Pienza glassware dots the table and her plethora of beautiful tableware gives it more character, but she keeps everything else relatively simple to ensure the food remains the center of attention.

Though Lennard doesn’t often host her family and friends for the Thanksgiving meal, she’s already looking forward to next year when everyone will finally gather at her beach house.

Being from Australia, Kendall Knox—the cofounder of Olive Ateliers along with her husband—didn’t have a personal reference of what the holiday “should” be or look like. This gives her the freedom of deciding what it is for her family. “What it has become is an intimate table: my husband Ben, our son Rhodes, and Ben's mum,” she says. “No rotation, no kids' table, no forty-pound bird. Just the four of us and a dinner that runs long because nobody is in a hurry to leave it.”

Since Knox isn’t beholden to the classic Thanksgiving decor, she tends to keep her home and her table rather simple, “shopping the house,” as she puts it to decorate instead of buying something new. She keeps a rather neutral palette of rich browns, creamy whites, terracotta, and silver, then uses antique linens, mismatched silverware, and candlesticks that have collected wax to capture the true lived-in feel.

Her focus tends to be on the table and the meal, letting the food add more color. If a tablecloth has a wine ring on it from the year before, all the better—it complements the stacked plates at the end of the meal.

“The objects that come out every year, that get a little more marked each time, are the ones that end up meaning the most,” Knox adds. “Thanksgiving is just a very good excuse to use them.”

Will 2026 go down as one of the best years in video games? There just simply aren’t enough hours in the calendar year to play through all of the amazing games that came out before Thursday, November 19, and now we have to add on hundreds of hours of Grand Theft Auto VI.

Of course, the industry’s economic woes cannot be overstated. It’s a dark time when even prolific studios downsize, slash budgets, and lay off talented developers with invaluable institutional knowledge. Even the GTA VI designers—whose game is already heralded as a masterpiece—are still fighting for better working conditions. So it’s hard to actually call 2026 a great year in gaming when it’s also been a year of heartbreak and uncertainty for the future.

But the games are still great. Even just a few hours into GTA VI, it’s obvious that it’s a massive achievement for Rockstar Games that’s well worth the wait. And just this past month, Nintendo dropped a remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time that features beautifully upgraded graphics from the original 1998 classic. Elsewhere, Resident Evil Requiem nailed the franchise’s decades-long identity split with a game that’s half third-person action and half first-person survival horror. Plus, action games like 007 First Light, Control Resonant, and Onimusha: Way of the Sword let loose the power fantasies we’ve only dreamed of.

But it’s not all blood and guts and guns and swords. Earlier this year, the coming-of-age Mixtape caused a ruckus among gaming circles, though anyone who’s played it and loved it will tell you just how much it’s a love letter to bygone summer days. Oh, and a spin-off Pokémon title about building adorable towns and protecting the environment is currently the highest-rated game in the franchise’s thirty-year history.

So, unless you’re already dedicated to spending all of Thanksgiving and Christmas in GTA VI’s Vice City, some of the best games Esquire’s editors have played through in 2026 so far.

GTA VI hadn’t even been out for twenty-four hours yet and critics were already calling it a masterpiece. To be fair, video game critics call a lot of titles masterpieces, but it was clear from everyone’s immediate reaction to Vice City that Rockstar Games spent the last decade making something truly special. Sure, there are hundreds of hours of side quests and town-foolery to get lost in, but GTA VI also features a Bonnie & Clyde-esque love story between its two playable protagonists—Jason and Lucia—which breaks up the beloved smash-and-grab chaos of the Grand Theft Auto franchise and makes the gamer question just where the point between a video game and real life begins.

At its most basic, Nintendo’s The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time remake isn’t all that different from the classic, 1998 original. The game certainly looks a lot better on the Nintendo Switch 2 than it did on the Nintendo 64, now that the polygonal characters and sharp outdoors have been converted to more living and breathing graphics. But at the end of the day, you’re still playing the same game that you played in 1998. Don’t take that as a knock, though! Ocarina of Time still tops “Best Video Games of All Time” lists for a reason.

There are times playing Star Wars: Galactic Racer when you will feel like you are going too fast. And yes, the rate at which it will feel like that will shock you. But that’s pod racing! And better than any racing sim, let alone a Star Wars video game with voice acting as good as Galactic Racer, it truly feels like you’re speeding around in a hunk-a-junk that you just cobbled together with space garbage and jet engines.

Following up Fire Emblem: Three Houses was already a tall task, let alone having to follow Fire Emblem: Engage. Both titles combined the best of Japanese RPGs with the new slice-of-life school relationship activities introduced in Three Houses. And now Fortune’s Weave, something of a trilogy-capper for the series, is the most maximalist entry yet. With dozens of characters, choices, a vast map, and four unique story quests that all overlap, there’s so much to do in the latest Fire Emblem game that they could wait another five years before the next entry.

The Blood of Dawnwalker is the debut game from Rebel Wolves, a new studio started by former creatives from The Witcher 3: Blood Hunt. So if Dawnwalker feels eerily like a Witcher game, you’re not crazy. That being said, the vampire game’s controversial timer—which forces players to make quick decisions with big impacts on gameplay—fully separated the RPG and proved that the new studio was ready to try out exciting ideas all its own.

Remedy’s Alan Wake 2 topped Esquire’s Best Video Games of 2023 list three years ago, so you can bet we were excited for the studio’s next game, Control Resonant. The latest is a sequel to 2019’s Control, which now finds its former protagonist Jesse Faden missing. So, support character Dylan Faden, now in the spotlight, takes control to deal with the foreign entities and save his estranged sister.

Can video games have too much of a good thing? Apparently not. Phantom Blade Zero, the umpteenth samurai RPG since Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice and Ghost of Tsushima, dazzled gamers with its combat and fluid martial arts combos. And while it firmly isn’t a soulslike—the developer explicitly rejects the genre, actually—that still doesn’t mean Phantom Blade Zero is a walk in the park either.

How about a game that’s a little less stressful? If you need a break from the fast-paced action, check out Big Walk, a co-operative puzzle game about, well, going on a long walk. Plus, the characters are cute as hell.

Star Wars fans ate good this year. If racing isn’t your thing, check out Zero Company, a turn-based tactics game that falls somewhere in between Fire Emblem and XCOM. Though the game isn’t all that challenging on easier difficulties, players have found a lot of fun and repeat plays with the game’s permadeath mode that doesn’t allow characters to return after they die.

Rock out with your claws out. Insomniac applies its winning formula for triple-A superhero games to flesh out the most popular X-Men member of all time. The five-foot-four berserker stars in his own solo adventure in a universe where mutants live in secret. While piecing together his own memories, Wolverine meets a telepath named Jean Grey. Together they must stop a powerful industrialist from unleashing a weapon that could kill all mutants. A hyper-violent tour through some of its title antihero’s classic comics, Wolverine doesn’t quite reach the same heights as Spider-Man, but the way it lets players truly feel like “The Best There Is” is gold.

If you could rewind to your last day of school, what would it look like? Who are you with? And most importantly, what’s on the stereo? At the start of summer, developers Beethoven & Dinosaur released the gorgeous narrative game Mixtape, a loving homage to coming-of-age cinema with a tastefully curated soundtrack. (We’re talking Joy Division, Silverchair, B.J. Thomas, and more.) Set in the summer of 1995, the game sees three California teens relish their final hours together before going their separate ways. While Mixtape kicked up a storm among gamers over what defines a “game”—you can’t die or fail, nor are any of the things you do in Mixtape remotely challenging—it’s still a special experience that I argue illuminates how special games can be as a storytelling medium. Your adolescence probably didn’t look exactly like Mixtape, but the game can nonetheless trigger old memories.

As the world waits with bated breath to see who will play the next James Bond on the big screen, video games have a Bond to call their own in actor Patrick Gibson. From developer IO Interactive, of the smash Hitman series, comes 007 First Light, a high-octane espionage action game that follows a new James Bond in the early days of his career at MI6. Unlike the Hitman games, which are mired in the darkness of Agent 47’s deadly profession, 007 First Light revels in the fantasy of Bond’s perilous and pleasurable lifestyle. It’s basically the closest any of us will get to feeling like James Bond short of renting an Aston Martin for a test drive.

Now Yacht Club has done it again with Mina the Hollower, which mines plenty of inspiration from gothic dungeon crawlers like Castlevania and Game Boy–era Legend of Zelda games, with modern flourishes à la Dark Souls. Players control Mina, a genius 19th-century mouse who must slay monsters and restore power to her home. Don’t let its cute looks fool you: Mina the Hollower is a challenging game that will have you sweating and smiling all the way through.

There’s no shortage of killer samurai games nowadays. Which is perhaps why Capcom saw fit to resurrect its dormant Onimusha franchise with the exceptional Onimusha: Way of the Sword.
