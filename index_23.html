#6933. AI Ambitions Are Running Into a Network Infrastructure Trust Problem

Enterprise ambitions around AI are running into a fundamental problem: Orgnanizations aren't always confident in the networks those projects depend on, according to a report from telecommunications provider Arelion.

The research found that concerns about network providers have caused organizations to rethink strategic initiatives over the past two years. Among respondents whose initiatives were affected, AI and data-driven projects were the most cited, at 49%. That put AI ahead of security and compliance programs at 44%, digital transformation at 42%, and cloud migration at 38%.

The findings come from Arelion's report, "The Trust Illusion: Why AI and Digital Transformation Need More Than a Reliable Network," based on a survey of 518 senior enterprise network decision-makers in the U.S., U.K., Germany, and France conducted by Savanta.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

Another tension runs through Arelion's findings. Enterprises need networks they can trust to support AI projects, but many of the same decision-makers are concerned about AI's growing role inside those networks. A total of 54% of respondents identified AI and automation in network management and security as the factors most likely to negatively affect network trust over the next three years. That ranked ahead of data sovereignty expectations and geopolitical instability, both at 39%, and regulatory requirements and quantum computing threats to current encryption standards, both at 38%.

At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. Proactive AI-driven fault prediction was among the capabilities they said would matter most to future trust, alongside stronger built-in security, greater accountability, and responsive support. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. "As businesses become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, enterprises are looking for network providers that can demonstrate transparency, accountability, and operational excellence, not just availability."

Forty percent of survey respondents cited a lack of specialized infrastructure required by AI as a main challenge to adopting a formal AI strategy, up from 9% in the company's 2024 research.

The Global Data Insights Survey 2026 points to a widening mix of infrastructure models behind enterprise AI. Half of respondents host AI models in private clouds, 86% are pursuing or exploring sovereign AI, 92% tie data-location strategy to their AI plans, and 88% follow a distributed-data approach.

The research is based on an online survey of 2,131 IT decision-makers across 19 countries, and 11 industries, conducted from Nov. 23, 2025, through Feb. 4, 2026.

Organizations also appear to be shifting from AI planning toward execution. The share actively executing AI to achieve operational efficiency or reduce costs rose from 27% in 2024 to 37% this year. Specialized infrastructure led the survey's list of AI adoption challenges at 40%, followed by regulatory and personal-data concerns at 35%. Data availability and quality, storage capacity, interconnection, compute resources, and staffing also ranked as significant constraints.

Those requirements are also shaping workload placement. Integration with existing IT or data infrastructure ranked first at 43%, while regulatory compliance and performance were each cited by 37%. Data proximity, sovereignty, sustainability, cost, and intellectual property protection also factored into hosting decisions.

Private cloud features prominently across the report's AI hosting categories, but enterprises are clearly using a mix of private cloud, public cloud, on-premises infrastructure, and colocation. Private cloud accounted for 50% of custom-built AI model hosting and 47% for open source or fine-tuned large language models (LLMs).

Sovereignty is another part of that placement decision. #6901. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. #6813. How Palace Kept Its Cool

It’s rare that almost every member of a company’s upper management team is a diehard skateboarder. But at Palace — the British apparel brand founded in 2009 — it’s par for the course. Gareth Skewis, who co-founded the company with Lev Tanju, cites this as one of the core reasons the international, multi-million-pound business has been able to expand and maintain its credibility in subcultures across the globe.

This week, the brand aired an exclusive capsule with Burberry at its new store on London’s Poland Street — a milestone for a brand that bet on quintessentially British skate style at a time when competitors were largely inspired by North American streetwear. Burberry and Palace had many conversations over the years, discussing projects and the right time to make the collaboration happen, Skewis tells Vogue Business. Then, the right opportunity arrived. “They’ve been amazing. [Burberry creative director] Daniel Lee and his team, and [CEO] Joshua Schulman have been really great to work with.”

The new Palace store opened to the public on Thursday.

Palace spawned from a motley crew of Southbank skaters, who rented a rundown flat in South London, known informally as “The Palace”. Together, they named themselves the Palace Wayward Boys Choir (PWBC), with core members including Tanju and the brand’s head of creative, Stuart Hammond. Tanju and Skewis were in discussion about a brand around the same time Tanju secured a slot on photography and film duo Tim & Barry’s “Don’t Watch That TV” YouTube channel — a precursor to grime’s streaming boom — to air his skate news program, The PWBC Weekly News, every Wednesday at 4.20pm. “The first time I saw any of that, I was like, ‘This is the most interesting thing I’ve seen in skateboarding in 20 years,’” says Skewis, who decided after episode one that Palace would be the brand name.

In 2015, Palace set up shop on Soho’s Brewer street, which thrived — not dissimilar to the likes of Supreme, Gimme 5, and the Hideout — on its cult appeal, staffed by skaters and DJs who naturally communicated the brand’s messaging. During the peak of the late-2010s streetwear boom, the locale was consistently thronged with teens and twenty-somethings who would camp out for hours ahead of big drops. In recent years, whenever I’ve visited the store, it’s been quieter, but still busy.

Gareth Skewis, co-founder of Palace, believes in the power of IRL experiences to channel brand messaging.

Fast forward to now, and the brand is a familiar figure within fashion, luxury, and sportswear, having partnered with the likes of Harrods, Vivienne Westwood, Moschino, and Arc’Teryx, as well as niche labels, such as Japan’s Beams Plus and Evisu.

For UK trading, in the year ended January 31, 2026, the company reported £27.6 million in turnover, down from £34 million the year prior. The filing on Companies House attributed this to product volume discipline and strategic release cadence in line with a focus on long-term positioning over short-term revenue. Before that, the company had reported year-on-year growth amid the post-Covid bounceback. (The brand declined to comment directly on financials, although it made clear the results are not representative of global trading — there is a holding company on top of Palace Skateboards.)

“Lev [Tanju, co-founder] and I have the luxury that we’ve never done anything we didn’t want to do,” says Skewis. “Not everybody gets it all the time, and I think that’s a good thing.” Sometimes, that means collaborations or product innovations that are more niche (Palace x artist Dick Jewell), while other times, it’s a simple win. The 10-piece Burberry capsule — launched exclusively in the new Palace London flagship — falls under the latter. “Who doesn’t like [Burberry’s] Camden car coats?” Skewis shrugs. “We’re south of the river, and they’re north of the river, so it’s an interesting juxtaposition,” he adds, observing the city’s internal rivalry.

The limited drop and new store come at a pivotal moment in Palace’s trajectory. Last weekend, the company celebrated its first year partnering with Nike, following the end of its 10-year contract with Adidas, which manifested in a recurring collaboration alongside other ad-hoc partnerships. Nike took the spot in 2025 with the arrival of Manor Place, a Grade-II-listed Flemish revival building housing a skatepark that transforms into a five-a-side soccer pitch through state-of-the-art hydraulics. Architecture studio Jam designed the multifunction venue; it’s also the Palace office.

With Nike, Palace has produced a series of ongoing collaborations, while Manor Place functions as a contemporary “third space”, open to the public for daily use and offering a year free in studio space to resident artists and creatives, a gallery space, and boxing classes. (It’s also hosted London designer incubator Fashion East’s shows since Fall/Winter 2026.)

The skatepark, architecturally inspired by famous skate spots across the British capital, has ostensibly been a major hit. (Anecdotally, my personal visits confirmed consistent footfall.) It also provides Palace with a pipeline for new talent — rappers, skaters, hosts, and photographers — who collaborate with the brand on commercial videos, as well as a consistent flow of cultural happenings to build TikTok and Instagram content around.

The hydraulic five-a-side-pitch-cum-skatepark at Manor Place.

For big brands seeking to capitalize on youth culture, the space is a case study for why giving back and engaging organically matter. “When we first discussed it with Nike, it was really interesting to see how much they got behind it,” recalls Skewis. “And truthfully, anyone who’s living in any inner-city on the planet currently knows that there are not many places young kids can go and do things that are both free and safe.”

Like most office workers, Skewis loves Fridays. “Manor Place opens at about 12pm. Anyone who’s in England will just come and skate,” he says. “You’ve literally got 12-year-old kids skating with people who are either professional skateboarders or who have jobs running ad agencies or in the city.”

For Skewis, physical retail is the best way to communicate a brand’s message, stronger than out-of-home and digital advertising or exclusive brand dinners. “I like standing in spaces where you understand what that brand smells and sounds like, and the way the customer service and staff work with you,” he says. Manor Place, which has a shop, does this — and hopefully the new store will, too.

The new 250-square-meter store — primed with David Sims photography, parquet flooring, and huge screens to play skate videos — opened to VIPs on Wednesday, followed by a public unveiling on Thursday. Alongside Palace’s concession in Dover Street Market London, Poland Street is the brand’s core direct-to-consumer (DTC) location in London.

Inside 8 Poland Street, Palace’s new London flagship.

The two-story space is specced with light wells, stained-glassed windows, bare concrete, museological displays, stone plinths, and a number eight-shaped door handle. (The eight motif also appears in the Burberry collection.) It reflects Palace’s USP as a culturally curious, luxury-adjacent skate brand. “It’s got that tension: certain bits of iconography that don’t feel like they fit in classic skateboarding retail,” Skewis adds.

The designer of the new store, Steve Oh, has worked with Palace for several years, designing the brand’s spaces in South Korea, Hong Kong, Shanghai, and Singapore. Indeed, Palace has ramped up its expansion in the Asia-Pacific region, now counting DTC outposts across Japan, South Korea, China and Singapore, as well as its US locations.

But how has the brand carried its hyper-London brand messaging and subcultural capital to foreign climes and consumer landscapes as vastly different as East Asia’s? Through meeting fellow skaters and creatives on the ground. “If you’ve skated for long enough, there are natural scenes,” Skewis says. “We’ve got really close friends in Shanghai, Hong Kong, Seoul, New York.” In fact, Skewis has known Palace’s strategic advisor for the China region, Stéphen Khou, for some 25 years, acquainted through his travels in France and London’s ground zero for skating, Southbank. “It’s really been an organic approach, and I think that’s kind of what Palace is. We’re not trying to be anything that we’re not.”

The roadmap for the next 10 years? Skewis plays it cool. “If we can continue to do things like Manor Place, put out skate videos, do the things we love… That’s really what we’re about.”

NKBA identifies ventilation, water filtration, touchless faucets, and sound-absorbing materials among the report’s wellness features. #6790. After the Midterms, Millennial Optimism Makes Sense Again

None of us are the same people we were in 2008. But it’s okay, just for a day, to bask in hope.

I have told this story many times in writing, but in 2008 I was so hopeful on election night that I stripped off my clothes with a bar full of people, right before they called Pennsylvania for Obama, to take a picture and commemorate the moment. They took the photo. We got up off the sticky bar floor, pulled our clothes back on, and literally danced in the streets with the cops while the Obama reggaeton song played. This was peak millennial political optimism, and the rush of it could make you believe that all the contradictions of this country didn’t matter. That we were all moving forward together.

In 2008, I was a junior researcher at a Black history museum, a place where people thought deeply about legacy and historical sea change. I remember being so deeply excited, after seven years of political insanity post-9/11, that maybe the United States was finally waking up. It was a shock, then, to talk to my older coworkers, most of whom had been involved with or studied the Black radical tradition, and hear their cautious indifference to what felt like a coming revolution. “We’ll see,” most of them said—a pragmatism about this country that I didn’t fully understand back then. Back then I hoped, deep down, that what those older coworkers were experiencing was merely sour grapes.

In 2026, of course, we are all living through the political backlash that they were sure was coming and the missteps of moderation during the Obama years that set the stage for Trump. I don’t really hope about politics anymore. Yes, I know hope is a discipline. Hope is something that, by definition, you have to have when things are dire. But the way hope lives in my body—a frantic, desperate thing that plummets when it meets catastrophe—means national politics aren’t a good place for that emotion. When I talk about politics now with friends, it’s mostly to see who can make the darkest joke about our current timeline.

But after last night, things are different. Like a lot of people, I’m allowing myself one calendar day to actually feel excited about the state of the world. I’m still saying “Let’s see,” but for right now, every time someone tries to bring up why we’re still doomed, I keep scrolling. I can do bad all by myself tomorrow.

Since Trump entered the scene 10 years ago, there’s been an active disdain for sincerity in politics. The reasons for this are many. Trump and MAGA ran on the belief that becoming great could only be measured by how badly the people you hated were suffering. Those who would oppose Trump grew cynical about the national Democratic party’s dance of fearmongering about the state of the country and steadfastly refusing to do anything meaningful about it when given the power to do so. Between these two extremes, it felt foolish to care. It felt smarter and safer to view politics the way a consultant or a pundit might, with the distance of someone who keeps telling you he has no skin in the game. As if that position was a superior one. As if that distance could save you any heartbreak or keep the threat of a layoff or a medical bill or an eviction at bay.

To care is to be cringe, and in the last few years, being cringe has been one of the greatest cultural sins, a devastating social faux pas. But last night was such a seismic shift in American politics that the idea of cringe is currently blissfully irrelevant. Being cringe enough to care brought millions to the polls and a historic midterm turnout. Being cringe enough to care meant that people like James Talarico, Abdul El-Sayed, and Angie Nixon launched campaigns even as everyone told their potential base that voting was irrelevant, that nothing could ever change about their states.

I’m not the same person I was in 2008; hopefully none of us are. And this is not the same country. But to make sure this country is one where I want to be present, I have to believe on some level that it’s possible. To change anything in this world, you first have to care enough to want to do it.

Then he lost the part to Jesse Eisenberg. Director David Fincher had seen what, today, is obvious: Humanity flows fast and deep through Andrew Garfield. Fincher cast him instead as Eduardo Saverin, the betrayed cofounder and emotional counterweight to Zuckerberg’s opacity, who cries, sneers, throws a feint, and smashes a laptop (that’s one scene).

The movie made him a celebrity, and he brought an unusual depth of emotion to this job too. While serving as Spider-Man, he begged the public, via a handwritten sign held up for paparazzi, to care about a more important cause than what’s up with famous people. While promoting 2021’s Tick, Tick…Boom! on The Late Show With Stephen Colbert, he talked about the death of his mother; the clip still gets shared by grieving people.

Boxers by Hermès. Sneakers by Bottega Veneta. Socks by Jacques. Watch by Rolex.

Now, 15 years after The Social Network, Garfield has picked back up the tech-CEO thread to play Sam Altman in Artificial, Luca Guadagnino’s film that covers the founding of OpenAI up to the coup that led to Altman’s firing—and his re-entrenchment. (OpenAI has an agreement with Condé Nast, the owner of GQ, which allows OpenAI to display its content in search results for a limited term.) The movie seems destined to occupy the same orienting role as The Social Network, helping us understand the technology and the men who are shaping our futures, almost in real time. But whereas that film was a legal drama about status (Harvard guy vs. hotter, richer-born Harvard guys), Artificial plays as a horror movie where the monster, AI, is real. (And it just learned how to do theoretical physics.)

Garfield brings Altman to life as a mad guidance counselor: rubbing the shoulders of engineers, taking security meetings cross-legged, and sucking green juice in the faces of his adversaries. Here, finally, is an ascendant tech overlord that makes unsettling use of Garfield’s famous sensitivity. When Garfield-as-Altman cries, you’re not sure whether they’re the tears of a true superintelligence believer or a duplicitous power-seeker. Or which one would be scarier.

When we meet in early October, Garfield is transformed by a beard and shoulder-length hair. He grew it all out to play the magician Roy Horn, of Siegfried and Roy, in next year’s show Wild Things, though they ended up putting a wig on him anyway. He was filming that limited series in Berlin immediately after wrapping The Uprising, a Paul Greengrass movie about the Peasants’ Revolt of 1381, which shot before Artificial was even technically wrapped. “I did these three things back to back last year, and I did have a bit of burnout, to be honest. I should have just gone to Berghain and just rode it out.” Now, though, Garfield is visibly buzzing, both from watching Artificial with an audience at the New York Film Festival the night before—and from the chatter coming out of Silicon Valley.

The pitch is that Volufiline is basically filler in a bottle. But does it actually check out?

Volufiline is a trademarked cosmetic ingredient made with Anemarrhena asphodeloides root extract (a source of the compound sarsasapogenin) and hydrogenated polyisobutene (an emollient that softens and smooths skin). “The theory is that sarsasapogenin encourages fat cells to develop and store more lipids, making them fuller,” says Kseniya Kobets, MD, director of cosmetic dermatology at Montefiore Einstein Advanced Care. Essentially, the hope is to counteract volume loss that naturally occurs as we grow older.

We’ve spent the last year sampling the latest releases in men’s skin care, hair care, and more. Our favorites will help you level up.

In a small study by Sederma, the French company that manufactures Volufiline, 28 women applied a cream containing Volufiline to one breast for 56 days. While the treated breast’s average volume increased by 2.2%, the result wasn’t statistically significant. Kobets also points out that the comparison was with an untreated breast—rather than the same cream without Volufiline—so it’s unclear if Volufiline or other ingredients in the cream drove the increase in volume. Plus, we can’t exactly extrapolate the results seen in a female breast to a man’s face.

Despite the lack of robust clinical evidence supporting its anti-aging claims, Volufiline is the latest topical du jour that people are crediting with their plumper, more supple skin—namely in areas prone to volume loss like the undereyes, smile lines, cheeks, and lips. Some before-and-afters on social media are visually compelling. But the bigger question is whether Volufiline meaningfully increases facial volume or if the improvements are simply due to a temporary plumping effect—or even the other ingredients paired alongside it.

“Some improvement likely comes from the moisturizing formula: hydration softens fine lines, emollients smooth the surface, and that finish changes how light reflects off skin,” Kobets says. (Changes in lighting and specific camera angles can also make the after photos look more striking.) While Volufiline might help your skin look better, the jury’s still out on whether it meaningfully restores lost volume. “Making skin look hydrated and plump isn’t the same as rebuilding the fat underneath it,” Kobets says.

As for those filler in a bottle claims? Alexandra Bowles, DO, a board-certified dermatologist at MONA Dermatology, sets the record straight. “Volufiline is designed to support volume in the skin, and some people may notice slightly plumper, fuller-looking skin over time,” she says. Meanwhile, injectable filler adds immediate volume and structure and can reshape areas like the tear trough, lips, and jawline. “A cream or serum works at the surface and builds gradually, so it can’t create that kind of change,” she says. Read: While it may complement your skin care routine, it won’t replace or replicate results from filler.

Both derms are open to patients experimenting with Volufiline, but with a few caveats. “I think Volufiline is worth trying if you have realistic expectations, but it’s definitely overhyped as an alternative to filler,” Bowles says. If you really want to (pardon the pun) move the needle on undereye hollows, loss of lip volume, or a less-defined jawline, she says carefully placed filler or other in-office treatments may be more effective.

Kobets doesn’t rule out trying Volufiline, either, so long as you’re not expecting to restore facial fat. That said, she advises being cautious if you have a chronic skin condition like eczema or rosacea, or if you’re acne-prone. Try a patch test first and stop if your skin stings or burns.

Bowles suggests prioritizing reputable brands with clear ingredient lists and modest claims around a gradual improvement in the look and feel of skin. Be wary of products that promise instant results, have vague ingredient lists, or come from unverified sellers. “Don’t experiment with raw ingredients or assume a higher percentage works better,” Kobets adds.

The TL;DR: Volufiline is intriguing, but the hype is outpacing the evidence. More rigorous research is needed to address its volume-enhancing claims for facial skin, so take them with a grain of salt. Consistently using a well-formulated product might help your skin look more supple and hydrated; just don’t expect any miracles or a true dupe for injectables. “Good skin care improves skin quality, but it can’t do every job we’re asking of it,” Kobets says.

#6772. Everything You Need to Know About Fiber and Crohn's Disease, According to Dietitians

Just thinking about fiber can make many people with Crohn’s feel nervous. After all certain fiber-rich foods can be tough to tolerate, but the ones that cause trouble aren’t always obvious. Even a seemingly harmless omelet or a fruit smoothie can trigger symptoms in some people. So if you’ve been avoiding the nutrient, that’s understandable—and not uncommon. Research suggests people with Crohn’s tend to eat relatively little fiber, even during remission. One 2015 study found the average daily intake for individuals with the condition was just 16 grams. For context, experts recommend that men aim for 38 grams a day.

But here’s the thing: Having an inflammatory bowel disease doesn’t necessarily mean you should stick to a low-fiber diet. Some studies have found that eating foods rich in the nutrient may actually lower your risk of future flares, improve gut health, and reduce inflammation not only in your gastrointestinal tract but throughout your body.

We get it, though—navigating the sometimes confusing and land-mine-filled world of fiber can feel overwhelming, especially post-flare, and the stakes are high. No one wants to feel crummy or worry about being stuck near a toilet all day. That’s why we asked a group of registered dietitians to answer some of the most pressing questions about Crohn’s and fiber. Here’s what they said about getting enough without upsetting your stomach, which types may be harder to tolerate, and more.

Let’s take a step back and review some nutrition science 101.

In the simplest terms, fiber is a plant-based carbohydrate our bodies cannot fully breakdown or absorb. It resists digestion in the stomach and small intestine and depending on the type, is either partially or fully fermented by gut bacteria in the colon. This makes it different from other nutrients like fats and proteins, which the body breaks down and absorbs.

There are two main types of fiber, each with its own functions and benefits. Here’s a quick overview of each:

What it is: A type of fiber that passes through the GI tract largely intact, adds bulk to stools, helps keep you regular, and aids in regulating blood sugar. Foods rich in insoluble fiber are typically the ones we think of as hard to digest.

Where to find it: Pear and apple skins, raspberries, wheat bran, leafy greens like kale and collard greens, nuts, seeds, and legumes, among other foods.

What it is: A type of fiber that dissolves in water and forms a gel in the gut, slowing carbohydrate absorption. It helps control cholesterol, increases satiety, and makes stools easier to pass.

Where to find it: Beans, oats, barley, carrots, and the flesh of pears, among other foods.

The short answer: yes. But proceed with caution. During an active flare your fiber intake should depend on what you can tolerate, with amounts gradually increasing as symptoms improve.

“The goal is not to eat as much fiber as possible,” says Anya Argosh, MS, RD, a registered dietitian and the founder of Birchwell Clinic. “It’s to build as much dietary variety as your gut can comfortably and safely handle.”

A lower-fiber diet is often recommended during flares to give the GI tract a “rest.” However, Dahlia Marin, RDN, LD, CGN, a registered dietitian specializing in GI issues and co-founder of Married to Health, says limited evidence supports this approach for people without strictures (abnormal narrowings in the GI tract). “Historically, people with active Crohn’s were often told to follow a low-fiber diet, particularly when experiencing diarrhea, abdominal pain, or increased bowel movements. However, current research does not strongly support eliminating all fiber during a flare,” Marin says.

Instead, Marin recommends tailoring food choices to your symptoms and tolerance.

For example, if certain textures are hard for your body to handle, changing how you prepare foods may help.

Consider “choosing well-cooked, peeled, blended, or puréed fruits and vegetables rather than large amounts of raw, fibrous foods,” she says. That might mean roasting carrots or pears instead of eating them raw in a salad or opting for puréed vegetable soup or smoothie.

During a flare foods high in insoluble fiber—such as fruit and vegetable skins, fibrous leafy greens, nuts, seeds, popcorn, and corn on the cob—may also need to be temporarily limited until symptoms improve.

If you have a stricture, eating softer foods or modifying textures, limiting sources of insoluble fiber, and chewing thoroughly are especially important to help reduce the risk of an obstruction. However, fiber tolerance varies from person-to-person and if you have a stricture, you should work with your healthcare provider to come up with an individualized plan.

Kelly L. Burgess, MBA, RD, LDN, the founder of To Better Health LLC and the creator of the HealthyBites HealthyLife app, says she sometimes suggests a low-FODMAP diet during an active flare. This short-term approach may help people whose symptoms are triggered by FODMAPs, fermentable carbohydrates that can be hard on the gut. High-FODMAP foods include certain beans and legumes, as well as vegetables such as cabbage, cauliflower, garlic, and onions, which can cause symptoms like gas and bloating in some people.

A low-FODMAP diet is typically used on a temporary basis in people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) to identify symptom triggers and may help relieve symptoms in people with Crohn’s who have IBS-like symptoms.

Whether you’re coming off a flare or in remission, it’s best to take a “low and slow” approach to adding more fiber to your diet, Argosh says. Introducing too much too quickly can worsen symptoms, leaving you bloated and uncomfortable. “Pace your fiber—don’t force it,” Burgess says. “Begin with small amounts and increase them gradually as tolerated.” In practice, this might mean starting with small portions of well-tolerated fiber-rich foods spread throughout the day, then gradually expanding the types and amounts of fiber you eat at meals and snacks.

Some experts suggest a multi-step approach, starting with easy-to-tolerate, soft fruits, veggies, and grains—think potatoes, white rice, and bananas—and gradually progressing to harder-to-digest salad, seeds, corn, celery and dried fruit.

It’s worth noting that hydration should also increase as fiber intake rises to soften stools and prevent constipation.

When it comes to getting fiber, the dietitians agree that food is generally the best place to start. Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and other plant foods offer more than just fiber—they also provide other vitamins, minerals, and antioxidants that support overall health and may help with inflammation.

That said fiber supplements can sometimes come in handy, especially when you’re traveling or don’t have access to the foods you typically eat. Finding one that works for you may take some patience, though.

Marin says different supplements can affect your gut in very different ways. “For example, psyllium is a soluble, gel-forming fiber that holds water but is not heavily fermented, while fibers such as inulin and fructooligosaccharides are much more fermentable and can have a greater effect on the gut microbiome.” In practical terms these variations mean one supplement might sit just fine with you and another could leave you feeling gassy or bloated. Which brings us to…

If you’re having trouble eating enough, losing weight without trying, finding yourself cutting out more and more foods, or feeling unsure how to add fiber into your diet after a flare, consider seeing a registered dietitian who specializes in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). These professionals can help you get the nutrients you need while figuring out which foods and textures work best for you so you don’t unnecessarily restrict your diet. They can also check for nutrient deficiencies and help tailor your diet if you have a stricture, have had surgery for Crohn’s, or are dealing with other Crohn's complications.

A registered dietitian can also help you identify your trigger foods. Triggers generally vary from person to person, which can make them tricky to pin down. Some people tolerate oats and nuts, while others can’t eat either. The same goes for raw fruit and veggies, whole grains, and other fiber-containing foods. It comes down to trial and error: figuring out what works for you—and what doesn’t.

Your gastroenterologist should be involved if you have new or worsening symptoms, particularly if you’re experiencing persistent abdominal pain, frequent diarrhea, blood in your stool, unexplained weight loss, worsening fatigue, and/or vomiting. Get urgent medical attention if you have severe abdominal pain, repeated vomiting, abdominal swelling, or can’t pass stool or gas. These can be signs of an intestinal obstruction, which is especially important to watch for in people with Crohn’s-related strictures.

Young men are spending more money than ever on looking good—even on their grays. Rather than shopping for cheap cover-ups, they are forensically researching science-backed cosmetics. Part of this also means a rejection of chemical-heavy products and a more skeptical attitude towards unnecessary cosmetics. Hair dye, after all, is not entirely harmless.

Online communities like “Gray and Proud” and “Silver Revolution” have tens of thousands of members. Paul Mescal and Jeremy Allen White have also done their part for gray-hair representation among young(ish) people: The Times dubbed them “silver fox cubs.”

Maybe things really are shifting. But if the demand for anti-gray products is any indication, the revolution is still a way off. Anti-gray serums are becoming big business. The global market is expected to jump from around $1.3 billion to $2 billion by 2031, according to Deep Market Insights. Cosmetics Business magazine calls it the “next men’s grooming trend,” with growing interest in peptide-based products. On the internet, you see both sides. On Reddit, a 26-year-old guy shares a photo of his gray head of hair, saying that he’s already got more than his dad. The top comment reads: “Do your due diligence, of course—bloodwork, diet etc.” Someone else follows up with: “[Guys] with gray hair are [hot].”

One person making the case for Gen Z’s grey-haired men is model and musician Charlie Jeer. The 24-year-old first found fame on the Netflix dating show Too Hot to Handle. He has the chiseled jawline and cheekbones of a conventional heartthrob, but there’s another feature viewers can’t seem to get past: his hair. “I feel like it gets brought up in conversation every single day in my life,” he says.

Jeer first noticed the grays as a teenager, and mostly shrugged off the comments. Online, it’s a different story. “Why are you going gray?”; “Can’t tell if bro is 15 or 60; ” “Err this old man.” “It’s relentless,” he says.

Now, Jeer sees the grays as an opportunity to stand out. They’ve helped build his profile as a model, too, partly because of the “age ambiguity” they give him. He’s picked up credits with brands like Davidoff and Adidas. “There’s a massive commercial potential for me to make a black hair dye and just start marketing that,” he says. “I never did because I was like, ‘No, I’m proud of my hair the way it is.’”

On TikTok, Jeer takes on his critics with captions like: “Joke’s on them when I’m a silver fox by 30.” He really likes it when he hears from other young guys going gray. “I’ve had a little gray-haired community of people come forward and present themselves,” he says. “It’s nice to hear someone else say that they’ve had people come at them for being gray-haired and that they’re going to continue to wear it with confidence.”

Jeer might be on the young end of the gray-hair population, but as a guy, he’s riding a well-worn track. Men have always been afforded the privilege of associating their grays with looking more distinguished. You don’t have to be George Clooney (or a Too Hot to Handle contestant) to benefit from that. Women, meanwhile, aren’t part of that deal: gray hair is more often simply read as old, an obviously unfair double standard.

It still come with baggage for men, though: age, lost virility, fading relevance. “The first gray” is practically a trope in its own right—a visual shorthand in films, books and TV for a character beginning to age, often followed by the inevitable midlife crisis. There are dozens of those scenes, in everything from The Simpsons to Vanilla Sky.

Matt Newman is a New York–based hairstylist who hates his gray hair. “There are so many examples of men with premature grays that people love and adore,” he says. “But in your 20s, that might not be an aesthetic you’re ready to dive into.”

Perhaps that’s why Jackass star Johnny Knoxville waited until 49 to finally embrace his natural hair color. He started going gray in his 20s and dyed it for most of his life. Then, during lockdown, he asked his wife to give him a buzz cut to reveal the silver underneath, on Instagram. “I should start a challenge but I don’t want to call anyone out,” he wrote on Instagram. Going gray, declared one Mr. Porter headline, was now a “punk power move”.

That’s not quite how Saavan Varambhia, 25, a data analyst from Buckinghamshire, would have described it. Not at first, anyway. Like Chudley, he was at school when a group of female friends spotted his grays and tried to pluck them out. “I was like, ‘Hmm, I don’t know how I feel about this,’” he says. A few years later, he had enough gray hairs on his head to “stop people in their tracks.”

For Varambhia, the contrast was hard to miss. He’s South Asian, with naturally deep-black hair, so the grays stood out. The Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology reports that people of Asian descent also tend to go gray later, often in their late 30s. According to Professor Desmond Tobin, a professor of dermatological science at University College Dublin, many Eastern cultures have traditionally been less accepting of grays than the West, with men and women both more likely to dye them.

“At first, I felt like I didn’t want to seem a lot older than my peers,” Varambhia says. Over time, though, he came to see the silver as something that set him apart. “I’ve always been one to stand out, and it was another attributing factor to that.”

When there are too many gray hairs to pluck, do you reach for the Just for Men, or join the silver fox cub club?

Turning gray at any age can make you wonder why it’s happening. According to Tobin, the answer is usually in your DNA. “The first thing to do is just to look at your [previous] generation of relatives,” he says. “They’re going to give you a road map for where you’re going to be. And if you have early grayers—teens, early 20s—then that’s really mapped out quite starkly in the family.”

Other things can speed up the process, too. Stress is a big one. Tobin says it can damage the cells responsible for producing hair pigment, acting like a “sledgehammer” if the process has already begun. Just look at Barack Obama’s hair before and after his time in the White House. Nutrition can play a role, too, and a blood test can pick up any deficiencies. But Tobin says the nutritional deficiencies linked to premature graying tend to show up in countries where malnutrition is more widespread.

Crucially, though, Tobin says he hasn’t seen hard evidence that young people are going gray earlier across the board. Modern life might come with its share of chronic stressors, he points out, but humans have been enduring trying times since long before social media, climate change, and cost of living crises entered the picture.

Tobin is skeptical of the “overstated claims” made by supplements and serums that promise to “tickle your stem cells.” If you want to slow the process, he says, your best bet is the boring stuff: less stress, plenty of sleep and an antioxidant-rich diet. That might hold things off for a while. But for anyone genetically predisposed to graying, it might not make that much of a difference.

“Would maximum antioxidant intake really stop somebody from graying who’s genetically headed that way? It’s not likely to be a very significant contributor,” Tobin says. “It might, in a multi-prong way, add up to something, but if you’re graying significantly in your teens, that is probably the die that’s being cast for you.”

The good news? Going gray early doesn’t mean the rest of you is aging at the same rate. In fact, there may be a silver lining. People who go gray prematurely are more likely to keep their hair; “nearly all people who bald [do so] with their natural hair color,” says Tobin.

But for anyone who doesn’t want to live with their grays, the hunt for a treatment is on. Research into gray hair has surged alongside the wider boom in longevity science. Tobin says the past five years have felt like a return to the 1980s, when the topical hair-regrowth drug Rogaine first shook up the haircare world. He has consulted for startups developing anti-gray treatments aimed at reducing oxidative stress or extending the life of melanocyte stem cells. The science is promising, but so far, nothing has produced evidence strong enough to satisfy regulators.

Researchers are beginning to understand that graying may not be irreversible, as once thought. Melissa Harris at the University of Alabama has studied the melanocyte stem cells behind hair pigmentation, and immunotherapy as a way to reverse graying. Other research has shown that some hairs can spontaneously regain their pigment, with changes appearing to track periods of stress. It hints that some aspects of aging may be less linear than we assume.

Dr. Greg Williams, a hair restoration surgeon at the Farjo Hair Institute, has watched the field develop. He hosts conference sessions on gray hair and, a couple of years ago, set up a WhatsApp group where specialists around the world could swap ideas and compare notes. “Gray hair is very poorly understood,” he says. “There’s not a lot of research in humans around it.” Maybe that’s surprising, given how universal it seems to be. But commercial fallout is huge. The global hair-and scalp-care industry is worth nearly $9.4 billion, and Williams thinks anyone who figures it out “would be a Nobel Prize winner”.

As for Varambhia, the science seems to fit his experience. Genetics are clearly part of the picture: his dad’s hair has been white for years, and he still has a full head of it. Varambhia also suspects stress sped things up. His grays arrived rapidly as he settled into corporate nine-to-five life. Since hitting the gym regularly, he thinks the process may have slowed.

Knowing other people going through the same thing has helped. “I know eventually I’ll go fully gray. I hope it’s a bit later on than sooner,” he says. But when the time comes? “I’ll embrace it.”

#6766. Why Do We Keep Coming Back to Godzilla?

Like most people born in the western world after 1954, I knew of a monster named Godzilla before I’d heard of a country called Japan. It’s not a rare perspective. For as long as nearly any living person can remember, Godzilla has been hugely popular, far exceeding the global recognition afforded most leaders, athletes, and certainly his human co-stars, those men and women whose dramatic dealings are the universally agreed-upon bargain for apocalyptic havoc that millions upon millions have gladly paid to see for over 70 years.

What Godzilla hasn’t been is prestigious. The irradiated dinosaur of very few words—mainly deafening screams—is a beloved figure more in the key of Harry Potter, Batman, or James Bond than the loftier fare that typically gets recognized by major festivals or awards bodies. But exceptions to the rule do exist. The Criterion Collection issued Ishiro Honda's 1954 original on a deluxe Blu-ray in 2012 and marked their 1,000th release with a lavish set of Godzilla’s Shōwa era (1954-1975), which both documents film history and calls to mind John Huston’s Chinatown line about sticking around long enough to become respectable. Writing an introduction to Honda’s biography A Life in Film, from Godzilla to Kurosawa, Martin Scorsese deemed the series’ first director "an extraordinary artist and craftsman" and placed Honda’s 1954 film (alongside spin-offs Mothra and Rodan) among “pictures that haunted the imaginations of young moviegoers like myself and millions of others for years to come.” Kiyoshi Kurosawa—perhaps Japan’s greatest living filmmaker, and certainly its finest practitioner of austere horror—deemed Honda’s film an early lesson in national trauma. Thomas Pynchon, too, had ambitious plans to write a novel about the insurance adjuster who responds to Godzilla’s wreckage (the idea never fully materialized, though it did become a nice tangent in his 1990 novel Vineland). We can look at the films and countenance such appreciations from geniuses. In the meantime, good luck to anyone counting the exact number of action figures, coloring books, sticker packs, and lunchboxes boasting Godzilla and friends.

A critical shift started somewhere in the last decade. Toho ostensibly shelved Godzilla with 2004’s Final Wars, a grand finale that was the big-budget equivalent of a child smashing toys. (That is a compliment.) Godzilla LLC boasts enough films and ancillary products to be financially solvent until the sun burns out (or an indomitable monster rises from the sea), but that retirement was short-lived. Toho resurrected the franchise in aggressive form with 2016’s Shin Godzilla, both a complete series reboot and (across several stages, each grotesque and terrifying) character reinvention that dispensed with typical monster-movie hijinks. An unholy experiment that would make the series’ many mad scientists proud, Shin Godzilla fused the hilarious bureaucratic hallway-rushing of In the Loop or Veep with cosmic horror befitting its director, Hideaki Anno, whose contributions to Japanese culture are nearly Godzilla’s equal on the strength of Neon Genesis Evangelion alone. With time, it’s downright eerie to see Anno’s foreshadowing of the global unrest of Trump, Brexit, COVID, and our endless misinformation era. But rather than keep the great filmmaker on retainer for any number of sequels or spin-offs, Toho regrettably followed Shin Godzilla with an anime trilogy produced for Netflix, a rare class of films I’ve been too embarrassed to even log on Letterboxd.

Whether genius or dreck, none of these films quite anticipated 2023’s Godzilla Minus One. It’s one thing to become the highest-grossing Japanese-language release in U.S. history—any Godzilla movie that doesn't at least aim for such a distinction isn’t worth its salt. It’s harder to imagine anyone, even or especially the franchise’s most ardent fans, anticipated lavish public praise from a quintet of Best Director winners in Steven Spielberg (who claims to have seen the film at least three times), James Cameron (who offered to work on a sequel), Christopher Nolan, Guillermo Del Toro, and Bong Joon-ho. In a recorded conversation with Yamazaki, Tom Cruise and Christopher McQuarrie credited the film as an inspiration; the actor went so far as to call it “perfect.” Which is to say nothing of the fact that Godzilla Minus One earned the series its first Oscar, a VFX prize awarded to none other than writer-director Takashi Yamazaki—a nice encapsulation of the film’s balance between spirit and spectacle.

Because Godzilla Minus One resonated more than anyone could have anticipated, Godzilla Minus Zero, which arrives in theaters this weekend, shoulders the burden of expectation. The first shot across the bow was its world premiere at last month’s New York Film Festival, where Zero debuted alongside prestige fare like Luca Guadagnino’s Sam Altman biopic Artificial and Tony Gilroy’s star-studded Behemoth! There, NYFF’s artistic director Dennis Lim introduced the movie to a sold-out crowd at the Alice Tully Hall, praising Yamazaki for exemplifying "the endless possibilities of creative VFX and genre filmmaking." Minus Zero indeed thrives on spectacle: its opening sequence blazes a merciless path of carnage aided by an elegant long take, while subsequent scenes centered on one of Godzilla’s classic foes, the three-headed Ghidorah, inspire genuine terror more than cheap thrills. This is the first Japanese film shot for IMAX, a large-scale exhibition that’s often torqued for discomfort. Yamazaki’s centerpiece battle pitting the monster against a squadron of fighter planes is dogged by fatalistic doom: a mother and child screaming as their house crashes to the ground don’t get the last-minute salvation we’ve come to expect, and even the sentimental-seeming inclusion of a pilot named Honda is complicated by his eventual fate.

If not quite so grave as the visions of death that imprinted upon a young Kiyoshi Kurosawa, Yamazaki’s narrative—including a face-melting sequence that U.S. distributor GKIDS have asked not be spoiled—provides a stark contrast to the bloodless carnage of American blockbusters. It’s quite the cultural coup, too, that a film this brutal and generally skeptical of American imperialism has secured a simultaneous international release: every English-speaking actor (none recognizable, each portraying arrogant allied military) bears an amusingly off-market quality à la the nefarious white time-travelers of 1991’s altogether underrated Godzilla vs. King Ghidorah.

Nearly all Godzilla films are a total restart. Minus Zero is immediately notable as a direct sequel. The results suggest that Yamazaki overestimated his initial project. Godzilla is not much of a presence in Minus Zero, first appearing around the 50-minute mark and then again in a suitable, albeit brief climax. Far more time is spent with those we came to know and like during Minus One, each reprised by an entirely competent ensemble that fails to elevate interest higher than a certain co-star’s 50 meters. (The protagonists’ adopted daughter from Minus One returns here for the sole purpose of standing in place and crying.) Whatever its pretensions towards grandeur, this is still a film where a scientist explains his master plan by smashing together two monster figures. It takes little to delight in this moment, a literal and figurative collision of serious storytelling and childlike abandon. More of that spirit might’ve served the final picture.

My mild disappointment is nothing compared to the agony Godzilla has wreaked on the district of Ginza, which last week hosted the first public screenings of Minus Zero through the Tokyo International Film Festival. Godzilla’s annihilated the city’s landmarks enough times that when we think of all-out destruction on the sprawling megalopolis that is Tokyo, we’re probably picturing its uber-fashionable shopping sector laid to ruins. When he emerged from the sea in 1954, he wrecked the district’s historic Nichigeki Theater, which was again destroyed (in real life, of humans’ accord) and reopened as the Yurakucho Mullion building in 1984—the same year as The Return of Godzilla, where he inflicted yet more damage on the brand-new structure. Godzilla Minus One recreated the Nichigeki Theater in loving detail only to destroy it again in a sequence that deployed modern visual effects to cause maximum tactility and human misery. That Hiroshima-like site is revisited in Minus Zero, leading one character to declare that “Ginza is again the battleground.” Time’s healed those wounds, however. The night before Minus Zero’s Japanese premiere, a small crowd gathered outside the Yurakucho Mullion’s Seiko watch for an extravagant display: the clock lifted, and out came a small set of Godzilla puppets that strike chimes while Akira Ifukube’s iconic theme plays.

Ginza’s Wako Clock Tower has fared no better: it was torn to ribbons in the 1954 original, 1995’s Godzilla vs. Destoroyah, Shin Godzilla, and Minus One. After a time you must wonder what keeps bringing Godzilla back to the neighborhood. Is it animal instinct, or mere coincidence, or an atomic hatred for its glowing consumerism? The Wako Annex, a nearby pastry shop that Google not-inaccurately describes as “chic,” currently advertises a Christmas-themed cake recreation of the oft-doomed Clock Tower with Minus Zero’s monster looming over it; accompanying text informs us that “Godzilla strikes Ginza’s Wako once again.” The nearby Toho cinema is within Hibiya Godzilla Square, featuring a small but impressively detailed statue and a plaque reading, “The human race must coexist with Godzilla.” Immediately adjacent is a three-story shopping mall hosting a Toho store selling numerous Godzilla ephemera. (I bought the tea cup.)

Even Americans accustomed to the fetishization of disaster would be impressed by Japan’s ability to marry suffering and spectacle to great financial gain. Perhaps it’s the same haunted fascination of a young Martin Scorsese or Kiyoshi Kurosawa cast unto the world at large; Godzilla might be scary as he destroys Ginza, but he’s something to admire when shrunk down to human size and displayed inside the Yurakucho Mullion’s lobby. (The photo-snapping crowds that gather every time I walk past suggest that bit of marketing paid off.) Even as Minus Zero’s first reviews show a less rapturous response than Minus One, this series’ viability—and its newfound prestige—shows little indication of flagging. Ginza’s residents were beyond happy to see Godzilla, at least in ossified, action-figure form. But who's to say they wouldn't be just as excited if, one day, his path of destruction broke through the screen?

It is exciting because it’s like theater for people with ADD. You sit, you wait, you’re excited, and then 12 minutes later, it’s done.

And you’re like, “Oh, I witnessed this cultural experience, and I didn’t have to sit through two and a half hours and an intermission.”

It’s true. I didn’t have to watch a whole feature film. You’re so mad at me for making a movie. You’re like, “Why wasn’t it a short?”

I find it to be. I’m proud that, in the beginning screenings, I wasn’t telling people the runtime. I’d ask them afterward, and they’d say, “It felt like 1:40.”

I went with my 23-year-old club-kid colleague, and it was as if I were watching a foreign film. Edward was translating for me. We also discovered afterward that Edward had received an email asking him to be an extra.

Every gay in New York City did. It’s very much that Madison Beer meme: “I was supposed to be in the video.” Every gay guy in New York.

We had two amazing producers I brought on, Zach Stone and Ben Hubley, who were already in the party scene. I couldn’t have done it without them.

I went to college with Zach Stone, but anyway…

He’s great. He’s the best. They basically sent out mass texts and emails to everyone they’d ever partied with, and people showed up. It was actually a beautiful part of the process, seeing the community show up for creativity. It moved me to see everyone in one space together. They were there to have a good time and party.

I want to know what your relationship with club culture is. I think of you as an LA person, both because of I Love LA and before. Why did you set this in New York club culture rather than LA club culture?

To me, New York club culture has so much history. We’re standing on the backs of giants. From Studio 54 to the ’80s to the actual Club Kids days in the ’90s, it has such a lineage and such a vibe. LA has things going on, but it’s a bit less specific. I also think the idea of marathon partying has changed. I say in the movie, “It’s the Berlinification of New York, mama.” I think that’s happened, where marathon-style partying, starting on Friday and going until Sunday, is a new thing for New York.

Yeah. People really stay out all night. You end up at an afters, then go to another party. I don’t think it’s really meant to be in New York because New York isn’t Berlin. You have to hustle all week. You have to work really hard. I just heard a girl in the Vogue bathroom screaming at someone, “You need to keep your phone on!” So there’s a different vibe. She’s going to watch this and be like, “Oh, shit.”

But I think there’s a different grind in this city. When you put that next to the party scene, it creates a hamster wheel that I think a lot of gay men are living through, loving, but also having problems with. It was interesting for me to set the movie here. Also, New York and a kid are cinematic. There’s a lineage of movies like this that feel cozy, movies I wanted to replicate from the ’70s, like Kramer vs. Kramer.

Gloria, Kramer vs. Kramer, and obviously Big Daddy, which we’ve gotten a lot of comparisons to. There’s also a movie called One Fine Day. Do you remember that?

That was a visual reference. Uptown Girls, obviously. Such a classic. I wanted to be part of that canon, and I felt like a queer retelling of the story was deserved and made sense. We have this delayed coming of age as gay men. We don’t have the same pressures to have kids or settle down, so we keep partying. But eventually that becomes an issue, even for your body. It’s a hard burden to carry, continuing to party every weekend.

When I’ve taken writing classes, they always say that novelists write what they know in their first novel. They write something that feels true. What’s the germ of inspiration for this story? I don’t think you actually have a club kid, but where are you coming from?

It came from a very simple idea. I was at a dinner party with friends who had an eight-year-old son. I was saying inappropriate things in front of him and making him laugh, and the parents were laughing too. I thought there might be something funny in the dynamic between me and a kid.

I feel like a kid in a lot of ways. I think I have a childlike spirit, and I’ve always gotten along with kids. I really like them and relate to them. That was the germ of the idea. Then I had a boyfriend in Berlin, so I was there a lot, partying. It was really fun, but the comedowns were hard, and I think it took away from things.

2021–2024. I basically wrote the first draft before the breakup, and it got really good after the breakup. I’ve been calling it my Adele breakup album.

It is a breakup movie in a lot of ways. There’s a feeling of love in the movie, and I think that comes from the love I had and have for my ex, the times we spent together, and the parties we had together. I see it as a love letter to him. We haven’t spoken since, and I hear he’s doing well. I want him to do well. I felt like I had a lot of love to put on the page, and that’s when it got really good.

I read that you crashed real parties during filming and brought crew members with you. What did that look like? Paint the scene.

I didn’t crash. There were weeks of discussion with the party planners. My friend Noah Shakoor throws a party called Pit Stop, and he was like, “Come shoot the party.” Really, all you have to do is put up a sign saying you’re being filmed. There’s also a very famous afters in New York that goes until 3:00 p.m. the next day. It starts at 7:00 p.m. on Saturday and ends at 3:00 p.m. Sunday.

What do you do for fake bundle or kundle?

There’s actually a funny story here. There’s a scene where I’m supposed to be selling drugs at the party.

For listeners at home, bundle is cocaine.

Bundle is the original. Everything stems from bundle. Kundle is K, gundle is GHB, which I heard you didn’t know about.

You shouldn’t try GHB. I love you, but I don’t think you should try that drug.

I’ve been told I’d be even more insane. People have said I have no chill.

I think molly is good for you. I think you’re a molly girl.

Feel it out. Feel the music. Hug someone. That’s the vibe. Mundle, molly.

Now there are all the numbers—3-MMC, 4-MMC—so thrundle, frundle.

This is too much. You’re supposed to remember this when you’re high?

Everything is undle, so you just add the first initial.

What about ayahuasca? Is there ayawundle?

Oh, wow. I’m going to be using that all week.

I’m imagining the Woody Allen big sneeze scene. Tell me about people doing fake cocaine.

Here’s the story. I was selling fake drugs, and people knew there was a camera there, but they didn’t really know what was happening. They thought something else. They were high. I gave one girl a bump, and she said, “What the fuck? That’s not K.” She started getting really mad at me. I told her it was fake drugs, just powdered lactose. She said, “I’m lactose intolerant.” I actually ended up drugging someone with fake drugs by giving them that.

I had powdered vitamin D or C and powdered lactose. I found that the lactose looked the most like coke. The vitamin C looked more like K. It was a little saltier-looking. There’s a lot of trickery. Diego, our props guy, shout-out. What a fun job for the props department.

We talk a lot at Vogue about the different etiquette for different situations. What is club etiquette?

I think club etiquette is actually very boundaried and respectful. If you lose control at the club, it’s not a good look. Especially in Berlin. When I was new to Berlin, my friend was DJing behind the decks. I was trying to say hi and get his attention, and someone told me no. It’s a no-no to distract the DJ. There are definitely rules. You don’t want to be messy. Having a good attitude is important, too. You don’t want to be a bitch.

Anytime I’m in a group and people have an attitude, I’m like, “What are we doing here?” We’re here to have a good time. Be open to new people. Read the room. If someone doesn’t want to talk to you, move on. Those are my rules at the club.

Those are good rules. You also cast a lot of people who are club habitués. How did you find these individuals? What was it like working with people who aren’t actors? This was also your first directing experience.

I did everything they say you shouldn’t do on your first film. I worked with a kid, worked with non-actors, shot on film, shot at real parties, and shot in New York. There were days when I thought, Why did I do this to myself? But as far as the non-actors go, they just came. We had so much history together by that point. We were friends, and I’d been partying with Saturn for years. I’d also partied with Alaska [Riley].

She always felt like such a maternal figure in the LA party scene, but also had this power and confidence while being warm. I always wanted that for the character. The movie is about the different types of families we can create. Saffron is a mother figure to Arlo in a lot of ways.

She shockingly convinced me, as someone with a four- and six-year-old, that it was a good idea for him to DJ.

That’s the trick of the movie. You see it and you’re like, “Sure, we’re making this argument.” Then the second time, you’re like, “This is a terrible plan.” That was intentional. I wanted you to feel like you’re watching this kid learn and express himself creatively, and you’re like, “Yes, yes, yes.” Then you’re like, “No, no, no.”

It’s interesting to think about because there’s obviously a way to nurture creativity, even in a DJ setting, without actually bringing a kid to the club. There are so many merits to having these queer ideas in a kid’s life, while also being realistic and practical. That’s the whole thing with Peter. He’s doing the baseline of parenting, which is love. He’s also a pushover to everyone, especially Arlo. He just wants Arlo to have everything he wants.

I love him. This kid is such a star. I hear he also teased and bullied you.

I gave him permission. He would say things like, “You’re being really cringe right now,” or, “That’s such a dad joke.” I became the embarrassing dad to him. Now I do things to make him cringe because I enjoy it. I feel like a dad to him sometimes, and it’s fun. I think that’s what made him comfortable on set: being able to make fun of me.

It’s hard to work with a young person in that way, and also to get to know them.

That’s one thing I hope people take away from the movie: having a more honest relationship with kids. It’s something I wish I’d had when I was younger. I didn’t know my parents were people. That never occurred to me. I don’t think they really saw me as a person, either. They saw me as their kid. I wish I’d known more about their struggles or what they were feeling inside. I think it would’ve taught me more about what life was going to be like. We have these barriers between parents and kids that I think we could soften a little.

I want to know whether Reggie is actually into music. I was impressed with how seamless it was.

Yes, majorly. But his music taste has completely changed. He’s at that age where he’s impressionable. He came in loving Slipknot and Metallica. He was like, “I like the hard stuff.” Now he loves Cocteau Twins and Massive Attack. His whole playlist has changed. We have a shared playlist now.

Wow. Did you start that during the movie?

We started it a bit after the movie, but obviously the movie inspired it. It’s really sweet to watch him change through this experience. Watching him start doing press and interviews, he’s become so mature, grown-up, and funny. I feel like a proud parent. I understand why parents cry watching the school play. You’re like, “Oh my God, this is my kid. He’s up there doing it, and he’s so good.”

He’s so good. I hear you guys have been DJing together.

He DJs, I’m the hype man. I’m in the back. I don’t know all the knobs and stuff. Saturn taught him the basics. He knows his highs, lows, and mids.

Absolutely. I also heard that you brought the straight film crew to Basement and were teaching them some lingo. What was that Duolingo lesson like?

Duolingo, cundle edition. We need to get the Duolingo owl.

That’s an A24 idea. Are you here? That’s a really good idea. Pay her some money. You have a good job; you don’t need the extra funds. I’ll take the Duolingo license.

The question was about bringing your straight crew to Basement and teaching everyone.

They just had the best time. It’s not like they’d never partied before, but exposing them to the exact type of queer people the movie was about was important. I wanted everyone to deeply understand the details. The movie comes alive with the details, even the types of furniture a New York gay guy has in his apartment. We have Butt magazine, and we have our own specific things.

A lot of chrome right now. There’s one kind of chain-link table that a lot of gay guys have. It’s a lot of chrome and black. Black leather couch. Chic, but also a little grimy.

I want to know if you ever considered having someone else play Peter.

Keanu Reeves. No, this was always my goal and my dream. I thought of people like Lena Dunham, Donald Glover, Phoebe Waller-Bridge. I knew I had a voice, and I knew I could act a bit, so it was always the plan.

I’m always impressed with people who can do all three. You wrote it, directed it, and acted in it. How did you move between those three roles?

I tried to think of it like Pandora’s box. The movie was the box, and everything went into it. All my jobs were funneling into the project. I tried not to separate my actor brain from my writer brain or my director brain. If I did that, I’d start to freak out and lose control of the whole thing. Everything was in service of getting all the ideas, vision, and craft into one thing.

That’s such a good question. I think it’s both a mistake I made and something I don’t regret. You can tell in a club scene when people are faking dancing. I had a looped beat and thought we should dance to it to get the energy up. My sound guy said we really needed to do takes without sound because the edit would be a nightmare. The edit was a nightmare because you have to go beat by beat. But the crowd had more energy because the music was playing. It was a double-edged sword: harder in the edit, but better for the movie.

You’ve cast a lot of your friends in your projects. Do you ever find it difficult to work with friends? Is it harder to give direction?

I found that both the party host in me and the people-pleaser in me conflicted with directing. I wanted everyone to have a good time. Part of shooting is doing a lot of takes. It’s not comfortable and it’s annoying. Some of my friends didn’t realize that they’d do a scene full-out, and I’d say, “That’s the wide, and we’re not really going to use the wide.” By the time we got to their coverage, they didn’t have the same energy as they did in the wide.

Especially in those group scenes, where I had every cool gay guy in New York, excluding your friend who didn’t show up to work. I felt bad asking them to do 10 takes of dancing. They were sweaty and tired and they weren’t getting paid that much. That was the hardest part: getting what I needed as a director while keeping everyone happy.

I want to know about your crazy T-shirt collection. That was one of my favorite things. We’ve been talking a lot on the Vogue fashion team about how there’s a real slogan T-shirt renaissance right now.

What’s your favorite slogan T-shirt in the rotation right now?

I have some things I’m making. We made an “Innit Babes” one for the movie. We’re going to make a “Mama, Let’s Research” one.

I saw you made “I Boofed at Cannes and All I Got Was This Lousy Shirt.”

That was a good one. I’m going to go with “I Boofed at Cannes” because people didn’t understand what either of the two T-shirts I made for Cannes meant. There was “I Need a Doll Line and a Sickening Dose Stat” and “I Boofed at Cannes and All I Got Was This Lousy Shirt.” The beauty of them was that no one understood either of them.

I loved your character in I Love LA. You’re a stylist. What did you do to research for that role? Are there stylists you look to?

There’s one stylist whose aesthetic I’d been looking at. I won’t say who it is. In LA, you’re around a lot of creative people. Being friends with Charli, you see her whole glam team. Makeup people and hair people have their own personalities. Stylists have their own personalities.

I’ve been around a lot of stylists, so I was channeling some specific behaviors. But I was also thinking about the loneliness you can have as a gay stylist. You’re basically a paid best friend to these celebrities. You’re there when they need you, but then at a certain point you have to leave and go do more work for them. Sometimes they yell at you. Sometimes you mess up, and sometimes things aren’t perfect. It’s a hard job being a stylist, and you don’t get a lot of gratitude from the world or sometimes even the people you work with.

I Love LA has started production on season two, or not yet?

Ended. It comes out very soon. I don’t think I can say the date, but soon. Sooner than you could ever imagine.

What’s the vibe on set with that crew? Do you have group chats? How much are you guys in touch?

All the time. We’ve become family, and the set is so easy and fun. We improvise, help each other with our looks, and it’s definitely family vibes now.

I want to know what your cultural diet is right now. Have you seen the Elizabeth Holmes documentary?

I have. I was in the room at Telluride when they dropped it.

It was a pure, unadulterated gag. I can’t remember the last time I was squeezing someone’s hand like that. I couldn’t believe it. The “Who’s on First?” thing really got me.

Sorry. Never mind. It was a once-in-a-lifetime kind of thing. It only would have worked with those two people together. There aren’t two other people who could make that movie work. I think it’s a real accomplishment.

Remi Wolf. Her new album is so good. She has a song called “I Won’t Cry” that’s one of the best love songs I’ve ever heard. I’m also getting into the new Troye Sivan album. “Fucking Forever,” another good love song. I’m newly in love, maybe, so I’m into love songs right now.

What’s the dream date when you’re newly in love?

I actually like a party. I love a classic walk. If we’re in Paris, walking, eating, and sitting by the river is always romantic. New York, too. A little rain never hurt anyone. I love a first date with kissing. Sitting at a bar for a long time and just kissing is really hot to me.

Okay. Let’s do it. Then I have to run upstairs.

Who’s the best type of person to party with?

Strappy. We’ve seen it. Strappy and midriff. We’ve seen it.

I’m such a gay guy. I think a white tank top is hot and looks good. A black tank top, too. Shorts. Athletic shorts are so hot at the club.

That’s when you want loosey-goosey, not cracky.

Loosey-goosey, not cracky-wacky. You’re not only learning lingo, you’re inventing it. I’m helping gay culture. You’re really pushing the culture forward.

An espresso martini at the club is kind of a vibe.

That doesn’t conflict with the mundle or the trundle?

If you’re doing G, you can’t drink anything. If I’m being real, a crisp Diet Coke. Or a mate. In Berlin, they have Club-Mate. Mate and vodka is really nice.

Favorite post-club snack? This is where our interests can overlap.

You know what? I like a soup. Something nourishing, like a vegetable soup or pho. You want to go fried, but your body actually wants something nourishing.

Let’s go pho. You say to someone, “Let’s go pho. Let’s get out of here and go pho.”

#6758. Montana’s Newest Congressman Dresses Like a Ralph Lauren Model

You couldn’t ask for a better embodiment of the blue wave than Sam Forstag, the 32-year-old rookie Montana Democrat who defeated his Republican rival in yesterday’s midterms to flip an important House seat. While unaffordability was a key issue in the midterms across the country, it’s especially noticeable in Montana, where an influx of billionaires and tech companies has sent the cost of living soaring. Like many of his compatriots, Forstag campaigned on promises of affordable housing, better healthcare, and getting money out of politics. Unlike his fellow Democratic challengers, however, he did it in a wardrobe that looked straight off a rack at the RRL store.

Last night, in front of a crowd of supporters in Missoula, Forstag delivered his victory speech dressed in a hickory-striped work shirt, jeans, a big western belt buckle, and a pair of weathered brown forestry boots. Forstag’s fit stands in contrast to the standard election night business-casual look favored by political hopefuls on both sides of the aisle, but he’s far from the first candidate to lean into the dress codes of blue-collar Americans. From Tim Walz’s camo hats and Filson Mackinaw jackets to John Fetterman’s Carhartt hoodie, workwear remains a potent symbol of working-class solidarity. In this case, however, it’s not just blue-collar cosplay.

Forstag at a student get-out-the-vote gathering at Montana State University in October.

Straight out of college and looking to pay off his student loans, Forstag joined the US Forest Service as a wildland firefighter and spent the next eight years battling blazes around the country, including four years as a smokejumper parachuting into fire zones. As a result, he comes by his love of Levi’s and sturdy, Goodyear-welted footwear honestly, and—Montana being a place where jeans and work shirts are considered business-casual for many—didn’t need to change his look for the campaign.

Forstag addressing voters at a town hall in August.

As authentic as Forstag’s election-night outfit was, he also appears to be a guy who understands the power of styling. While his shirt likely didn’t have Ralph Lauren’s initials on the tag (wildland firefighters only make $20 an hour), the way he wore it—half unbuttoned, layered over a red thermal henley, and tucked into his jeans—owes a debt to the romantic take on Western style Lauren has been trafficking in since the ’70s. Or, equally likely, the Montana cowboys who the designer borrowed it from. Either way, Forstag’s look, like the progressive politics he and his Democratic colleagues campaigned on, will be a welcome disruption to business as usual on Capitol Hill.

#6741. Thanksgiving Cornbread

Traditionally, cornbread stuffing requires making cornbread, cubing it and letting it dry out, mixing it with stock, eggs, meat, and aromatics, then transforming it into a stuffing (or dressing). This process produces some truly memorable casseroles, but I have an alternative that streamlines the process without compromising flavor: stuffing cornbread. It still smells enticingly like the classic Thanksgiving side as you set the baking dish on the table. I’ve still packed in a medley of woodsy herbs, sweet onions, vegetal peppers, and savory sausage. And it still slices into lofty servings to perch on your plate next to the sweet potatoes and green beans. It just takes half as long to make.

The intentional two-to-one ratio of cornmeal to flour in this cornbread results in a moist, tender crumb that still has enough structure to hold up against the sturdy mix-ins. The sugar might seem like a lot, but it balances out the savory sausage and aromatics perfectly.

For more Thanksgiving recipes, tips, menus, and inspiration, head to our 2026 Thanksgiving Hub.

Place a rack in middle of oven; preheat to 400°. Line a 13x9" baking pan with parchment paper, leaving generous overhang on long sides. Coat pan and parchment with unsalted butter. Melt ½ cup (1 stick) unsalted butter in a large skillet over medium-low heat, then pour into a small bowl.

Cook 1 green bell pepper, halved, ribs and seeds removed, finely chopped, 1 medium white onion, finely chopped, and 2 celery stalks, finely chopped, in same skillet, stirring occasionally with a wooden spoon, until softened, about 4 minutes. Add 1 lb. spicy and/or sweet Italian sausage, casings removed, and cook, breaking up and stirring occasionally, until browned (it may still be a little raw in the center at this point), about 5 minutes. Remove from heat.

Stir together 4 cups (624 g) fine-ground yellow cornmeal, 2 cups (250 g) all-purpose flour, 1 cup (200 g) sugar, 1 Tbsp. plus 1½ tsp. baking powder, 2 tsp. Diamond Crystal or 1¼ tsp. Morton kosher salt, 1 tsp. baking soda, and ¼ cup finely chopped sage, thyme, and/or rosemary in a large bowl. Gradually pour in 3½ cups buttermilk, then melted butter, stirring constantly until combined. Add 4 large eggs one at a time, mixing to incorporate after each addition. Fold in sausage mixture, then scrape batter into prepared pan.

Bake cornbread, rotating pan front to back halfway through, until golden brown and top springs back when gently pressed, 38–45 minutes. Let cool in pan 15 minutes. Using parchment paper overhang, lift cornbread out of pan and transfer to a cutting board. Cut into 12–18 slices; arrange on a platter.

Do Ahead: Cornbread can be baked 4 hours ahead. Store at room temperature; cut just before serving.
