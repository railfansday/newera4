#6999. OpenAI Intros Dots, AI Agents that Continue Working Between User Conversations

OpenAI has introduced Dots, GPT-6 Astra-powered agents that run on their own cloud computers, using connected applications to carry on with assignments between conversations and incorporating users' feedback as they go.

OpenAI is also previewing specialist Dots for organizations, with separate identities and credentials for defined responsibilities. Those enterprise deployments are beginning with focused pilots. The company is working with Microsoft on integration with Agent 365's governance and security controls.

The broader ambition seems to be to make delegation last beyond a chat session. That change could reduce the need to repeatedly explain a project. It also makes supervision a continuing obligation: An instruction that was appropriate yesterday may no longer fit today's circumstances.

Users currently cannot inspect or directly edit individual Dot memories. Deleting a Dot deletes its own context, while files and conversations it created remain separately stored. Plugin permissions are shared across Dots, ChatGPT, ChatGPT Work, and Codex.

For an organization, that creates two related governance questions: what an agent can retrieve now, and what it already knows. Revoking a connection addresses the first without necessarily resolving the second.

OpenAI's safety account distinguishes authorized tasks that continue in the background from unsolicited "proactive research." The latter uses tools restricted to reading permitted sources. Those research tasks cannot directly send messages, change connected application content, or control a browser or desktop. Any subsequent action must pass the usual rules and checks.

A separate system, Auto-review, checks certain planned actions against instructions and safety requirements. OpenAI says the agent cannot modify the controls enforcing those checks.

"Dots can still make mistakes," the company cautions.

OpenAI's Dots system-card appendix acknowledges that persistence and multi-agent systems are not new. Its evaluations examine how continuing work affects authorization boundaries.

In one test involving successive related tasks, moderate scope violations increased from 8.6% to 19.7% when the number of intervening tasks doubled from five to 10. Examples included carrying information between unrelated tasks or editing a shared document beyond the intended scope. The company reported no severe breaches or exfiltration in that evaluation.

These are controlled test results, not measured failure rates in customer deployments. They nevertheless identify a problem that a successful demonstration cannot settle: whether an agent keeps the right boundaries as its workload accumulates.

According to OpenAI's DevDay recap, Dots are available to Pro and Business Premium users in eligible markets. Enterprise, Edu, and Healthcare access is an administrator-enabled beta, off by default.

The operational question is how organizations will keep delegated authority current. A continuing assignment needs a human owner who can revise its scope and decide when to stop it. Otherwise, the convenience of leaving work with an agent can outlast the reason it was authorized.

#6997. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#6993. Claude Intros Sonnet 5.5 with Model-Switching Cybersecurity Safeguards

Anthropic's new Claude release, Sonnet 5.5, brings cybersecurity safeguards that can change which model handles a request during a Claude conversation. Higher-risk tasks can fall back to Sonnet 5.

According to Anthropic's explanation of model switching, automatic fallback is enabled by default in Claude. Users receive a notice, and the answer is labeled with the model that provided it. The chat then stays on Sonnet 5 unless the user changes the selection.

The company identifies penetration testing and exploit generation among activities that may trigger cybersecurity fallbacks. Source-code vulnerability scanning remains supported. Biology and reasoning-extraction safeguards can block requests outright, rather than route them elsewhere.

The distinction matters operationally. A block stops the request; a fallback allows an attempt to continue with a different model. Neither outcome should be treated as interchangeable with completion by the model the user originally chose.

Anthropic says Sonnet 5.5 generates output more than 30% faster than Sonnet 5 and can reduce cost per task by up to 30%, based on its testing. Token prices remain unchanged.

Those claims describe performance under the company's test conditions. An organization evaluating the service also needs to examine what happens when its own assignments encounter safeguards.

An internal pilot could record how often work is interrupted, whether fallback answers are usable, and when users need help. Such measurements would give buyers a more relevant basis for deployment than assuming every task receives the advertised model's capabilities.

Anthropic has announced plans to expand its Cyber Verification Program with tiered access for Sonnet 5.5, Opus 5.5, and Mythos models. Its current program guidance explicitly excludes the two new 5.5 models and says the expansion is forthcoming.

Organizations should distinguish those planned arrangements from access available today.

The broader enterprise issue is accountability. When an AI service changes models during an assignment, someone still needs to determine whether the resulting work is suitable. A visible handoff helps, but organizations must decide how employees should respond to it.

#6985. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

This option might be tough to imagine, given that kitchens are typically painted in a matte or eggshell finish. But that doesn’t mean that a shinier upgrade is out of the question. “High-gloss kitchens can be in a small or large space. For a smaller space, a butler's pantry might be a great place to play,” notes Danielle Davis, founder and principal designer of 56th Street Design Studio. “A larger kitchen will likely have a lot of millwork, so high-gloss paint can be applied to surfaces where it can really shine.”

If you want to make a statement in your kitchen using the budget-friendly quality of paint, a high-gloss finish could be the right move. Here’s how the pros recommend pulling it off.

When you’re considering a high-gloss finish, think of how it can be used to complement the rest of your kitchen. If you’re leaning toward trending darker palettes—like chocolate browns, emerald greens, and deep plums—then a high gloss will help bounce light around the room and make undertones more noticeable throughout the day. But if you’re doing just fine with a neutral backdrop, then a high-gloss will only make that look brighter. Either way, “high gloss” is paint speak for highlighter.

“The way that light reflects off a high-gloss finish adds a brightness that can make the space feel more expansive, even in moodier tones,” says Ariel Fischer, who is also a principal at Noa Blake Design.

Fischer and Goldberg would likely pick a darker shade on kitchen cabinetry for this effect, which Goldberg notes works on ultramodern panels and more ornate fronts. But Jen Levy, interior designer at Surrounded by Color, says it’s OK to go lighter—while looking up. “I love a high gloss ceiling in a kitchen,” Levy says. “My team and I see it used either way, but my preference is to have this paint treatment punctuate a lighter, brighter space.”

Paint is often regarded as DIY-friendly, but choosing this finish probably shouldn’t involve you rolling up your sleeves. “Hire a professional,” Levy says. “High gloss involves a lot of prep work to look right.” Since the results should almost resemble a mirror, Davis echoes this advice. “If a surface isn't perfectly sanded or smooth, high-gloss will show those imperfections,” she says. “It will ultimately take away from that perfect finish you’re after.”

As far as the best location for high-gloss paint, Davis has one strong preference. “High-gloss cabinets, from a practical standpoint, are forgiving in terms of wear,” she says. “You can easily wipe fingerprints, spills, and food right off the cabinet with a microfiber cloth.” Keep in mind, though, that chips will be noticeable, so this probably isn’t the right finish for busy households with young children.

As you determine the remaining details of your kitchen with a high-gloss paint in mind, remember to keep it front and center. Everything else in this space has to complement that sheen in order for the effect to be successful. “In a rustic kitchen, or one where natural wood is prevalent, high-gloss paint may clash with the overall vibe,” Davis says. The opposite is also true: A modern kitchen with too many shiny surfaces will feel like a carnival fun house.

To pull your kitchen together, seek out countertops and flooring with a matte finish alongside lighting and hardware that has a slight sheen. The goal is to find ways to ground the room, even as you center its high-gloss boldness. “I would love to have a client who’s daring enough to do a high-gloss kitchen,” Davis says. “Nevertheless, high-gloss paint can still have a timeless quality, while still having a big impact on the room.”

“Our lifestyle managers are the lifeblood of Quintessentially,” says Lauren Wilt, U.S. CEO of Quintessentially. And learning the ins and outs of their clients’ idiosyncrasies is the key to their business.

“There isn’t another concierge company that gets to know their clients the way we do,” says Wilt. “This isn’t a self-service booking platform with a suite of benefits. We are a highly personalized, proactive service.”

Proactive being the operative word, and fashion offers plenty of opportunity for a lifestyle manager’s creative problem-solving. “Our members often come to us saying, ‘I’ve heard about this experience—hook me up and book it,’” said Wilt. “But it’s our job to tell them when to go, when to avoid the crowds, and sometimes that the experience they’ve seen on Instagram isn’t actually the experience for them.”

The real luxury isn’t the Birkin, the hotel suite, or the private fitting—it’s in never having to wonder how to make it all happen.

#6806. What Is an Upper Bleph? Here’s What to Know About the Eyelid Surgery

Doctors explain how the procedure works, when a brow lift may be needed, and nonsurgical options.

In the world of plastic surgery, the nose, breasts, and butt have commanded a lot of time and attention under the knife. But as tech advances and patients seek out more subtle ways to manage aging skin, doctors have shifted their focus to other areas, some once deemed too risky to touch. Blepharoplasty, or bleph, for short, is a plastic surgery procedure that trims, redistributes, or removes excess skin on the eyelids.

The procedure can be done to help improve peripheral vision or for more cosmetic purposes, like removing hooded eyelids. Blephs can be done on the eyelid itself, which is known as an upper bleph, or a lower bleph, which is done below the eye to treat puffy or saggy skin. Even the most effective eye creams and potent skin care formulas sometimes may not be enough to correct excess skin around the eyes, but before you schedule your consultation, see what doctors have to say about the procedure to determine if it’s the best option for you right now.

An upper bleph specifically addresses the eyelid, where skin and sometimes fat closest to the nose are removed. Dr. Lyle Leipziger, chief of plastic surgery at North Shore University Hospital and LIJ Medical Center in New York, explains that the procedure involves removing skin and excising or repositioning deeper fatty tissue to address droopiness, puffiness, and under-eye bags. “As we age, the skin around the eyes naturally loses elasticity, which can cause the upper eyelids to droop, look heavy, or create a tired appearance. This can also be exacerbated by excess upper eyelid fatty tissue,” says Leipziger. During surgery, he explains, excess skin is removed, and surgeons may also remove or reposition small amounts of fat to create a smoother, more defined upper eyelid contour. “The goal is to open up the eyes and create a brighter, more refreshed appearance while preserving the patient’s natural eye shape,” he adds.

Dr. Lesley Rabach, a board-certified facial plastic surgeon and co-founder of Rabach Aesthetics in NYC, says that the best candidates for an upper bleph are people who have a “normal” eyebrow position with excess skin, also known as dermatochalasia. “When considering an upper bleph, your surgeon needs to look at the eyebrow position, eyelid skin, and fat, as well as how much the eye opens when determining if an upper bleph is the correct procedure,” she notes. Rabach says that sometimes a brow lift and/or ptosis repair—which reconstructs the eyelid muscles or connects it to the forehead muscle to lift it—is part of the surgery as well.

Additionally, Leipziger adds that blephs are ideal for individuals who have excess skin or fullness in their upper eyelids that makes their eyes look heavy, tired, or aged. “This is especially common as we get older and lose elasticity in the delicate skin around the eyes,” he points out. “Ideal candidates are generally in good overall health, have realistic expectations, and are bothered by drooping upper eyelid skin and possible fatty tissue.” He recommends having a consultation with a board-certified plastic surgeon to determine whether overhanging skin, excess fatty tissue, brow position, or another anatomical factor is contributing to the appearance of the upper eyelids or if it’s something else.

Rabach says the results are both subtle and obvious at the same time. “They remove the extra skin that often has a crepey texture while restoring the youthful appearance of the eyelid,” she says. “An upper bleph should restore the natural youthful architecture of the upper eyelid that is natural to the person.”

By removing excess skin and, according to Leipziger, when appropriate, repositioning small amounts of fat from the upper eyelids, the procedure helps create a more open and defined eye area. “Patients should experience a significant improvement with their eyes looking brighter and less tired, without ever looking ‘done’” says Leipziger. He also notes that while an upper bleph usually lasts a long time, it doesn’t slow down or stop the way your skin will naturally age. So you may see some sagging or loss of elasticity return over time. “The goal is a natural-looking enhancement rather than a dramatic change in appearance,” Leipziger expresses.

The eye area is very delicate, but blephs are less invasive than you’d think. Rabach performs the procedure at her practice several times a week using just local anesthesia. “However, we use very thin stitches that stay in place for close to one week and must be removed. While there is little to no pain, it’s hard to camouflage the stitches, so we tell our patients they will feel perfectly fine to work from home even after the surgery that day, but not be working from the office,” says Rabach.

Leipziger agrees that recovery from a blepharoplasty is generally minimal compared to other cosmetic surgeries. He says most patients experience some swelling, bruising, and mild discomfort during the first week, which gradually improves. “I typically recommend taking about five to seven days off from work and social activities for an upper blepharoplasty, and a little longer when a lower blepharoplasty is performed,” says Leipziger, adding that most people can resume normal activities within two weeks.

For long-term eye lifting and opening, blephs are the way to go, but Leipziger says several nonsurgical treatments can help improve the appearance of the upper eyelids—even if only temporarily. It all depends on your specific concerns. “Botox can provide a subtle brow lift by relaxing specific muscles, which can help open up the eye area. Certain laser and radiofrequency treatments can also improve skin quality and mild skin laxity by stimulating collagen production and tightening the skin. These options can be beneficial for patients with early signs of aging or minimal excess skin,” says Leipziger. The biggest difference is that the nonsurgical options cannot remove excess eyelid skin or fat. “For patients with significant skin laxity or hooding, an upper blepharoplasty is typically the most effective solution for achieving a more open, refreshed appearance,” Leipziger clarifies.

According to Rabach, a fat transfer is another option to restore hollowness along the orbital bone. This technique can help smooth crepey texture that can set in with age. She also recommends lasers such as CO2 to help improve skin quality, but specifies they aren’t a replacement for surgical removal of excess skin.

As previously mentioned, eyelid skin—even if there’s less of it—will continue to age after an upper bleph. “Long-lasting results come from general health and care of the area. Always wear sunscreen and wear sunglasses to protect the skin, as it’s the thinnest skin on the body and very delicate and prone to sun damage,” says Rabach.

Along with the non-negotiable sunscreen, Leipziger recommends medical-grade skin care products and ingredients such as retinol or peptides to support skin quality. “Additionally, patients may benefit from complementary nonsurgical treatments, such as Botox, laser skin rejuvenation, and/or chemical peels. While surgery cannot stop the aging process, proper skin care and maintenance can help patients enjoy their refreshed appearance for many years,” Leipziger concludes.
