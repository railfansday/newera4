#6880. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#6873. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#6866. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

The sample has an important limit: This year's study included only organizations where at least one ransomware attack caused business disruption. #6857. IDC Report Finds Significant Gap Between AI Spending and Measurable Financial Returns

Nearly 70% of organizations report a significant gap between artificial intelligence spending and measurable financial returns, according to a recent survey by IDC.

The research firm's 2026 Worldwide AI Economics and Cost of Ownership Survey found that 69% reported such a gap, while only 15% had achieved an AI return on investment exceeding 100%.

In a separate May 2026 survey, 73% of respondents identified excessive AI costs as a significant risk.

IDC highlighted the findings in an October 20 analysis examining what enterprise customers expect from AI technology vendors.

Enterprise customers are placing greater emphasis on measurable business outcomes when evaluating AI products, according to IDC.

The firm identified three priorities for vendors: demonstrating financial value, addressing governance and security requirements, and building support beyond IT departments.

The analysis also highlighted the gap between initial agentic AI deployments and broader adoption. Although 80% of organizations have started deploying agentic AI, only 17% are using it across the enterprise.

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. #6817. 6 Antique Trends Designers Are Watching for 2027

Above: The kitchen in designer Phillip Thomas’s Bellport retreat.

Do the words “antique” and “trend” really belong in the same sentence? In many ways, a great antique shop feels like the antidote to trend fatigue: a space where the true must-haves are hidden in dusty corners, with part of the fun in the thrill of the hunt. Yet, as we round out 2026, there’s no denying the current design cycle is casting off perfection for patina. Antiques are en vogue, and top designers are keeping a close eye on the periods, pieces, and yes, trends, that are poised to find new life in 2027.

“The return of interest in antiques reflects a trend towards more personal, unique interiors, and away from strict modern rooms which can often be cool and generic,” says ELLE Decor A-List designer Jamie Drake. This shift paves the way for more playful interactions between pieces, unexpected layering, and unabashed personality.

Other experts second the motion for more character. “I’m seeing clients really drawn to antiques with more to them than meets the eye: pieces that have a bit of humor or whimsy, or pieces with great provenance,” says ELLE Decor Rising Star William Cullum. (He lists a “ridiculously giant butterfly light fixture” from New Orleans, rattan chairs from Bing Crosby’s estate, and a disco ball from Cyndi Lauper’s Bonham’s sale in his list of recent acquisitions—professional and personal.)

Luckily for the amateur antiquer, not all trending vintage styles require a substantial space commitment or glamorous provenance—although an interesting backstory helps. “Antiques are always about a story: where you found them, who owned them, and how that story will continue on after us,” Cullum says.

Ahead, we asked top designers and experts to share the most inspiring antique trends of the moment. From neoclassic mirrors to hand-painted chests paired with modern art, their takes are sure to inform your next estate sale rummage, or inspire you to restyle an old heirloom.

We called mirrored surfaces a major trend to watch for fall 2026, and antique trends have followed suit. Unexpected (or better yet, artfully manipulated) reflections can add rich dimension and movement to any space, but in this category, antiques create a window to the past that’s tough to beat.

“One of the easiest ways to add a sense of history to a room is to include an antique mirror,” says Drake. “Whether you choose a sober and strict neo-classic piece or a more flamboyant baroque one, reflections of the past add depth and sparkle.”

“One trend I’m observing is an uptick in antique dowry chests,” antiques dealer Ali Mahon says. From Pennsylvania Dutch to Swedish Folk and Spanish carved, she’s noticed clients gravitating toward more delicately adorned storage over spare. With thoughtfully mixed furniture (from style to period) becoming more desirable than matching sets, Mahon sees more hand-painted antique surfaces in our future: “With their decorative tulips, leaves, birds, and hearts, dowry chests create a colorful splash in any room.”

ELLE Decor Rising Star Reagan Gail Struble agrees—although delicate designs are not a requirement of the trend, in her book: “Something about a playful wash of color on an awesome old piece of furniture is very alluring, especially when you can see the age and texture of the wood peeking through,” she says. “Hand-painted case pieces add a little punch of charm to any room.”

Luckily, these antiques can be just as functional as they are fashionable. Mahon recommends using a painted chest as a coffee table, at the foot of the bed, or in a space that could benefit from a bit more romance (and a few extra drawers). When styling your find in a modern space, Struble says that contrast is key: try a “chippy Swedish chest of drawers” with an unexpected photograph or painting above it.

According to ELLE Decor A-List designer Nick Olsen, Art Deco pieces are setting the tone for 2027 antique trends—although, if you ask him, they were never out. “I'm particularly inspired by the [art deco influence in the] Gujarat palace in India,” he says. “I'm fascinated by how every element seems to have a wide border: marble floors, carpets, walls of veneer and mohair velvet upholstery.”

According to Olsen, using Art Deco design compositions can do as much for a room as actual pieces from the period. “I like the idea of contrasting borders: solid textures punctuated by smaller graphic prints on furniture and more delicate lighting,” he explains. And while borders are easy to create with paint, paneling, and carpet seaming, Olsen challenges clients to push the Art Deco agenda further by bordering with luxe materials.

Whether you’re new to antiquing or simply trying to add more character to a space, lighting is an excellent place to start. “Lighting is the jewelry of the home, so it’s one of my favorite places to bring in something vintage,” says A-Lister Corey Damen Jenkins. “An Art Deco sconce or an antique brass lantern has a warmth to it that’s hard to find in something brand new.”

Adding to an existing space? Jenkins advises starting small: “Swap one overhead fixture or put a pair of vintage lamps on a console, and you’ll be surprised how much the whole room shifts,” he says. “It’s an easy way to add history without starting over.”

“In light of America’s 250th birthday, Americana in general is in vogue,” says Mahon. Among her clients, the trend has manifested in quilts and textiles, portraits of sea captains, trade signs, and ironstone—culminating in an aesthetic she describes as “elevated country—not corny.”

If you’ve noticed that no particular design period seems to reign supreme among designers’ top antique trend picks, it’s not an accident. Heading into 2027, there’s a strong argument to be made that mixed-period design is more influential than any singular reference.

“I love a room that doesn’t belong to any one decade,” Jenkins says. “A nineteenth-century chest with a 1970s lamp on top, or a Lucite chair pulled up to an antique desk. Those are the pairings that make people stop and look twice.” Drake adds that seemingly out-of-place seating is an easy way to add intrigue. “I love including antique chairs as sculptural notes, contrasted against modern upholstery and tables,” he says.

But when it comes to antiquing, there’s really only so much one can plan for. Letting a great find lead the way often turns out to be a better strategy than arriving to an estate sale with a rigid checklist. The best way to keep up with top designers when it comes to antiquing this year? Find a piece that has something to say, place it where it seems to belong, and then try it where it doesn’t.

#6799. Patrick Stewart Was a Serious Watch Guy. Here Are 5 of the Late Hollywood Legend's Best.

From a treasured Rolex to his beloved IWC, the late actor had a lifelong appreciation for the art of watchmaking.

“Someone once told me that time was a predator that stalked us all our lives," begins Captain Jean-Luc Picard, played by Sir Patrick Stewart in Star Trek Generations, in one of his most memorable lines. "I rather believe that time is a companion who goes with us on a journey, reminds us to cherish every moment, because it will never come again.”

Those musings on time feel particularly poignant now, following the British actor's death. There’s something rather comforting about the idea of time as a companion, particularly when you consider Stewart’s lifelong fascination with the very objects we use to measure it.

Because, as it turns out, Patrick Stewart was quite the watch guy. Not simply someone who happened to wear an expensive timepiece on the red carpet, but a genuine enthusiast with an appreciation for the craftsmanship, history, and ingenuity behind them. He bought his first watch at 18 and, by his own admission, never threw one away.

“The watch industry never ceases to amaze me,” he told the Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie in 2019. “Because a watch is more than just an object that gives the time. Behind every watch there is an idea, a design, a concept, an innovation, and the watchmaker's desire to create something that is greater than anything that has ever existed before.”

It's an enthusiasm that stayed with him throughout his life, from the Rolex he reportedly spent almost all his savings on after arriving in Hollywood to his enduring affection for IWC Schaffhausen. And, naturally, there were some rather lovely watches along the way.

So, in celebration of an extraordinary career and a life well lived, we've taken a look back at some of Patrick Stewart's finest watch moments.

Perhaps the most meaningful watch in Stewart's story is one for which we don't have an exact reference. Long before he became one of Hollywood's most recognizable actors, Stewart was given a rather unexpected piece of career advice by the actress Eva Marie Saint. She advised him that appearances mattered, and that an expensive watch might help convince producers he was already a successful actor. So, she took him to a Beverly Hills jeweller, where he picked out a Rolex. The only problem? It cost him his entire savings. “I was eating spaghetti and boiled potatoes for months after that!” he recalled in a 2019 interview with the Fondation de la Haute Horlogerie.

It's touching anecdote given Stewart's modest upbringing. Having grown up in poverty, he admitted that it took him years to feel comfortable spending money on beautiful things. But eventually, he came to realize there was nothing wrong with enjoying the rewards of his success.

Tissot Le Locle Powermatic 80 to the 2017 Canadian Grand Prix

And while most people were understandably distracted by the sight of Captain Picard downing champagne from a sweaty shoe, there was a rather lovely watch on his wrist, a Tissot Le Locle Powermatic 80. The reasonably priced Swiss automatic features a silver guilloché dial, Roman numerals, a black leather strap, and an impressive 80-hour power reserve. Proof that even a relatively affordable dress watch can look the part.

Long before his appearances at watchmaking galas, Stewart was already making a convincing case for the understated dress watch. In this archival portrait, the actor wears a small, gold-tone, rectangular timepiece, seemingly a Cartier Tank, on a dark leather strap. Paired with a pale linen-style suit and a knitted white top, it's a particularly handsome combination.

First introduced in 1917, the Cartier Tank has become one of watchmaking's most enduring designs, worn by everyone from Andy Warhol to Muhammad Ali. Its appeal lies in its simplicity: a rectangular case, elegant proportions, and a design that has barely needed changing in more than a century. First introduced in 2013, the reference 3904 is a particularly elegant piece of watchmaking. Featuring a 42mm case, distinctive Arabic numerals, and a railway-track minute scale, it's a slightly sportier proposition than the Portofino. Inside sits IWC's automatic calibre 89361, complete with a flyback chronograph function and an impressive 68-hour power reserve. The evening celebrated the Swiss manufacturer's renewed Da Vinci collection, but Stewart's choice of wristwear showcased another of IWC's most recognizable designs. And given that he once named IWC as his favorite watchmaker, it's hardly surprising that he had such good taste.

#6760. I Went to the World's Biggest Furniture Trade Show—These 6 Trends Are About to Be Everywhere

The furniture profiles, patterns, and palettes you’re about to see everywhere.

Twice a year, High Point, North Carolina, becomes the home industry’s giant showroom, where furniture and design brands unveil new collections for retailers, interior designers, and buyers to see and source. At House Beautiful, we’re your boots on the ground, looking for the ideas that may travel from showroom to store—and into your home next.

This fall, Market made a case for decorating with more character and muddier hues: green moved from the forest floor to the showroom floor, florals claimed entire sofas, and upholstery played with everything from a flash of leg to a full fabric wrap. Add wood-backed furniture as an accent, garden-inspired lighting, and a generous swish of fringe, and the message was clear: The details are ready to take center stage.

This year, green was the unofficial color of Market. Benjamin Moore and Behr had already put the hue on the radar with their 2027 Colors of the Year: Webster Green, a confident mid-tone balancing blue’s coolness with yellow’s warmth, and Grounded, a mossy olive softened by earthy brown undertones. Then came the showroom echoes. Versions of green, from earthy vegetable shades to deeper, moodier tones, appeared nearly everywhere. Kathy Kuo even created a “Music Room” (shown in our opening image) for Rowe Furniture inspired by the verdant shade. The appeal is easy to understand: Green brings the natural world indoors, lending a sense of calm and grounding a space without draining it of personality.

The design industry’s obsession with prints is now a love letter to botanicals. Florals erupted across Market in a myriad of scales, from small, almost sun-bleached botanicals to overscaled, tapestry-adjacent blooms. Even the tiniest prints made a surprisingly bold statement when carried across an entire sofa; the larger blooms brought the feel of a faded heirloom into full view. Both have that grandmotherly, another-era quality, but the impact is anything but timid. Yes, it’s a major design commitment, but why play it safe with a solid, barely-there pattern? It may not be for the faint of design, but we are all in a sofa fully dressed in flowers.

Here’s a furniture detail that changes the whole silhouette: Brands are letting wood take over the outside back—or sides—of upholstered seating. Baker’s sofa makes the move hard to miss, with a broad wood shell framing the piece. It’s a little like “inside-out” upholstery, where the outside gets its own treatment, but here the wood gives the piece architectural shape and presence. As floor plans open up and sofas float in family and living rooms, the back is no longer hidden against a wall. It’s part of the view, and part of how the seating area takes shape, making it all the more important that it feels worth looking at.

Upholstery continues to take its cues from fashion, with a focus on mixing silhouettes: Mini skirts that let those gams show; full, ballooning skirts with all the best parts of an ’80s prom dress; and what we lovingly call body-con upholstery, where the entire chair is wrapped head to toe in one fabric.

The appeal goes beyond a good fashion metaphor, though. Each treatment changes the chair’s proportions: a flash of leg keeps it light, a generous skirt adds volume, and an all-over wrap turns both seat and base into one continuous form, with no leg finish to choose or worry about. Each has its own distinct style POV, making your shopping choices that much more complicated, but in the best way.

If garden motifs aren’t your thing, don’t look up. The floral revival isn’t limited to upholstery and wallpaper; it’s reaching the ceiling, too. Visual Comfort & Co.’s Fairoaks collection, designed by Fisher Weisman, includes a chandelier, sconce, and mini flush mount. Sculptural branches and twigs cleverly disguise the chandelier’s ceiling cord, preserving the romance of the design. At Hudson Valley Lighting, a tulip-inspired chandelier brings a more literal bloom overhead. Both offer a way to bring the garden indoors through shape and silhouette, without committing an entire room to pattern.

Sometimes, all it takes is a little bullion fringe. The thick, tightly twisted cords fall in a dense, weighty row, adding plush texture and a satisfying swish to a skirted chair or sofa. It nods to the Victorian parlor, but stops short of period-drama excess; against a cleaner silhouette or in a spare room, the old-world trim feels playful and unexpectedly fresh. Consider it the design equivalent of a great pair of earrings: a little extra, in exactly the right place.

#6748. 4 Weekend Decluttering Habits That Make Small Spaces Feel Bigger

A few smart systems can keep your counters, closets, and junk drawers in check.

The easiest clutter to manage is clutter that never has a chance to form. And in small spaces like galley kitchens and studio apartments, mess piles up naturally on any flat surface. Keeping it under control is less about “magic bullet” tips and more about systems you can actually keep up over time. So when we asked the professional organizers about how to declutter small spaces, the answer was in mindset and habits. If you can limit what comes into your space in the first place, you can better control its organization.

“I could say, ‘Grab a garbage bag and pick up all of the detritus left in the wake of a busy life and then put everything else where it belongs.’ That’s obvious,” Gayle M. Gruenberg of Let’s Get Organized says. “The hard part is actually DOING it. It requires motivation, presence of mind, initiative, and the necessary tools.”

Below, find the pro-approved habits that make a difference when you have more stuff than your space can hold. You can start these routines in just one weekend, and it’s far more attainable than tackling the massive pile in the garage.

Everything needs a home in your house. That’s the power of a well-placed “drop zone.” Even in small spaces, a catchall tray, bowl, or mini basket belongs at entryways or places of transition. A hook for your keys and bags when you come through the door, a bin for loose throw blankets, or a basket for remote controls on the mantel.

Throughout the day, it can take quite a bit of mental load to always put everything back when you use it. That’s where Cathy Orr, cofounder of The Uncluttered Life, recommends having a “collection basket.” It’s just one catchall container, where you can drop miscellaneous misplaced items that you pick up throughout the day. At the end of the night, take about 10 minutes to go through the collection basket and return everything to where it belongs. It’s just one small habit, rather than something to keep up with all day.

Impulse shopping lends itself to clutter very easily. The “One In, One Out” rule can curb this. It works like this: for every new thing you want to bring into your home, one thing has to go. Greenhalgh suggests checking your closet, fridge, and pantry before you shop—going through your back stock will save you both space and money.

“In small spaces, items accumulate very quickly,” Macie Kreutzer, founder of For the Love of Simple, says. “Before shopping for new items (home goods, clothing) clear out at least one item from this category. Don't bring in more than your space can physically contain.”

Once you have everything you’re ready to get rid of, make the donation process as seamless as possible. Fill your donation bags and boxes in the hallway or in the car. This encourages you to actually finish getting rid of the clutter, rather than letting them collect dust in the garage.

“Nothing gets to wander back inside just because it once lived there,” Carroll Cartwright, professional organizer and owner of Neatly & Co., says. “And this is why I love moving everything out of the space and into the hall or a separate area first. The things you decide not to keep are already halfway out the door. Keep them moving.”

It is so tempting to take advantage of the freebies in life. But this is how linen closets and kitchen drawers get quickly overrun. Unless there’s a specific and immediate need for them, you’re better off getting rid of them.

“They are usually cheap and break easily anyway,” Diane Greenhalgh, owner of Tiny to the Max, says. “That includes airplane and hotel freebies, special event or conference swag, and extra takeout plastic utensils and condiments. Your junk drawer will thank you.”

If you already have a drawer overflowing with freebies, it’s time to start culling and evaluating. You might part with two or 10 things, or you might choose to keep it all. “The point is that you’ve looked at every single item and made a decision about it, instead of continuing to store things simply because they were already there,” Cartwright says. “More likely than not, you’ll find something that makes you say, ‘Why on earth do I still have this?’”

The advice your grandma gave you was spot-on: It’s easier if you wash the dishes as you cook. That same principle applies to keeping your small space tidy. Mess feels larger with less square footage to work with, so once you’re finished with something, put it away.

“Put away your makeup after you've applied it in the morning, stack dishes in the dishwasher and wipe down surfaces after each meal, and tuck away work or homework into a drawer once you're done for the evening,” Greenhalgh says.

For the days you can’t clean as you go, Cartwright suggests spending 10 to 15 minutes a day “closing your circles.” It’s what helps your home stay clean over time, instead of just for a few weeks. This period is when you put the scissors back in the drawer or hang up your jacket.

“Most clutter is just a collection of unfinished tasks,” Cartwright says. “This small commitment allows you to wake up to order instead of yesterday’s chaos, and that is a much better way to start the day!”

#6744. 5 Common Mistakes That Are Making Your Vintage Decor Look All Wrong—and How Designers Get It Right

Experts say the best rooms mix eras, styles, and stories.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Pictured above: This space, designed by Sarah West, strikes the right balance of vintage and contemporary decor, proving that a mix is always better than sticking to one design era.

It’s not just the Instagram algorithm playing tricks on you: Everyone really is looking to antique and vintage decor. Homeowners are growing tired of rooms that look as though everything was bought from a single brand in one fell swoop. We’re now at a point where a collected, curated, personalized feel has replaced quiet luxury.

“I think it’s important to add at least some kind of antique or vintage piece in every room,” says Sarah West, founder of Sarah West & Associates and a designer for over 20 years. “Even if you’re doing a more modern or transitional room, a sense of history adds weight to a space.”

It’s not necessarily as easy as plopping one antique into a room and calling it a day, however, and that can feel intimidating. Even once you learn to shop for antiques and vintage like a pro, what do you actually do with those finds when you’re home?

While the designers we spoke with are quick to point out there really are no strict rules here—it’s about your style and what you love—there are a few key mistakes that pop up most often when people start styling with antique and vintage decor. These designers have the scoop on how to avoid those missteps and make your treasures, and your entire room, shine.

Committing to a massive piece of furniture or tasking yourself with overhauling an entire room with antique or vintage pieces can get overwhelming fast. You may make decisions you don’t love down the line.

If you decide you’re going to decorate a whole room in a limited amount of time, and you’re set on sourcing antique or vintage items, you’ll likely end up settling for things just because you need them, says Nancy Cavaliere, a New York–based interior decorator, stylist, and vintage collector. Go slow and start small.

“I’d rather live with an empty corner while I wait for the perfect piece than fill it with something I don’t love,” she notes. “The most interesting homes evolve over time. I think a room should tell the story of the person living in it, not the story of one shopping trip.”

Ease your way into decorating with vintage and antiques by starting small. Think one piece of art, one mirror, one vase.

“Don’t overthink it,” West advises. “Take a small piece, try it out in the room for a couple of days, see how it feels.” Do you like the piece in that spot? Does it work with other elements of the room? Does it inspire other styling choices for the space? Try moving it around and interacting with it to find its perfect positioning and see where that takes you.

As you start out getting a feel for that smaller piece, remember that just because it’s unique doesn’t mean it has to be the focal point of the whole room.

“Don’t treat vintage pieces like they have to be the star of the room simply because they’re old,” says Cavaliere. “Not every antique needs a pedestal. Sometimes the most beautiful thing you can do is let an extraordinary piece sit comfortably alongside something completely ordinary.”

Fully incorporating vintage or antique pieces into a space imbues the room with that richness and intrigue.

“These pieces give feelings to a room,” West says. “If you put something in a corner, as you transition throughout the room, it gives this element of surprise.”

This includes creating high-low mixes, says Cavaliere, who points out, “a $20 flea market find can look incredible next to an expensive designer piece. It’s all about how you put things together.”

In fact, that mix is something to strive for, says Alex Bass, founder of Salon 21, Art Advisory & Interior Design Firm.

“I love a contrast, like a room with modern architecture that leans heavily into antiques or vice versa. If you want to incorporate pieces from Pottery Barn, CB2, that’s fine, but there are also so many affordable vintage finds. And something like an antique light fixture can make all of these pieces work.”

Speaking of contrasts, all of our designer sources say a very common misconception they encounter is that you can only decorate a room with pieces from the same era.

“Just because you have a Victorian chair doesn’t mean you need a Victorian living room,” Cavaliere says. “My own home is a mix of different periods, styles, colors, and patterns, and that's what gives it personality. I love the tension between pieces that seemingly have nothing in common. And it's fun!”

Bass cites the example of Art Deco being popular right now, while articles are claiming midcentury modern is “out.” She encourages people to think of pieces less rigidly, assuming they have to commit fully to one style, or toss another style because someone declared a new one the next big thing.

“These are not ‘trends,’ these are design periods that already existed,” she explains. “It’s about having the eye to pull pieces from different periods that speak to you and make sense in your room.” You can mix Art Deco and midcentury modern, for instance, by using elements characteristic of them both, like marble, warm woods, mixed metals, and bold color pops.

There can be a fear with vintage and antique pieces that changing anything “ruins” them. But unless it’s a significantly valuable piece you’re investing in or something you plan on having appraised, Bass says, embrace the ability to make special pieces work for your space.

“Don’t be afraid to reupholster,” she says—and not just if the fabric is in poor shape. “If I buy a piece and the structure is beautiful but I want to add a contemporary fabric and print, that can make for a fun contrast with the frame.”

For example, Bass fell in love with a modular midcentury sectional, but its fabric didn’t quite complement the space she was designing, so she chose a contemporary fabric that worked with its aesthetic.

This, Cavaliere points out, frees you up from obsessing over condition when shopping for pieces. If something has great bones but its fabric or finish has seen better days—and if you love it—don’t pass it up. Remember you can reupholster, strip and stain or paint, and repair.

Just factor the cost of those fixes into the price of the item to help make your decision. Especially because, as Bass advises, it’s better to consult a professional unless you’re well-versed in certain projects. Stripping and staining a wood piece is fairly easy, but when it comes to reupholstering and other repairs, you’ll likely actually save money by outsourcing to a pro rather than potentially damaging the piece yourself.

Similarly, it’s typical for people to feel they can’t actually make use of vintage and antique pieces, because they think they’re too precious. But especially considering how well-made many older pieces are, they’re begging to be put to work. Again, it’s actually incorporating them into your everyday life that gives your room and entire home that sense of personalization, curation, and storytelling.

“One hundred years ago, people were using their silver,” West says. “In my kitchen, you’re eating with hotel silver.”

This includes getting creative with how you use antique and vintage pieces—don’t feel bound by what something is “supposed to” be for.

“One of my favorite tricks is turning vintage drapes and valances into bed canopies,” Cavaliere says. “I did this in my daughter’s room and I absolutely love it.” Cavaliere also finds unexpected uses for small vessels and decorative objects: a creamer and sugar set in the bathroom to hold Q-tips and cotton balls; dishes, trays, and crystal bowls to sort accessories in her closet.

“A vintage china cabinet doesn't have to live in a dining room just because it was designed to hold dishes,” she says. “One of my favorite tricks is using china cabinets to display and store shoes and handbags in a dressing room or closet. Why spend a ton of money on custom cabinetry when you can repurpose a beautiful vintage piece that has so much more character?”

West agrees. “Purchase an armoire, flip the doors, add mirrors, and make it a bar,” West suggests. “Find an old door knocker you love? Hang a towel off of it. Use a tablecloth to make a pillow. Tell a story.”

#6692. Hublot Just Relaunched Its First Watch. Founder Carlo Crocco Explains How It Changed Everything.

In Milan, celebrating the release of the Classic Fusion Tributo, I got to catch up with the guy who altered the aesthetics of modern watchmaking.

If you spend a lot of time following the world of watches, Hublot’s origin story might feel as familiar as one of the fairytales you heard at bedtime as a child. If you don’t, though, it might feel just as fantastical as some yarn about a princess who feels a pea under a stack of mattresses or a prince who slays a fire-breathing dragon.

You start with an unlikely hero. This time around, it’s an Italian entrepreneur with a big, bold, brazen idea. He wants to make a watch, something unlike anyone has ever seen. It’ll be gold, with an angular case shaped like the porthole on a ship. Not so crazy yet. But wait. Because, breaking entirely with the conventions of the day, the strap won’t be made of exotic leather or precious metal but rubber. Humble, industrial rubber.

This is exactly what Hublot founder Carlo Crocco envisioned nearly half a century ago, and what he brought to the Basel Watch Fair—pretty much the place to be if you wanted to be a part of the business of timekeeping—in 1980. He spent three years developing the idea, all without doing a bit of market research. He just believed in his vision. And then the fair began.

“I was very anxious, because for the first two days, no one came into our booth,” he says. But the anxiety didn’t have long to fester. Word was spreading. And then? People galore. “On the third day, we realized that everyone was speaking about us because it was really something different from all the other watches.”

That first watch—36mm in diameter, with a quartz movement and a black rubber strap—quickly became a hit. Industry buzz gave way to the kind of clientele most brands can only imagine. The King of Greece bought one in Gstaad for himself and another for his brother-in-law, the King of Spain. More royals followed. (“By the way, they’re all related,” Crocco quips.)

But the biggest thing wasn’t getting insiders to a booth at a trade fair or convincing kings to strap Hublot watches—named, if you’re curious, for the French word for porthole—on their wrists. It was changing how the industry thought about the way watches are made.

“At that time, the mentality of watchmaking was very classical,” Crocco says. “They couldn’t think, normal people, of putting together rubber and gold. I personally thought it was a really good idea because it was against the current. It was very risky, but if you don’t risk, you have no result.”

The result was a watch industry that completely recalibrated itself. Crocco says that within four or five years of the original Hublot’s release, the brand was already dealing with imitators. Now, the idea of combining disparate materials is firmly embedded in the minds of both consumers and designers. You can see it on watches from brands that sell at the local department store and brands that only sell to folks who haven’t seen the inside of one of those since the first time their private jet took off. Crocco’s big idea changed the whole game.

Which means it’s a pretty good time to go back to the beginning of this thing. Last month in Milan, Hublot debuted a new take on that first, groundbreaking watch. Dubbed the Hublot Classic Fusion Tributo, it’s an homage but not a nuts-and-bolts recreation. Instead of the original 36mm case, it’s been upsized to a more 2026-appropriate 38mm. And instead of a quartz movement—Crocco thought it was “more practical” at the time but has come around to the enduring appeal of mechanical construction—a self-winding HUB1711 movement with a 50-hour power reserve keeps things ticking along. Oh, and now you can get it in white gold, too.

Then there’s that rubber strap. When Crocco designed the original Hublot, he was insistent that the strap was entirely straight; he felt it was crucial to the aesthetic proposition. On the Tributo, it’s ever-so-slightly, almost imperceptibly tapered, a necessary concession to keep the proportions intact after increasing the case size (which, handily, lets that automatic movement sit comfortably inside).

But, as with the first Hublot, it’s not all about tech specs and watch-nerd particulars. It’s about the spirit of the thing. And the spirit of this watch stays true to that pioneering design. There are twelve screws around the bezel, so you can keep an eye on the hour despite the dial’s lack of indices—something Crocco says was a non-negotiable when he started consulting on the new release. The yellow gold version keeps the abyss-like black lacquer dial and the white gold throws us a tiny (and welcome) curveball by using onyx. The combo of precious metal and rubber still hits, 46 years later.

If you prod him how he feels about the way that he shifted the watch industry, Crocco won’t brag. He left Hublot nearly 20 years ago, when it sold to LVMH. He knows his idea changed things; he’s happy about it. But this isn’t his day job anymore. Still, when he’s asked if he could be considered the godfather of fusing high and low in watchmaking—and you give him a minute to ponder it—he’ll tell you the truth.

#6685. How Her Love of Vintage Changed Writer Keli Goff’s Life

Her collection, which has given new life to some of the great Black designers, is now a museum show.

Goff, a writer who has been on the staff of shows including Being Mary Jane and And Just Like That, now owns work from major fashion figures like Patrick Kelly, Isaia Rankin, Stephen Burrows, and Ann Lowe, together making up one of the biggest private collections of work by Black designers. This month, the Houston Museum of African American Culture presents “Collecting in Color: Highlights from the Keli Goff Collection,” which was curated by John Guess and Danielle Finnerman. It’s the first major exhibition focused on her collection, and some of the pieces will be donated to the Bard Graduate Center’s Study Collection.

Goff’s interest in fashion design has intersected with her writing career in interesting ways. The estate of legendary costume designer Edith Head chose Goff to adapt her story for a biopic. “It’s all so surreal. I obviously have admired Edith’s work as long as I can remember, so my collection bringing this project into my life is a dream come true,” she says. Last year, Goff completed studying to become an art appraiser. She now consults in the art and auction sphere in addition to writing.

“People have been fascinated by my collection,” Goff says. “It’s a bit rare to see, frankly, collectors in this space like me. I mean like me in all sorts of ways, not just as an African American woman, but an African American woman who’s not from a privileged background. It’s important to me that I’m not the last one like me in these spaces.”

Goff spoke to ELLE about the joy of working with a museum in her Texas hometown, the old movies that inspired her love of vintage, and the designers she wishes everyone knew.

It really started by watching old movies with my mom and watching her dress up [when I was] a little girl. When I show friends old photos of her, people are always thinking, “Oh, she looks like a movie star or very Diana Ross-esque.” We would watch old movies, especially Imitation of Life and things from that era, the real glamour, the golden age of Hollywood. She would always say, “Look at the dresses, look at the dresses,” and I would. I really fell in love with old movies, which I do believe is part of how I ended up becoming a writer.

But also, I really fell in love with vintage clothes. I moved to New York and didn’t have a lot of money, and vintage was not trendy and didn’t cost thousands of dollars like it can be today. For one of my earliest jobs, I had to go to fancy events, but I had no money. I bought a couple of pieces from the ’60s at a flea market. I remember being at this event, and all these women who obviously had a lot more money to spend on clothes, asking, “Who’s this by? What boutique did you buy this at?” And I was like, “Oh, I got it at a flea market on Avenue B.” I think they thought I was joking.

I wrote a piece for The Daily Beast several years ago about the most influential Black designers in history. It was one of the most memorable pieces I’ve written. I don’t think at the time I was aware of how profoundly it impacted me, but I learned how many seminal Black American designers died of AIDS in a really short window of time. Willi Smith, one of the first Black designers to achieve major crossover commercial success in department stores, died. And so did Isaia Rankin and then Patrick Kelly. What I took away is that so much history was lost, but I also think about how different the future of American fashion might be today because these were really trailblazing and hugely influential.

They weren’t just influential Black American designers, but hugely influential American designers. Isaia Rankin basically had invented athleisure wear. It was his idea that you should be able to go from a coffee shop to a gym to the club. [He made] one of the first pieces by a Black designer I acquired online. I’ll never forget that the person selling it said, “This belonged to my aunt. Apparently, this guy used to be somebody,” which I just think is the saddest thing. He had this profound impact and then had been largely forgotten.

During the pandemic, when everyone worked from home with too much time on their hands and easy access to shop online, I ended up acquiring quite a bit, in part because a lot of the brick-and-mortar stores, particularly in Europe, were really struggling. They were so reliant on tourist foot traffic, and so a lot of them started selling online [for the first time]. And so there were a number of French boutiques I started acquiring pieces from, including some Patrick Kelly pieces that still have the original store tags from the 1980s, which I won’t remove. Those I won’t wear. The rest of them I wear when they fit. As life goes, they don’t always fit forever.

I’ve become friendly with Patrick Kelly’s widower, Bjorn Amelan, who’s a lovely, wonderful man and the keeper of Patrick’s legacy. It is important to me, when possible, to try to maintain relationships with the people I view as keepers of the legacies because I see my collection as serving the legacy of the designers. The pieces don’t belong to me. They belong to our wider community because they’re a part of our lived history.

The really cool thing is how many people I’ve met along this journey who have not only become cheerleaders but actually reach out to me about pieces. Writer Jill Kargman has become a friend and she gifted me a vintage Stephen Burrows dress. She said, “It belongs in your collection,” which is just the coolest thing ever.

I’m from Houston, and we have the Houston Museum of African American Culture here. We’re living in a really interesting time where people are having conversations about the politics of diversity, but one of the things that’s cool about fashion is that people of different political persuasions were like, “I think this collection’s really cool. I read an article about your collection, I ended up ordering a book about Elizabeth Keckley, or I ended up going down a rabbit hole about Ann Lowe.” At this moment, to be able to do an exhibition like this in my hometown felt particularly poignant.

Melanie Lawson has been a legendary anchor in Houston for decades. She was asked what she’s going to do in retirement, and she said that her husband, John Guess Jr., was the CEO of the Houston Museum of African American Culture. I sent him a note about how much Melanie inspired me and then mentioned my collection.

It was just kismet. They’ve actually never done a fashion exhibition. It was a real gamble. But what I so appreciated is that they had the same vision that, in a time in which our world is so divided, it’s really beautiful when you can find pieces of art that people can come together around.

#6682. After I Kicked Drugs and Alcohol, I Found a New Addiction: Cheating on My Fiancée

Thirteen years of sobriety taught me that the most dangerous thing for an addict isn’t getting caught—it’s getting away with it. And I keep getting away with it.

Ethical nonmonogamy relies on a foundation of total transparency. Partners openly agree to terms and continually negotiate them together. Betrayals such as infidelity can take many shapes and break the agreement a couple has made, monogamous or not. For people in recovery, however, the adrenaline of secret keeping can feel familiar. Clinicians call this behavioral, or process, addiction—in which compulsion lies in the thrill of the chase rather than the payoff. It doesn’t excuse betrayal, but it can help explain how a person committed to honesty ends up living a lie.

For this installment of the Secret Lives of Men, we spoke to Owen,* a 36-year-old brand strategist with 13 years of sobriety who spent most of the first two years of his relationship cheating on the partner he plans to marry. His story begs the question: What does it mean to carry out “living amends,” an Alcoholics Anonymous term for permanently changing your behavior for the better, when you aren’t ready to confess?

*Names and identifying details have been changed to protect anonymity.

Once a week, my fiancée has a therapy session over a video call that she takes from our bedroom. We both work from home, so while that happens, I’m usually barricaded in my office with noise-canceling headphones on, keeping up with work. Recently, I came out to the kitchen to pour a coffee after her hour was up, and she wasn’t home.

She takes walks, so there was no reason to panic. But my mind went where it has gone a lot lately: She found out. By whatever cruel trick of fate, she knew, and she was out there walking it off, letting the anger curdle. When she came back, she would bring hell with her.

I called once. She didn’t pick up. I didn’t try again. For half an hour, I paced the apartment unable to get a single thing done. I checked my phone every few seconds. I rehearsed what I’d say when she walked through the door: which parts I’d confess, which parts I’d still try to save. I worried myself sick. When she finally walked back in, it turned out she’d just been on a long phone call with her sister. She took off her headset, kissed me hello, and asked what I had in mind for dinner. My heart kept pounding long after she’d walked into the kitchen.

Until two years ago, I could say with real pride that I had never cheated on anyone. Not once. Not even when I was drinking and doing all those drugs, back when I lied about nearly everything else. I’d had opportunities, but infidelity was the one line I never crossed.

Advanced 12-step work taught me a strange paradox: Sometimes you have to sit with the agonizing truth that you are doing something wrong. That you aren’t ready or able to stop. I’ve built 13 years of sobriety on the idea that we are only as sick as our secrets. But I should admit something up front: I’m good at telling stories, especially about myself. Even now, I catch myself deciding which parts to share. So here it is: For the better part of two years, I cheated on the woman I’m going to marry. She still doesn’t know.

In recovery, you learn that the most dangerous thing that can happen to a person with an addiction isn’t getting caught—it’s getting away with it. And every time you skate by, the lie gets a little easier to tell. The disease gets a little more room to grow. I first learned that at 23. I had to learn it again in my 30s.

I come from a long line of talented, tortured people. My father sold commercial real estate and spent years in and out of a quiet, high-functioning addiction to painkillers. He went away for treatment twice. My mother was raised by an alcoholic and carried a lot of anger. My parents divorced when I was young, both remarried badly, and neither of their houses was a safe place emotionally.

At 19, while in college, I fell into a relationship I can only describe as wickedly codependent. We went through two miscarriages together and became trauma bonded. I didn’t know how to grieve, so I was drinking heavily and taking almost anything I could find: stimulants, psychedelics, dissociatives. Just before I turned 21, I caught my girlfriend in a lie so profound it ripped the wool off my eyes: She had been sleeping with my best friend for nearly the entire time we’d been together. One of the miscarried babies was actually his. I ended things the day I found out and took the situation as the perfect excuse to keep killing myself slowly.

I didn’t make it a year out of college before the consequences caught up with me. The fall after graduation, I was driving home from a concert when I got pulled over for a dead taillight. I was relatively sober and polite and was sure I’d talked my way out of it—right up until the officer asked me to empty my pockets. Inside were a bag of powder that I’d been promised was Molly and a few pills I didn’t have a prescription for. They booked me and sent everything off to the state lab. For three months, nothing happened. Meanwhile, I painted myself further into the corner before the floor ultimately fell out. When the charge finally came, I began a year and a half of court dates and probation.

As a first offender, I pled into a court-mandated, recovery-focused diversion program with regular drug tests. I followed the program to a T, at least for a while.

One weekend, when house-sitting alone for a neighbor, I took a drink out of boredom and pure spite. No test caught it. Then I lied about it to my counselors, the court, and my family. A month later, guilt finally won out, and I admitted it to my sponsor. A few days later, he brought it back up and told me I had to come clean to everyone.

“The fact that you were willing to conceal that from everyone for this long,” he said, “is the only evidence you need of how sick you are.”

The problem wasn’t the drink. It was how easily I hid it. I surrendered to the program and handed my life over to a higher power. I haven’t had a drink since.

I went celibate by choice for three years. I did a lot of therapy and learned that I had swung from codependent to avoidant. I had finally regained control, and I didn’t want to hand myself to anyone. Then, in my late 20s, I decided to give dating another shot.

Before long, I leaned into ethical nonmonogamy and dated as “solo poly,” which means I was seeing multiple people without a primary partner. Casual dating became my primary hobby. Some connections were purely physical; some were friends with benefits; some went emotionally deep. At my peak, I was sleeping with six people, two or three times a week. I felt I was winning at something. I was always honest with every partner about who I was, sobriety included. But I wasn’t honest with myself about why I needed so much of it.

Then, later in therapy, I came to understand that the dopamine that keeps people in an addiction loop is driven less by the thing itself than by the anticipation of getting it. Dating apps are built on that anticipation. For me, there were three hits: the match, the first nudes, the first hookup. In the program, we say the disease of addiction wears as many faces as it needs to. The chase was one of those faces.

I matched with Maya as she was getting ready to leave on a six-week work trip to Thailand. We messaged far longer than usual, trading long voice notes across the 11-hour time difference—her from hotel lobbies at night, me on morning walks. By the time we finally met, I already knew how she laughed. Our first date was electric, the best of my life. We sat at the bar until they started stacking chairs, talking about family, grief, books that had saved us. She was self-sufficient and emotionally deep. She was great at conversation. She was beautiful.

A few months into our relationship, we curled up on her couch on a Sunday afternoon and talked about ethical nonmonogamy. We’d both dabbled in it, but neither of us had done it inside a primary relationship. I told her I wanted it. She was open to it. We agreed to start monogamous and build the foundation first, but we never said for how long.

The truth is, our sex wasn’t great. Early on, I suggested something new in bed, and she went quiet and turned toward the wall. Neither of us brought it up the next morning. I internalized the idea that I couldn’t ask for what I wanted, that this was just how it would be until we opened things up. Instead of communicating, I let the resentment build.

Then, one afternoon at a coffee shop across town, I ran into Bree, a woman I used to hook up with. She had been the perfect friend with benefits: incredible chemistry but no desire for a relationship. We stayed and talked for an hour. When she mentioned she lived ten minutes away, neither of us pretended not to know what that meant. By that evening, I was at her place. It was the first time in my life I had been unfaithful in any way, and I felt awful. When I went home, I waited for the consequences. They never came.

A big part of addiction is justification. We were planning to be nonmonogamous, I told myself, so the way I’d just conducted myself was basically equivalent. It wasn’t that bad. I can see now how thin that logic was. Nonmonogamy runs on transparency and consent. I was keeping a secret.

Over the next year and a half, my infidelities came and went. A few more times with Bree. A handful of times with anonymous women in other cities. Trans women, sex workers. I traveled for work, and I took advantage of the distance. I kept my phone face down, hid dating apps, used end-to-end-encrypted texting. For each time I followed through, there were five times I stopped myself. Each close call made me feel like I had it under control.

Strangely, the woman I cheated with most also kept me from making worse mistakes. Bree became my confidante, the only person I was fully honest with about any of it. One night, I arranged to meet a sex worker. I was sitting in my car outside the hotel when clarity hit. The only thing I could think to do was call Bree. She talked me down. She and I ended up having sex that night. The program has a phrase for that kind of thinking: I want what I want when I want it.

Meanwhile, the relationship I was betraying kept getting better. We slowly began to practice nonmonogamy, and it took us far longer than either of us expected. We listened to the audiobook version of Polysecure by Jessica Fern together. We’d pause so often to talk that a half-hour chapter took two hours. It gave me a way to ask for what I wanted without it feeling like an attack on her. One night, Maya paused the book and asked me, plainly, how she could do better. For the first time, I believed she meant it, and I told her. The sex got better. Then, much better.

The final close call came in June. Bored at my desk, I connected with someone on an anonymous hookup site, and we moved to texting. A few messages in, after I’d sent a couple of nudes, my phone lit up with a long blackmail threat. Attached was a screenshot of Maya’s Instagram. This kind of thing had happened four or five times before, back when I was single. Go ahead, I would say. I never considered paying, and no one ever followed through. But this time, I closed my laptop, walked to the bathroom, sat on the edge of the tub until my hands stopped shaking, and blocked the number. This time, I had something to lose. I was forced to face how stupid I was being.

I shared the story in therapy. My therapist, who usually lets me do most of the talking, leaned back, looked at his clipboard, and said, “What the fuck are you doing? This is like the 20th time something like this has happened.”

I didn’t have an answer. Nothing had ever happened to me. That was the whole point.

This past August, on a weekend trip to the Catskills with friends, I proposed to Maya. She said yes before I could finish the question. I meant every word I said that weekend.

Maya and I have now officially opened our relationship. We’ve mostly played as a couple. When she sees someone on her own, she tells me everything the next morning. Last month, I sexted with a woman for days and chose not to tell her. Though I have permission, I can’t seem to give up the secrecy.

Growing up in the houses I did, I hid the truth to keep the peace. Recovery taught me to say it anyway, but I’ve only ever practiced with people who can’t leave, like a sponsor or a therapist. Telling Maya is different. Sometimes, I reverse the question: What if she were the one carrying a secret like this? I like to think that I’d find a way to accept it, because through everything I’ve done, I have never loved her any less.

In the program, when you can’t make amends without causing further harm, you make what’s called living amends. It starts with stopping the behavior. Then you try to love better, act cleaner, and stay present day after day, without asking for absolution. That’s where I am now. I pray regularly that this pain is my consequence. If that’s my penance, I’ll carry it.
