#6915. Survey: Most Organizations Are Not Consistently Measuring AI Value Against Costs

A new study from consulting firm KPMG found that while AI investment continues to grow, relatively few organizations consistently measure whether the technology's benefits justify its costs. The company's Global AI Pulse Q3 2026 report found that 89% of organizations report measurable value from AI, while just 12% consistently assess that value against costs across the organization.

Average planned AI investment for the next 12 months has risen to $210 million, compared with $186 million in the first quarter of 2026.

The findings are based on responses from 2,131 senior leaders across 20 countries and jurisdictions, surveyed between July 23 and August 26, 2026.

Most organizations have established ways to track AI spending: 61% review costs during project approval, and 59% monitor them once systems are operating.

Consistently connecting those costs to value remains less common. Beyond the 12% that do so across the organization, another 26% assess value against costs for most initiatives.

Organizations reporting established AI returns have stronger measurement practices. Among this group, 48% consistently assess value against costs, four times the proportion in the overall survey group.

Cost-monitoring dashboards are also more common among organizations reporting established returns: 77% use them, compared with 43% of those still experimenting.

"The organizations seeing a return did the implementation and the transformation properly: guardrails in place, workforce ready, data cleaned up," said Priya Emmanuel, global head of aIQ at KPMG International. "That is a sequence, not a secret."

Organizations are also developing more formal systems to manage AI deployment.

Fifty-five percent operate a formal AI management layer, which KPMG calls a "harness layer," comprising controls and tools between AI models and business applications.

Security and identity controls, along with data access controls, were the most common capabilities, each reported by 43% of respondents. Model routing, which determines which model handles a particular task, was reported by 23%.

Meanwhile, 53% assign accountability for AI-informed decisions to a named executive, the CEO, or an executive committee.

The proportion reporting significant employee adoption of AI agents rose from 25% in the first quarter to 34% in the third quarter.

Cybersecurity concerns were more common among organizations with mature AI programs.

Half of those reporting established AI returns identified cybersecurity as a barrier, compared with roughly one-third of those still experimenting.

Overall, 86% of respondents said their organizations are adapting their cybersecurity operating models to address AI-accelerated threats.

The report identifies accountability, operational resilience, and cost management as priorities as AI becomes more embedded in business operations.

"Many organizations have made AI models widely available and track their use," said Swaminathan Chandrasekaran, head of the US AI Center of Excellence at KPMG. "But consumption alone is not a strategy."

#6908. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

#6904. Sophos Launches Tool for Shadow AI Defense

Unauthorized artificial intelligence tools and agents are creating security risks for organizations as AI adoption increases, according to cybersecurity company Sophos. In a recent product announcement, the company highlighted the growing use of shadow AI, referring to AI tools and agents operating without formal organizational approval or oversight. The security firm cited research indicating that 69% of organizations suspect or have evidence of unauthorized AI use.

Separate research from Gartner and Cloud Security Alliance found that 82% of organizations discovered shadow AI agents in their environments during the previous year.

According to the security firm, organizations have struggled to identify which AI tools employees use, what information those tools access, and whether their use complies with security policies.

"Organizations are adopting AI faster than they can secure it, and most security teams can't answer three basic questions: which AI tools are running, what data is going into them, and whether that activity lines up with policy," said Kyle Falkenhagen, senior vice president of product management at Sophos.

The cybersecurity firm announced the general availability of Sophos AI Defense, a security offering designed to identify AI tools, monitor their activity, and enforce organizational security policies.

The product operates through Sophos Fusion, the company's cybersecurity platform, and uses information from devices, networks, identity systems, and browsers.

According to the company, AI Defense can inspect prompts, uploads, and other AI interactions to identify potentially risky activity and help prevent sensitive data exposure.

#6898. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#6893. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#6888. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

The sample has an important limit: This year's study included only organizations where at least one ransomware attack caused business disruption. Those are survey responses about victimized organizations, rather than a controlled comparison of security products.

Chris Inglis, former U.S. national cyber director and a Semperis strategic adviser, described the difficulty of recognizing AI's involvement. "Many attacks are not perceived as being aided and abetted by AI because they're just too slick, too sophisticated," he said in the report.

#6761. 9 Interior Trends Designers Are Officially Ditching in 2027

Some of this year’s biggest design moments may not age well.

The end of the year is a great time to look back and reflect. When it comes to home design, there’s a lot to cover, from new trends to furniture collaboration drops to specific internet-breaking interiors. One of our favorite things to do around this time is figure out what’s coming next in terms of interiors.

We love predicting what’s going to be hot in home design for the upcoming seasons; however, you can’t talk about what’s going to be “in” without also touching on what’s going to be “out.” We asked a handful of designers to share what design choices and interior trends they saw a lot of in 2026 that they’re more than happy to leave behind in 2027. Keep reading to find out the nine things they said.

We spend so much time making our interiors warm and reflective of our personalities, it would be a shame to ruin the look of these spaces by opting for one-dimensional lighting.

“It's time to move beyond relying on a single overhead fixture or wall-to-wall recessed lighting to illuminate an entire room,” designer Kristen Mendoza of Four Brothers Design + Build says. “Flat, overly bright lighting can make even the most beautifully designed spaces feel sterile and uninviting.”

Instead, incorporating layers of lighting to add dimension and create a warm atmosphere is much better for your home. Sconces, picture lights, table lamps, and thoughtfully placed accent lighting at various heights throughout the room, Mendoza adds, give a space a more intimate touch.

“I also recommend using warm 2700K light sources on dimmers to flatter people, highlight architectural details, and bring out the natural beauty of materials,” she explains. “Lighting should be considered early in the design and construction process, not treated as an afterthought.”

Part of the appeal of “grandma chic” decor is that it’s been collected over time. The pieces show their age, the interior is imperfect, and it’s very clearly reflective of the homeowner’s personality and life. However, “there is a difference between a room that feels ‘collected over time’ and one that feels inherited all at once,” designer Stephanie Kraus explains, and she’s tired of seeing this false presentation on display in people’s homes.

“Heavy Roman shades with lots of fabric, chintz on top of chintz on top of chintz, fringe on every edge, etc.,” she adds. “One of these details in a room can be charming, but if you use them all together, the room can start to feel like a period movie set.”

It’s often clear when this eccentric aesthetic is manufactured rather than effortless. Instead of trying to make the “ideal” interior in the span of a month, be patient and work on your home over a greater span of time. That way, the end product will look authentic.

Though curved sofas are massively popular right now and look gorgeous in a photograph, Kraus is ready to leave them in 2026. “A curved sofa rarely seats more than two people comfortably,” she says. “Once guests settle in, everyone ends up sitting awkwardly, and conversation feels harder than it should.”

She’s all for a sofa or couch being something that invites people in to linger over conversations and stay late into the night, and a kidney-shaped seat isn’t going to do that.

While designer Maggie Vollrath of Provision Studios loved seeing the return of decorative, playful details like ruffles and scalloped edges, she thinks we’ve gone a bit overboard in 2026. These touches are being applied to just about everything: upholstery, pillows, lampshades, bedding, furniture—the list could go on.

“These details can be incredibly charming, but when they're repeated throughout an entire room, they begin to lose the very character that makes them special,” she says.

Rather than treat every piece of a room with one of these details, Vollrath suggests opting for a scalloped lampshade or a beautifully tailored ruffled pillow instead to still capture that charm without drowning the space in them.

“Limiting these details to one or two thoughtful applications allows them to feel intentional rather than overly thematic,” she adds. “Sometimes, the most interesting interiors are as much about what you choose to leave out as what you include.”

“I'd love to see us move away from selecting materials simply because they imitate the appearance of something more expensive,” Vollrath explains. This goes beyond marble-printed contact paper and burl wood veneer; the designer means on a larger scale, like mass-produced tile that’s made to replicate the imperfections of handmade zellige.

“Part of what makes authentic zellige so beautiful is the craftsmanship and natural variation that comes from its production,” she says. “When those qualities are artificially reproduced, I think we sometimes lose the very thing that made the original material special.”

She would rather see a greater appreciation for beautiful, honest materials at every price point, and if the exact thing a homeowner wants is out of budget, then her solution would be to pivot the idea altogether rather than find something cheaply made that looks like what they desire.

“A classic Carrara marble paired with beautifully considered cabinetry can create an incredibly sophisticated interior, even when something as dramatic as Calacatta Viola isn't within the budget,” Vollrath explains.

We could probably say this every year, but it bears repeating: Don’t solely follow trends! “Just because something is trending doesn't mean it's right for your home,” designer Drew Michael Scott of Lone Fox says. “Polka dots are having a moment right now, but they don't make sense in many homes, or dare I say, most homes.”

He suggests thinking of home trends as prompts that should get you to brainstorm new ideas for your space that actually fit your style. Take polka dots again. If you want to introduce a new pattern to your home, but you don’t particularly mesh with polka dots, try a stripe instead.

“If you try to force something you don't love into your home just because it's trending, you're going to drive yourself crazy,” he adds. Let’s leave this line of thinking back in 2026 once and for all.

While we love a good built-in bookshelf and wooden ceiling beam, those types of architectural details don’t always make sense in every single home. “Decorative beams, arches, and elaborate millwork can feel forced when they don't make sense with the architecture of the home,” designer Dani Flax of Pearson Design Group argues. “I think there's a tendency to add these elements to make a space feel more interesting, but the result can actually take away from the architecture itself.”

A home in a modern farmhouse style, for example, would look a bit odd with an intricate ceiling medallion and excessive picture frame molding. Design your interior around the existing architecture and the style of your home, don’t incorporate these elements simply because they’re everywhere right now.

The next year is not about playing it safe with your interiors—designers are tired of the stark white home. While ubiquitous gray and white color schemes had their time, they can often make a home feel generic and devoid of character.

“In their place, we're embracing more intentional, tonal palettes and color drenching,” Mendoza says.

“Taking a considered color, whether it's a deep green, warm clay, or earthy mushroom, and carrying it across walls, trim, and cabinetry creates a cohesive, enveloping effect,” Mendoza adds. “The goal isn't necessarily to introduce more color, but to use it with greater confidence and purpose.”

This is definitely a hot take, but designer John Maloney McNamara of Coosa Interiors is ready to leave velvet upholstery behind. “I’ve tried, but velvet almost always reads as either a 1990s prom limo or a hotel lobby trying very hard,” he explains. “The sheen shows every crush and bruise, and it ages into something that looks tired rather than loved.”

If you love the look of velvet, he suggests going with a mohair fabric instead, saying it has all of velvet’s depth and none of its shine. It also only gets better with age. And though he knows creamy bouclé has been overdone, Maloney McNamara thinks it deserves a reprise, but only in a rich color that can replace velvet still.

From the kitchen hacks you’d never expect (like swapping milk for boiling water) to the little techniques that make all the difference in your final bake, this is Nigella Lawson’s best baking advice of all time.

Cooking might not require exact measurements, but baking definitely does. “I think every recipe has to be as precise as possible,” Lawson told Vogue in 2018. “Baking, you have to be really precise. If you’re cooking and it suggests 2 to 3 carrots, it doesn’t really matter because they can be different sizes. It’s not going to alter the taste.”

So, you’ve never really baked before. It’s okay to start small! A three-tier chantilly cake your first day in the kitchen probably isn’t your best bet. “It’s important to go for a simple recipe. Read through the recipe to make sure you understand everything that’s being told to you at the time,” she said. “Look for a recipe that gives you an indication of what to do at each stage. This is what I always say to people: remind yourself you’re not running a restaurant.”

If you’ve been making your chocolate cake with milk, here’s your sign to stop. Lawson suggests swapping the dairy for hot or boiling water instead to “bloom” the cocoa, dissolve the sugar, and loosen the batter. The result? A crazy moist crumb!

Instead of using plain water when baking bread, Lawson suggests swapping it out for leftover pasta or potato water. “It will help the bread’s texture and rise,” she wrote on X in 2020. “Taste it before adding, though. If very salty, dilute it with fresh water and, obviously, leave any salt in the recipe out.”

It might sound weird, but Lawson swears by cream of tartar for fluffier, softer scones. “These are the best [ones] I’ve ever eaten, which is quite how it should be since they emanate from one of those old-fashioned cooks who starts a batch the minute the doorbell rings at teatime,” she wrote on her website in 2017. “Yes, I know they look as if they’ve got cellulite—it’s the cream of tartar, which is also why, despite their apparent solidity, they have that dreamy lightness.”

Lawson’s genius baking hack? DIY self-rising flour. “Nigella tends not to use self-raising flour in her more recent books as she doesn’t bother to keep both plain (all-purpose) and self-raising flour,” her site reads. “Partly because keeping just one type of flour saves on storage space, and partly because if you don’t use self-raising flour regularly, then it will lose its raising power over time.”

Instead, simply add 2 teaspoons of baking powder for each cup of plain flour. Sift the flour and baking powder together into a bowl before using (and make sure the baking powder is fully integrated), and you’re golden!

We’re all a little extra busy around the holidays, so if you’re planning a dinner party or holiday get-together, it’s okay to make shortcuts. Even Lawson says not everything has to be baked from scratch.

“I have a bit of a weakness for this sort of cake, which is essentially an assembly-job number, a construction of alternate layers of a rich, boozy cream and some store-bought sponge or other,” she told the BBC in 2023 about one of her favorite holiday desserts. “Here the layers comprise a confection of cream and mascarpone, seasonally spiked with Advocaat, full of crystallized ginger, figs and nuts, and that essential component of Christmas: gingerbread.”

Fashion’s love affair with the silver screen stretches across decades. Coco Chanel designed costumes for two Jean Renoir films; Hubert de Givenchy created Audrey Hepburn’s black sheath dress for Breakfast at Tiffany’s. Today, the relationship runs in both directions. Julian Klausner’s spring/summer 2027 collection for Dries Van Noten borrowed the language of cinema through imagined movie posters splashed on slips and party skirts, while the Angelina Jolie-starring Couture turned its attention to the women behind the seams.

Letterboxd has turned cinephilia into a social, gamified experience, while celebrities now revel in the chance to highlight their most obscure Criterion Collection favorites. Fashion, ever attuned to shifts in culture, has followed suit, moving beyond the familiar fields of costume design and cinematic campaigns to support and sometimes produce filmmaking itself.

This year’s program brought together mentors such as Celine Song and Gina Prince-Bythewood, who worked directly with participants.

Rosenthal points out that the number of female-directed films has, unfortunately, dipped in recent years. After over a decade of the program, “What we’re finding is that the community is really intentional,” she says. “Our advisory board wants to be here. They come back year after year. They want to be part of it, because they want to help support the next generation.” Miu Miu’s Women’s Tales, established by Miuccia Prada in 2011, has similarly become a platform for female filmmakers—including Ava DuVernay, Claire Denis, and Mona Fastvold—to tell original stories. Those initiatives were early signs of a broader shift.

And one of the most accessible forms of storytellingis film.”

Like Gondry’s lovers disappearing into the subway station, the most memorable fashion stories don’t end when the collection arrives on the runway or on the shop floor. They only become more interesting out on the streets, where clothes become characters and the city becomes the set.

More research is needed to understand whether these sweeteners directly contribute to cognitive changes,” she adds.

Zwerling elaborates on these potential mechanisms that may be at the root of it:

Artificial sweeteners may alter the composition and function of the gut microbiome. These changes may influence inflammation. “In other words, there is a change in the communication between the gut and brain,” Zwerling explains.

Some sweeteners may influence insulin secretion, glucose metabolism, and hormonal responses. “Insulin signaling is important for neuronal function, synaptic plasticity, and memory. Truly, what we eat affects how we think,” Zwerling cautions. Additionally, she notes that empirical studies have shown that consuming aspartame can lead to increased cytokine IL1β—a signaling protein in the body that regulates immune and inflammatory responses—thereby inducing systemic inflammation as well as suppression of tumor necrosis factor (TNF) alpha, another naturally occurring protein that helps coordinate immune responses, regulate cell survival or cell death, and increase inflammation. Zwerling notes that further research needs to be done to see how artificial sweeteners impair signaling and promote inflammation in humans, specifically related to the biomarker pathways associated with Alzheimer’s disease.

The 2025 study raises concern that regular consumption of certain low- and no-calorie sweeteners may be associated with accelerated cognitive decline. However, it does not establish that artificial sweeteners cause neurodegeneration or dementia. Not all artificial sweeteners affect neurotransmitters the same way. “The study showed this risk in the younger cohort. Given that artificial sweeteners limit sugary beverages and risk for obesity, further studies are needed as midlife obesity has been associated with risk of cognitive decline,” Zwerling clarifies.

Preclinical and computational research has proposed potential inflammatory and molecular pathways through which aspartame could influence biological processes relevant to Alzheimer’s disease. However, these mechanisms have not been established in human studies, and their relevance at typical dietary exposure levels remains uncertain.

The answer depends on several factors, mainly a person’s health history and potential risks. Zwerling says that some sweeteners may influence gut microbiome composition. Others may affect glucose metabolism and insulin responses. She also notes that observational studies have raised questions about cardiovascular and cognitive health. Replacing sugar with non-nutritive sweeteners can reduce caloric and sugar intake, but Zweling cautions that this does not necessarily translate into long-term health benefits.

Brief agrees, adding that we shouldn’t automatically label artificial sweeteners as bad, but we also shouldn’t assume that replacing sugar with a zero-calorie alternative automatically makes a product healthier. “Different sweeteners have different properties and potential physiological effects. For someone who regularly consumes sugar-sweetened beverages, switching to an artificially sweetened, lower-calorie, lower-sugar option may be a practical way to reduce added sugar intake,” Brief explains. “Just like everything in nutrition and wellness, this is relative to a person’s individual diet.” She encourages people to gradually reduce their reliance on intensely sweetened foods and beverages and prioritize minimally processed foods. “The goal isn’t to eliminate carbohydrates or naturally occurring sugars, but to be mindful of excessive added sugar intake,” says Brief.

According to Zwerling, no established amount of artificial sweetener has been demonstrated to be optimal for long-term brain health. According to Zweling, certain regulatory agencies have established acceptable daily intake (ADI) levels for individual sweeteners. “These represent amounts considered safe for daily lifetime consumption, not thresholds at which cognitive effects have been ruled out.” She reiterates that excessive added sugar consumption is associated with adverse metabolic health and may indirectly contribute to cognitive decline through insulin resistance, inflammation, and vascular disease.

She adds that neither excessive sugar nor heavy reliance on artificial sweeteners should substitute for a nutritionally adequate dietary pattern. For brain health, Zweling says a reasonable approach is to minimize excess added sugar and avoid relying heavily on artificial sweeteners as a long-term substitute. A dietary pattern emphasizing whole foods, fruits, vegetables, fiber, and minimally processed foods is preferable. Brief co-signs the advice: “I generally encourage people to avoid excessive reliance on sweeteners and focus on building a diet that doesn’t require everything to taste sweet.” She recommends exploring ways to add natural sweetness without relying heavily on sugar substitutes. “Fresh or frozen fruit, mashed banana, unsweetened applesauce, cinnamon, and vanilla extract are all great options. Honey, maple syrup, coconut sugar, and date syrup are better-for-you, less-processed options that are still sources of added sugar,” says Brief.

Beyond the research pool, Zweling adds that work is needed, especially in food desert areas, to ensure these nutritious foods are available. “This emphasizes the need for health providers and patients/caregivers to work with community-based organizations to ensure access to healthy foods,” Zweling stresses. “Brain health is not simply a matter of telling people what to eat; it is also about ensuring they have access to the foods that support healthy aging,” she concludes.

#6722. You May Be Thawing Your Turkey Wrong—Here’s How To Avoid Spreading Salmonella

A frozen bird can save you money, but cutting corners could come at a cost.

Thanksgiving is a marathon, not a sprint. And the most involved, time-consuming dish is undoubtedly the turkey. The centerpiece of your holiday table needs hours in the oven and at least a day of brining, but don't forget the time it takes to defrost beforehand, too.

Frozen turkeys are generally the more accessible and affordable option. But they do require a little extra TLC and planning. So if you buy a frozen bird this year, you'll need to know how to work with it.

Making sure it tastes good is one thing, but ensuring that it's safe to eat is something else entirely. Like chicken, turkey can spread Salmonella and other harmful bacteria. You should exercise caution at every stage of the process, including thawing, to avoid making your guests sick. Here’s what you should know.

The USDA says it's also safe to defrost turkey in cold water, but in our opinion, it's not worth the hassle. The best bet is to thaw it in the refrigerator, where the bird can gradually defrost at the safe temperature with less risk of bacterial growth. What you should never do: thaw it on the counter, in the garage, or with warm water.

These environments are less controlled and can expose the turkey to the temperature danger zone (between 40°F and 140°F), where germs grow rapidly. Even if the center of the turkey is still frozen, the thawed exterior can become a hotbed for Salmonella by the time you're ready to roast.

Roasted turkey drippings make great gravy. But raw turkey juice dripping all over your fridge? Not so much. Even shrink-wrapped birds can leak, so you should always store your turkey on a baking sheet or roasting pan while it thaws.

Food safety experts also recommend storing raw meat on the lowest shelf. That way, any leaks are less likely to drip onto other ingredients in your fridge.

We know you're probably keeping a lot of other ingredients in your fridge, so it can be hard to set aside extra room for the turkey. That said, it's a step worth doing. Giving your turkey plenty of personal space minimizes the risk of raw meat or its juices coming into contact with other ingredients.

In the event that your turkey does contaminate another ingredient, here's what to know: Foods that won’t be cooked, like salad greens, should be tossed if they’ve come into contact with raw turkey juices. Cooking contaminated foods thoroughly can kill harmful bacteria, but you’ll also need to clean any surfaces the juices touched.

Turkeys are big, so they take a long time to thaw all the way through. The experts at Butterball say you should allocate a day for every four pounds of turkey if you're keeping it in the fridge. That means if you're working with a 16-pound bird, set aside four days just for defrosting. Once it's fully thawed, Butterball says your turkey will stay safe in the fridge for up to four days.

And on top of that, brining can make your turkey even more flavorful. If your frozen bird didn’t come pre-seasoned, consider setting aside another day or two for that step. (Want some guidance? Check out our brining guide.)

It’s extremely important to keep things clean while you’re handling a turkey. One of the most common ways Salmonella spreads in the kitchen is through cross-contamination caused by poor hygiene.

So wash those hands (including under your fingernails!) with soap and water for at least 20 seconds. Make sure you also thoroughly clean any utensils and surfaces that come into contact with raw turkey or its juices using hot, soapy water.

And above all, don’t stress! Americans prepare millions of turkeys every Thanksgiving, so you're not alone in your turkey-roasting journey. Follow the tips above to ensure yours turns out safe to eat and delicious.
