#6847. The 5 Types of Coats Every Guy Should Own

A great winter outfit starts with the right outerwear.

Years ago, I was talking to a very menswear-y buddy at a very menswear-y event, and he proposed a theory of outerwear that’s stuck with me: Your coat is your car. Sounds pretty … menswear-y, right? But bear with me here, because I think it actually makes perfect sense, so long as you’re willing to make a couple (major) concessions.

No, your coat cannot help you travel at 75 miles per hour down an interstate. You cannot fill it up with gasoline or top it up at a charging station. It contains, ideally, zero percent steel.

But if you live in a place where you spend more time walking or taking public transit than you do behind the wheel, your coat does indeed become quite car-like. You ensconce yourself in it when you move about the world. It’s the first thing people see when you arrive and the last thing they see when you depart. It shields you from the elements while simultaneously signaling to friends and strangers alike your combined senses of utility and aesthetics. So, yeah, if you squint and look at the situation sideways, your coat is your car. Even if you live somewhere that demands personal automotive transportation, your coat is your second car—unless you’re one of those kooky types who just dart from the interior of a sedan to the interior of a restaurant in nothing but a sweater—for all the reasons stated above.

The point is: Your coat is the most visible and functional part of your winter wardrobe, and you should approach the buying and wearing of said coat with all due respect for its position. Which is exactly why I created this guide. We’ll run through the key styles that every guy should know, where to get a great example (or three!) of each, and how to wear them with style all season long. You won’t find truckers or bombers here—those are jackets—but you will find parkas, topcoats, trenches, and more. All of them will serve you well until the spring thaw, so let’s get into it.

If you’ve been keeping up with Esquire style guides, you’ll know that we’ve covered a lot of ground over the past few years, breaking down everything from the shoe styles you should have in your rotation to the winter essentials you shouldn’t go without. One story that really resonated with readers was about sweaters. In it, we talked about the fact that you can score very good knitwear from a huge range of lifestyle brands these days. We also mentioned the prudence of keeping a few category specialists on your radar. The same advice holds true for coats.

On the lifestyle side, the brands will look familiar to longtime readers. You’ve got Todd Synder and Buck Mason. J. Press and J. Crew. Ralph Lauren and Drake’s and Alpha Industries and Madewell and Billy Reid and Filson ... the list goes on. You’ve also got fashion behemoths like Burberry, which famously started out with the trench coat. And then you’ve got brands that may not just do outerwear but are truly steeped in it. That could be a heritage player like Mackintosh or Schott or Gloverall, a parka expert like Canada Goose, or an upstart label like Wythe, which makes a wonderful range of products but has a cult following for its exceptional overcoats.

As we’ve warned before: This list is not exhaustive. But it’s a good place to start. Now, on to the coats themselves.

This is the dressiest of the bunch, though it wasn’t always. If you’re a real traditionalist, you might have a velvet-collared Chesterfield in your closet to wear with your fanciest togs. Most of us, though, don’t really need something that formal. The topcoat is similar to and derives from the Chesterfield, but it’s less stuffy.

Still, it looks plenty dressed up to modern eyes. It’ll have lapels, which can be notch or peak (for a little extra personality), and can be single- or double-breasted. If you suit up with any regularity, that means it’s likely the most essential for your wardrobe. Look for one made of wool or a wool blend; this is not the time to overspend on synthetics.

Colors are usually pretty conservative—think camel, navy, gray, and black. That might seem limiting, but remember that this thing should be able to play nicely with your whole wardrobe, so opting for a more subdued hue is usually the right move. As for length: Some folks will swear something that hits above the knee looks more modern. We just think it looks off. Go for at or below the knee.

(Oh, and if you’re wondering if a classic polo coat really counts as a topcoat as opposed to an overcoat, well, you might be right to question us. Technically. But considering its cultural associations—rich New Englanders, Wall Street power brokers—we’re giving it a home here.)

Eschewed by many as a bit too fusty and old-fashioned as recently as a decade ago, the raglan overcoat has come roaring back into the spotlight in recent years. We enthusiastically endorse its return.

The style is defined by its raglan shoulder construction, in which a diagonal seam runs from around the collarbone to the underarm, allowing for an uninterrupted panel over the top of the shoulder. Don’t worry too much about the technical details, though. The main thing to remember is that this shape is more relaxed and tends to layer over other garments (a chunky sweater, a sport coat) more easily and comfortably than a traditional “set in” shoulder seam.

The raglan overcoat also tends to be cut wide and long, so you can bundle up underneath it and never worry about chilly knees. It leans more casual than a topcoat, for sure, but you can still wear it with a suit. And it lends polish to something as simple as jeans and a sweatshirt. One of our favorite subgenres of raglan overcoat is the balmacaan, which features a Prussian collar (which looks like a shirt collar instead of a lapel), a “fly front” with a placket that covers the buttons, and buttoned fasteners at the wrists so you can cinch up to keep out the cold.

Wythe Raglan Overcoat in Diamond Ombre Tweed

If you’re into the warm and wooly vibes of the two styles above but simply can’t stand a long coat, the peacoat is for you. Double-breasted and hip length, it’s a shorter—but no less capable—take on the woolen overcoat that dates back about three centuries.

Originally created for sailors of European (and, eventually, American) navies, the peacoat is tough enough to defend from open-ocean winds and sea spray, which means it’s definitely up to the task of keeping you toasty on your way to work. There are a lot of versions out there these days that are done up in frankly wimpy wool blends that might feel nice at first but don’t do the job.

Go for a heavyweight wool like melton, but make sure there’s enough room to layer up underneath it—and keep an eye on the lapels. They should be big. Big enough that you can pop that collar and shield yourself from an incoming gust without feeling even a tickle of wind touch your neck. Plus, it looks cool.

J. Crew Dock Peacoat in Wool Blend with PrimaLoft

Thanks to a quirky little phenomenon we like to call “global climate change,” there’s a very good chance that some of the winter days ahead will feel a lot more like fall or spring. On those days—especially if it’s raining—you should reach for a trench coat or a mac. The two styles aren’t synonymous, but they’re related in both vibe and history, so we’re coupling them here.

Maximalists will prefer the trench, with its storm flap and epaulettes and double-breasted closure. Minimalists will reach for the mac, with its clean lines and streamlined shape. It’s really a matter of preference, because both fulfill the same roles in your outerwear lineup.

They’ll keep you warmish, especially when combined with additional layers. They’ll help block the wind when it kicks up. And although we can’t guarantee you’ll walk away dry if you stand around for an hour during a deluge, they’ll fend off a shower—or a sprint through a thunderstorm—quite capably.

Burberry Long Kensington Heritage Trench Coat

If it’s really fucking frigid, you need a parka. Make sure it’s properly insulated, whether with a synthetic fill or down. And unless you like freezing your literal ass off, get one that’s hip length or longer. You could get something technical and bright that looks like it just stepped off the ski slopes, but we’re more inclined toward rugged-looking styles that take their cues from the military and outdoor exploration.

The N-3B, or “snorkel parka,” is a burly beast that looks absolutely fantastic with a casual outfit and, if you ask us, can be fun with a suit too. (Though if you’re worried about raising eyebrows or breaking a dress code, maybe opt for a topcoat instead.) Something like Canada Goose’s Langdon parka is a little more pared back in looks but overdelivers on warmth, meaning you’ll be cozy in even the worst weather. The trick is finding one that balances form and function so that it looks as though you intended to wear it on the way to the bar—not like you wandered off some snowcapped mountain and forgot to change.

Like the peacoat, the duffle coat is a part of maritime history. It was adopted by the British Royal Navy in the late 19th century and widely issued to British troops during World War II. Thanks to the sheer number produced and postwar surplus, it quickly became a civilian staple and eventually hopped across the pond to become a fixture of the American preppy uniform. Made of thick wool and equipped with a big hood, it’s very warm. And thanks to the large toggle closures, you don’t even need to take your gloves off to fasten it. Is it a little odd? Perhaps. But that’s what makes it so charming.

#6843. Survey Finds 84% of Organizations Reported at Least One AI-Related Incident in the Past Year

Companies are running into problems as they give artificial intelligence more control over critical business applications, with 84% reporting at least one AI-related incident in the past year, according to a new survey from identity security firm Delinea.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Those gaps can make it difficult to identify segregation-of-duties conflicts, in which a person or agent has permissions across systems that should be kept separate.

Despite those issues, 95% of respondents said they were confident their access controls would withstand scrutiny in an external audit.

However, 26% reported access-related audit findings that had recurred in consecutive years, while 25% said some findings had remained unresolved for three or more years.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

Meanwhile, 60% reported an increase over the past two years in the number of critical applications operating outside the scope of access governance or Sarbanes-Oxley Act compliance.

The findings highlight the oversight challenges associated with giving AI agents authority to act within business systems, particularly when organizations cannot consistently identify who owns an agent or trace its actions.

Frank Vukovits, chief security scientist at Delinea, said organizations remain responsible for the actions AI agents take within their systems.

"When an agent can approve a transaction or change permissions, the business owns the outcome. You need to know what every agent can do, who owns it, and who approved it, before an auditor asks or your financials are misstated," Vukovits said.

#6839. Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

The findings echo a recent report about management software and AI e-mail assistants. Research from Fortra and Barracuda showed how criminals are using legitimate administrative tools and hidden AI instructions to manipulate users and compromise systems.

#6834. Study: Sign-In Problems Are Primary Drivers of Help Desk Tickets

Sign-in problems account for up to 53% of help desk tickets at individual enterprises, but network issues can take significantly longer to resolve, according to a new study from ControlUp.

Analyzing 1,069,110 help desk tickets, the IT operations company identified password resets, account lockouts, and multifactor authentication (MFA) issues as major drivers of support demand.

At one large enterprise, account-related tickets took a median of 11 minutes to resolve, compared with 50 hours for network problems. Meanwhile, 87% of network and phone tickets were reassigned between teams, compared with 20% of account tickets.

MFA enrollment and re-enrollment problems appeared at 40% of organizations, with one in five linking these issues to employees replacing or upgrading their phones. Virtual desktop problems appeared at 55% of organizations analyzed.

Traditional phone support remained a major channel for IT assistance.

Among organizations that recorded how support requests were submitted, 57% reported that phone calls accounted for approximately two-thirds or more of contacts. At one organization, that figure reached 99%.

Machine-generated tickets from monitoring, security, backup, and patching systems appeared at half of the organizations studied. At 15%, automated tickets represented between 25% and 50% of all requests.

The research covered enterprises across retail, healthcare, financial services, manufacturing, construction, energy, and IT services.

ControlUp said the findings highlight the need to evaluate recurring problems and their operational impact rather than relying solely on ticket volume and resolution speed.

"IT organizations have traditionally measured support performance by how quickly they close tickets, but that doesn't tell the whole story," said Simon Townsend, senior vice president of marketing and office of the CTO at ControlUp.

"A password reset that takes a few minutes and an infrastructure failure that disrupts an entire location shouldn't carry the same operational weight."

#6829. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

Gianandrea Redaelli, a car nut whose family has long roots in the Italian printing business, is firmly in the “road book” camp. In early September, I climbed into the copilot seat of his 1939 Aston Martin C-Type Speed Model Roadster Bertelli—just one of two ever built, with an experimental aerodynamic body that earned it the nickname “the Flying Pea”—and stayed there as he drove six hundred kilometers of “left at the traffic lights, right at the cow” for the Louis Vuitton Dolomites Classic Run. The rally started in Venice and ended, after a scenic but challenging detour high into the Dolomite mountains, at Monza, the home of Italian Formula 1, just in time for practice day of the Grand Prix.

Venice might not seem the most logical starting line for a car rally given the proverbial wetness of the streets. But the city was chosen this year because it was also the end point of the last LV rally of its kind: the La Serenissima Run from Monte Carlo to Venice in 2012. Gianandrea won that one as copilot, with his father, Giuseppe, at the wheel. The pair also won the one before it—the Boheme across Hungary, the Czech Republic, and Austria—in 2006. This time, however, the pressure was on me too.

I have been a copilot before, and I can tell you it’s no picnic. Rally racing in vintage cars is less about speed and more about timing. It’s an exercise that requires pinpoint accuracy at the time-stamp points in the overall course and, most critically, in the regularities. These are fiendishly short time-over-distance sections in which crossing the line just a few hundredths of a second late—or early—earns strict penalties. Sensibly, the day before the race began, Gianandrea took me out in the Aston in the Veneto countryside to practice, especially those critical regularities.

I could have opted for an easy life. The rally allowed competing cars, dating from 1931 to 1969, to use the road book or follow the course using a map in a dedicated phone app. But where’s the fun in that?

“If you join a competition, you follow the regulations and strive to do your best,” Gianandrea says. “As an Italian, if you paint numbers on the door of my car, I will want to win.”

Every car in a rally like this comes with its own idiosyncrasies. On flat ground, someone driving a souped-up 2026 sports car would find these tests difficult to nail. In the reality of race conditions, the regularities often involve steep climbs or descents and unexpected corners, alling for an expert hand at the wheel—and foot on the gas pedal. The tendency of some car doors to swing open at hard bends (ours has the “suicide” doors with hinges on the back instead of the front, beloved by early carmakers) only adds to the excitement. The purpose of driver and copilot is to battle and overcome all the mechanical and computational challenges—and get our ride to the finish line bang on time.

Louis Vuitton has been involved in these glamorous and exacting vintage rallies since 1993. The Dolomites Run represents an enthusiastic return to the genre after a fourteen-year gap and, perhaps tellingly, the company’s global sponsorship (since 2025) of Formula 1. Unconfirmed rumors circulated in the field that the choice of ending the rally at a Grand Prix could well be repeated—and soon—in other countries in the future.

It’s easy, after all, to see why a fusion of style and cars with six- and seven-figure price tags might appeal to Louis Vuitton and competitors alike. Not surprisingly, at every coffee and lunch stop, the LV effect sprinkled the race with an added sense of luxury. In between, our snaking caravan carried the glamour of a century of motoring along with it. At every hairpin you got a glimpse of a gull-wing Mercedes, a 1931 Bugatti two-seater (my personal fave), and a shimmering behemoth of a Bentley in quick succession. These cars would turn heads anywhere. But in Italy, arguably the home of the sports car, they brought smiles, applause, and gasps in even the tiniest village that passed by in our slipstream. I swear I even saw a nun with a glint in her eye at one set of traffic lights.

Navigating, I was quickly learning, is not so much about providing a constant babble of information as it is knowing how to give clear and simple instructions—and only when strictly needed. As the finish neared in Monza at the end of two long days on the road, I was able to give Gianandrea the most helpful instruction of all: “Nothing for 15 kilometers.”

“That,” he very kindly replied, “is the mark of a pro.”

Even after the engines cooled, we were still clueless about how well we’d done overall. That changed at the final event, a dinner inside the Sforzesco Castle in Milan. Gianandrea stepped up, beaming, as winner of the race, and for the first time as driver. I stood somewhat sheepishly behind him as copilot as he received the trophy from Pietro Beccari, CEO of both Louis Vuitton and Louis Vuitton Fashion Group. It was an impressive swirl of Murano glass created by Venini, housed in its own equally impressive traveling case, made (naturellement) at Louis Vuitton’s atelier in Asnières.

Gianandrea has promised me visiting rights.

#6794. “It Was Never About Fashion”: Emily in Paris’s Costume Designer Reveals the Method to Her Madness

“Emily in Paris: Fashion Without Rules,” a new show at SCAD Fashion in Atlanta, is devoted to the series’s unique style

Emily in Paris premiered when audiences needed it most. The Netflix original, created by Sex and the City producer Darren Star, offered an escapist fantasy: a young girl trying to find love and success in Paris in October 2020. The show functioned as a reprieve from the pandemic, a dreamy alternative in which we could vicariously live through Emily (Lily Collins) as she gallivanted around the City of Light. It was and still is delectably digestible television.

The love for Emily in Paris was anything but unanimous. Critics wasted no time tearing the show apart, with some targeting its depictions of French culture or claiming the script lacked the bite of Star’s previous television shows. The criticism extended to Emily’s idiosyncratic wardrobe, with some writing that the clothing leaned hard on American stereotypes of French fashion or looked cartoonish, with clashing patterns and bright colors. Costume designer Marylin Fitoussi took these criticisms head-on and doubled down. Emily’s outfits weren’t meant to be couture or designer; they were meant to dress a girl finding her sense of self, expressing her desire to explore the unknown.

For six seasons, Fitoussi curated a wardrobe fit for Emily, a bright-eyed young woman unafraid to try something new, to break the rules. This mirrors Fitoussi’s personal philosophy on clothing: that fashion is meant to break rules, to push boundaries, to provide a space of unrelenting experimentation. Ahead of the sixth season, SCAD FASH Museum of Fashion + Film compiles six seasons’ worth of outfits in “Emily in Paris: Fashion Without Rules,” on view through March 14, 2027. The exhibition lives up to its name; for the designer, clothing is a mode of expression that lets anyone feel free, wherever they are. It offers a character study, tracing Emily’s evolution from a doe-eyed expat to a well-versed Parisian resident. (And worry not, there are also looks from Emily’s chic boss Sylvie—played with devastating elegance by Philippine Leroy-Beaulieu.)

Ahead of season 6, Bazaar sat down with Fitoussi at SCAD Fashion in Atlanta to discuss working with Lily Collins, her swan song for Emily in Paris, and how everyone has the potential for creativity.

Marylin Fitoussi: I don’t recall a contemporary TV show being in the spotlight in this way. Generally, when you think of an exhibition, you think of period pieces or couture pieces. The show is really part of pop culture now!

MF: I was surprised when fashion magazines [criticized the show] at the beginning. I’m not a fashion designer, [but] I love fashion and I love clothes. This show was not about fashion at the beginning. [Emily] is a bubbly character with a very strong personality from Chicago; she couldn’t have come dressed in Comme des Garçons or in Yamamoto. I needed to have this cultural shock. She’s a fish-out-of-water character, and we needed something to catch the audience’s eyes. When you want to show people that you love the culture but you can’t express it because you don’t know the codes, you don’t speak the language, the only way you find it is through clothes. It was never about “fashion.” Sometimes, it’s about creativity, individuality, making something not trendy at all. The audience got that, and fashion magazines didn’t get it, so they killed me.

MF: Many times, I’ve asked myself, “Why does this little gingham suit have so much influence?” It’s quite simple. It was the most duplicated, copied, reposted, fan-arted, and used as a Halloween costume. It touched people because it was for them. The French can have their own version of it, their own concept, but for the entire world, it was this one little girly, timeless outfit with the red beret, the red lips, the red soul.

MF: She read the bad press after the release of season 1, and we had a talk. I said they hated it in France; they find it hideous. [I asked her] “Do you want me to change [it]?” She was smiling and said, “No, we can’t, and we won’t please everybody. We need to keep this strong DNA. We need to continue to have this unexpected silhouette because it’s what catches the audience’s eye.” It’s a guilty pleasure. We can say, I love it.

It opens a way for the audience to see fashion in a different way, where you can mix in an awkward way and free people from this law where you shouldn’t [do that]. Coco Chanel used to say, “Before leaving a room, look at yourself in the mirror and take something out.” I always say, “Put something more on.”

MF: I wanted to present a more elevated version, more mature, more evolved, but still true to what she was and what she is, with an eclectic combination and a little bit awkward. She knows the French rules, but as I do, she finds French fashion so boring. In general, I find that fashion is less and less interesting. Why are fashion and celebrity stylists sourcing more and more from archives? What it’s showing is that now everything is the same. Everything is boring, and I understand why. They need to sell the show! We don’t have to sell anything. We have to sell dreams, illusions, craziness, boldness. People watch the show in groups. Some people say, “I love it,” and some say, “I hate it,” and they have a dialogue and fight and have a good [time]. We love that.

MF: A young designer wants to be famous right away. Fame doesn’t happen, and it doesn’t matter. You need to be happy with what you’re doing. With costume design versus fashion design, you can be much freer. You don’t have anything to sell, and you don’t have to design the “It bag.” If your show is successful, it’s a win-win for everybody. To see that, you need to be true to your vision. You need to be true to your gut feeling, to your vision, to your DNA. Talent is not enough; you need to be at the right place at the right time. Being discovered by Darren Star … he changed my life. At some point, you find the right project for you.

You need to work a lot to take care of every detail. You need to be strong and push the boundaries. You need to try to show a different way to see costumes and clothes, to see that everything is possible…. Style begins when fear ends. You don’t want to please everybody, and I’m always attracted to people who dress in a different way, and I’m never judgmental. To create something is to expose yourself. It’s very vulnerable. It’s brave.

#6766. Azzedine Alaïa Changed Fashion on His Own Terms

A new exhibition showcases 80 garments from the rule-breaking designer.

In the 1980s, Azzedine Alaïa acquired a title that followed him for the rest of his career: the “King of Cling.” The moniker was a fitting one: the Tunisian couturier was renowned for his sculptural, body-centric designs. He experimented with stretch jersey and “anti-stress” fibers to sculpt the body without restricting movement.

But the nickname was also limiting, failing to capture the full scope of his work. The first major U.S. retrospective devoted to Alaïa, “Azzedine Alaïa: Sculpting Fashion,” at San Francisco’s Legion of Honor, brings his broader legacy into focus through 80 garments and selections from his personal collection of historic fashion.

“As my research went on, I came to realize how important the U.S. market was in Alaïa’s launch,” Laura L. Camerlengo, co-curator of the exhibit and curator in charge of Costume and Textile Arts at the Fine Arts Museums of San Francisco, tells ELLE. “By the [brand’s] third season, the U.S. was the second highest market after France, [with interest from] both luxury department stores and small independent retailers.”

The exhibition took shape after Camerlengo reached out to Olivier Saillard, director of the Fondation Azzedine Alaïa in Paris, who became her co-curator. As visitors enter the Legion of Honor’s Rosekrans Court, they encounter a dress designed for Grace Jones: a spring/summer 1986 haute couture look that Jones modeled on the runway and wore to the 1985 Oscars de la Mode. Part of the opening display, titled “The Women Who Made Alaïa,” it sets the tone for a show that recognizes the muses—both famous and private clients—who wore and supported his work.

Also on view are looks made famous by Rihanna—namely, a sheer red halter gown with a flowing train that she wore to the 2013 Grammys—and Tina Turner, whose silk-organza dress embroidered with gold-tone beads from Alaïa’s spring/summer 1989 concludes the exhibition.

The show then turns to Alaïa’s iconography, as Camerlengo puts it: the bandage dress, his work in denim, and zippered dresses, as well as rare, lesser-seen gems. “I was really keen to have early works on view, particularly the pieces from the 1980s, when he was launching his brand,” the curator says. “These are pieces that would have been shown at Bergdorf Goodman when he had his first fashion show in the United States.”

The exhibition also places Alaïa’s designs in conversation with the museum’s permanent collection. Belle Epoque-style ensembles from his spring/summer 1992 collection appear among 18th-century European paintings and furnishings, including a Canaletto view of Venice and a sofa commissioned for Marie Antoinette.

Alaïa’s own collection of historic fashion, previously shown in the 2023 Paris exhibition “Azzedine Alaïa, couturier collectionneur,” is represented here, too. The gallery includes pieces by fashion houses he admired, such as Lanvin, Chanel, Balenciaga, and Schiaparelli.

Though Alaïa, who died in 2017 at age 82, is remembered for the body-conscious designs that earned him that “King of Cling” title, he was also a renegade in an industry built around constant production. He showed collections only when he felt they were ready—a maverick approach that still resonates today—and had his creations worn by a who’s who of the modeling world, from Naomi Campbell to Gisele Bündchen. Many of his designs were featured in the pages of ELLE over the decades.

“He was someone who pioneered a new way of thinking about fashion,” Camerlengo says. “He was one of the first to question the fashion system and seasonal calendars and the need for trends. He was focused on meticulous craftsmanship and intentionality in design, a sort of precedent to what we think about today, where we are critical of overproduction and overconsumption in fashion.”

“Azzedine Alaïa: Sculpting Fashion” is on view at the Legion of Honor Museum from Nov. 21, 2026, to Apr. 11, 2027.

#6695. Will There Be Another Hunger Games Movie—and Which Character Will It Be About?

Sunrise on the Reaping details the story of Haymitch Abernathy’s Hunger Games and sets the stage for his involvement in overthrowing President Snow in the Second Rebellion, but there are so many stories within Suzanne Collins’s rich universe that are begging to be told. But is there another movie in the works, and if so, who would it be about?

“It’s interesting, because it’s always a surprise when she writes a book. She never tells us ahead of time, and Suzanne always surprises me and Nina [Jacobson], the [franchise’s] producer, with usually a pretty close-to-finished manuscript,” Francis Lawrence, Sunrise on the Reaping’s director, told ELLE in a September 2026 interview.

Here’s everything we know so far about a possible next installment in the Hunger Games movie franchise. Warning: Book and movie spoilers ahead.

There are currently no details about any future Hunger Games movies, but if Collins releases another book in the series, that has the potential to change.

It’s possible that Lawrence and Jacobson would return if another film is greenlit. Lawrence has directed every movie in the franchise since Catching Fire, and Jacobson has produced each installment.

There have been no details on a plot for a future Hunger Games movie, but there are already more than a few popular characters and topics that fans would like to see, should the franchise receive another installment:

First introduced in Catching Fire with the arrival of the Third Quarter Quell, Finnick quickly became a fan favorite thanks to his confidence and charm, which was only amplified when Sam Claflin starred as the victor in the book’s respective movie adaptation. But Finnick’s backstory is much more tragic than meets the eye. After becoming the youngest Hunger Games winner at 14, he was forced into sex trafficking and prostitution by President Snow.

Instead of perpetuating that pain, Finnick chose to protect those around him, notably his mentor, Mags, his romantic interest, Annie Cresta, and Katniss. If Finnick gets a movie of his own, it would provide details on the 65th Hunger Games, which takes place nine years before Katniss volunteers to participate in the 74th Hunger Games.

Also introduced with the Third Quarter Quell, Joanna (played by Jena Malone) became a beloved character in the franchise for entirely different reasons than Finnick. She’s outspoken, fearless, publicly opposes the Capitol and President Snow, and is never afraid to make others uncomfortable (see: the infamous Catching Fire scene in which she undresses in front of Katniss and Peeta in the elevator)—and she has every reason to be that way.

Joanna won the 71st Hunger Games by pretending to be a “sniveling, cowardly fool” and later proving that she was incredibly skilled with an axe. Like Finnick, she was also forced into sex trafficking by President Snow, who killed or tortured her loved ones if she didn’t cooperate; by the time fans meet her in the Third Quarter Quell, all of her loved ones are dead. A movie could dive into Joanna’s strategy for the 71st games, and how she became radicalized enough to participate in the Second Rebellion.

A movie about Mags could offer a unique bridge between the events that took place in Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes, President Snow’s ascent to power, and the revamping of the Hunger Games system as we know it. Mags won the 11th Hunger Games—a year after Lucy Gray did in Songbirds, though she was later erased from history—and was the first victor in the history of the games to experience monetary prizes and life in the Victor’s Village, in addition to being the first to do a Victory Tour after winning, changes that were included at Snow’s insistence.

In Sunrise on the Reaping, Mags (Lili Taylor) works as Haymitch and Maysilee’s mentor, offering him a shoulder to cry on after Louella McCoy dies. By the Third Quarter Quell, Finnick revealed that Mags (Lynn Cohen) essentially raised him, and she volunteers in Annie’s place, ultimately sacrificing herself in Catching Fire when it becomes too difficult for her to move on.

Several clues are given about Wiress’s time in the 49th Hunger Games when fans meet her in Sunrise on the Reaping. During her games, the arena was completely covered in reflective and shiny mirror-like surfaces, making it difficult for the tributes to fight their way out of the arena. She won her games by hiding out in a blind spot until it was over and did not kill a single tribute. Visually, a movie with Wiress’s arena could be a feast for the eyes.

Wiress (Maya Hawke) appears in Sunrise on the Reaping and acts as Haymitch and Maysilee’s mentor; she later returns to the franchise in Catching Fire (Amanda Plummer) and uncovers that the arena works like a clock, inspiring the rebellion to make a new plan to break the arena.

Plutarch, a Capitol citizen with decades of wealth and influence to his name, has never been selected to participate in any Hunger Games, but his presence is deeply felt within the franchise.

In Sunrise on the Reaping, he (Jesse Plemmons) works on props for District 12 and lets Haymitch know that he’s actively working on the rebellion by giving Haymitch tips on how he can advance in the games and show resistance to the Capitol. While Haymitch is unsuccessful at being the spark needed to kick-start the Second Rebellion, Heavensbee stays on and works his way up to head gamemaker by the 74th Hunger Games with Katniss; he ends up becoming the brains behind the rebellion and leads it to victory. A Plutarch prequel could give longtime fans of the franchise a look into how a character with such proximity to the Capitol could become turned against it.

While fans are always hoping that Collins expands upon the existing Hunger Games characters, some are also looking for a fresh story that details a Hunger Games with a brand-new tribute that has no existing backstory in the franchise. Similarly, the exact events of The Dark Days—also known as the First Rebellion, which drove District 13 underground and started the Hunger Games after their years-long civil war with the Capitol—were never shown on-screen, and that could be a compelling story to give context to the Second Rebellion.

By the time the garden has mostly called it a year, the Thanksgiving cactus is just getting started. Its red, lavender, or soft salmon flowers appear at the tips of flat green stems, often right around the holiday, and its stems arch over the rim of the pot.

You’ve probably seen one without knowing exactly what it was. Thanksgiving and Christmas cacti often share a shelf and a label (“holiday cactus”), and both can flower in December. The Thanksgiving cactus (Schlumbergera truncata) is a flowering cactus native to the forests of Brazil, where it grows on trees instead of in soil. The flowers, which come in shades including white, red, lavender, and salmon-orange, typically open from mid-November through late December.

Look at one of the flat stem segments. On a Thanksgiving cactus, the edges have pointed teeth; on a true Christmas cactus (Schlumbergera × buckleyi), they’re rounded, like the edge of a scalloped plate.

How do you stay open in an industry that demands toughness? Bella Hadid embarks on a new chapter with a renewed sense of optimism and her boundaries firmly in place.

I first met Bella Hadid on a fashion shoot I was styling more than a decade ago. We went on to shoot several stories together, including her first major American magazine cover. On one of those shoots, she arrived upset because she had just found out she didn’t get cast in the Victoria’s Secret show. I will never forget telling Bella that one day, Victoria’s Secret would be begging her to walk for them. Cut to today, and Bella is not only one of the most in-demand models, but she opened their most recent show. When I saw her hit the runway in a beaded floral bra and matching wings, I let out a scream because I knew what that moment meant to her.

A few days after the Victoria’s Secret show in October, Bella and I sat down to reflect on her career so far, forgiveness and success, turning 30, and where she goes from here. I have always felt connected to Bella because she, like me, is a heart-on-her-sleeve kind of gal. She is deeply vulnerable and human, and it’s what makes her one of the most compelling figures in fashion today.

Samira Nasr: This is a full-circle moment. On one of our first shoots together, you were really upset because you didn’t get the Victoria’s Secret show. And I said to you, “One day, they will be begging you to be in their show.” And I could tell you didn’t believe me. Your whole body shut down. And three days ago, you opened Victoria’s Secret. Holy shit.

Bella Hadid: I still don’t believe you, to be honest. I feel like people are just AI-ing me onto the runway. I think that it’s so crazy how time passes and you still have some kind of impostor syndrome. No matter how hard you work, there’s always another girl who is more deserving, more beautiful, and has worked so hard to be able to get that spot. Ten years ago, I remember that moment too, where I never thought I would get the show, but I think that God always has a plan, and I truly believe at that time I wasn’t ready. I don’t think I’ve been ready until this year, to be honest. In past years, I was going to my horse-show finals in the morning, flying straight to the Victoria’s Secret show that day, doing rehearsal right before, and then pulling what we call a Bella, and Belinda comes out, and we just act. But this year, I felt really strong.

I have had to make a decision for myself to have boundaries with my own health. And I think it’s gotten me to this point now where I’m able to do the jobs that I do want to do with people that I love, that I can feel inspired by. And that’s truly what [working in] fashion is about: being around people you feel inspired by. Until you find that inspiration, it’s hard to go back into our industry. I think I needed a little bit of time just to be able to be like, “Let me just remember why I’m doing this again,” because I know I’m more than just a face.

SN: We don’t really support a culture of care. I have always believed that boundaries are bridges.

BH: They really are. We know so many people who have been working for 30, 40, 50 years in this industry with zero boundaries. I’ve watched people over the years just get more depressed because they don’t know themselves and they don’t know who they are. And that’s how I was for 10 years, when I didn’t know what my favorite color was, what I liked to eat. I stopped listening to music. To not be able to know yourself in an industry that tells you how to be every single day is really hard.

SN: Going back to Victoria’s Secret, did you feel empowered, or was that your alter ego? What is her name again?

BH: Belinda. You know what? Fortunately and unfortunately, I think it was Bella this year.

BH: I made up my alter ego, Belinda, a long time ago to deal with the intensity of everything happening in my life. So she could deal with that, and I can deal with this, and we can do this together, which I’m realizing I should probably go to therapy about again. Belinda is the one that people are like, “Wow, this, that.”

I have POTS [postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome], and I have a lot of PMDD [premenstrual dysphoric disorder], endometriosis, plus my Lyme and things that affect my body very deeply. Right before I walked out on the runway for my opening look, I went to go stretch and my whole clavicle completely … I don’t even know what it was. My lungs almost collapsed, and I couldn’t breathe, and then it was like, “Five, four …” So my body goes through these different motions of feeling really good and then all of a sudden crashing on me at the worst moments. I need to realize maybe my body can’t handle all that I have going on. Because I know how to walk. One thing I can do is I can strut. But it’s a lot of pressure; we all have our own insecurities. Sometimes my body just can’t connect with my brain. My brain wants to do it, and my body is like, “No, we can’t do that today.” And I’m like, “Okay, great. So let’s figure out another way.” But I think people don’t realize how much I struggle with the fatigue and the body pain, and so I just try my best.

SN: You’ve achieved so much in your 20s. You just turned 30. What are you looking forward to in your 30s?

BH: It’s funny, today I woke up with—I wouldn’t say the word is anxiety, but this feeling of “Oh my gosh, my life is starting.” I want to get rid of what doesn’t bring me joy and bring in what does. I’ve tried to put myself in positions for work for a very long time that have made me feel out of body or like I don’t belong. Years ago, I was just grateful to get a job, and I would say yes to everything. I lacked boundaries then, but I do believe that that gave me the strength to be able to have the confidence in my own boundaries now. I’ve achieved pretty much all the goals that I wanted to when it comes to fashion as a model. And now I do want to go into other ventures to eventually get to peace and happiness and love within this universe that God has put me in.

In the next couple of years, it’s just about getting back to community, getting back to using my voice again. I got beaten down pretty heavy for a long time, which pushed me into this very big struggle with my physical and emotional health, which makes my brain turn off and makes me not be able to write or read. When you’re not around enough people that are like-minded, you feel really ostracized. That’s almost where I got to. Of course, I always want to speak from my heart, which I hope will never disrespect anybody, but still be able to have the confidence to speak my truth and hope that people will walk beside me along the way when it comes to that.

SN: Is there something you’re most proud of achieving?

BH: Well, first and foremost, starting [my fragrance line] Orebella was something that I truly needed for my own creativity and to be able to build community. I am not a 50/50 person. I’m an all-in type of girl. Being able to move forward into my 30s, really focusing on building the things that I’ve started—Orebella was something that I pushed so hard for since 2018, and the fact that it’s really now coming to fruition makes me so proud.

When I was 17, going to my first go-see with a stylist I will not name, I was basically told I was too big to wear Saint Laurent and needed to lose a couple of pounds. And that’s when I was just a baby coming out of high school. Twelve years later, now I’m the face of Saint Laurent. I’m the face of Prada, which is incredible and insane. Times have changed so much, and the people that have come into the industry to make it a better place for us as models are so important. Not that that comment ever impacted me. I went through enough struggles with my body before that, so by the time she said that, I was like, “Okay, girl, I’m actually coming out of something that I dealt with very heavily when it came to my body. And now I look at food in a different way, and I love my body, and that’s okay if you think that I don’t look the way that you think I should look.”

I just would love for everyone to think about if every day they had to walk outside and be photographed, what that would feel like, because it’s an uncomfortable feeling for anybody. My face would change so much. I would be super puffy one day, and one day I would look yellow and emaciated, and people would think I was on drugs or doing this or that. And when anybody knows about, one, hormones, just as a woman in general, and two, about immune problems and how weight fluctuates—again, I just think that there needs to be more sensitivity in our industry.

SN: I’m curious if you’ve always been this person—so sensitive, empathetic—from a little girl.

BH: I’ve always felt like my sensitivity and my openness were a disservice to myself because I felt like no one else was like me in those ways, so I shut it down. Now I realize that there is a generosity of thoughtfulness that is so much deeper than just buying somebody an expensive gift. I think sitting on the side of the street with somebody, even if you don’t have any money, is more important than just walking by without caring.

SN: You’re able to access compassion when a lot of people can’t, and that is a superpower. I want to talk about style because you mentioned your archive. I remember you once mentioned you collect Gaultier. Do you still collect?

BH: I do collect. Behind me are bags to sell. But downstairs, I have my Gaultier dress that the atelier made. It was a Naomi Campbell dress that she had worn in one of their shows, and I was obsessed with it. I called their PR, and within a week and a half, because that dress was in a museum, I had it made and sent to Cannes, and that was that white-and-black dress that I wore on the carpet. And so, that’s here. My Coperni dress is here. I have a whole section for Virgil [Abloh].

SN: The last time I saw Virgil was with you, actually.

BH: I don’t think I’ve done a real, full Fashion Month since he passed away. And Vivienne [Westwood] and Karl [Lagerfeld] passed, and I felt like all of my idols were leaving, and it felt really scary. I have all the art Virgil gave me, his letter he wrote me, his bags, his shoes he made me. And then I have my Prada area, my Cavalli area, my Bob Mackies that I’ve been collecting over the years.

BH: Hopefully for a future wedding. There are just some dresses you can’t find again. And Bob did it best.

SN: He’s an American icon. I wanted to talk to you about Cannes. You’re such a queen of Cannes now, and I look forward to seeing you there. Is it fun?

BH: This past year was fun. I felt grounded. To do the red carpet and feel, in those moments, sober and happy and healthy and strong, and go to sleep not having anxiety about going out or how I’m going to look the next day, and just being with loved ones. It did really well for me this year.

Cannes has always held a very special place in my heart. It begins long before I arrive. Working closely with designers on custom gowns, exchanging references, and watching those first sketches come to life is such a beautiful part of the experience.

This year, being there to support Marie-Clémentine Dusabejambo’s Ben’Imana, the first Rwandan film in the official selection, was especially meaningful. It explores memory and reconciliation after the genocide against the Tutsi through women whose lives carry its legacy. That’s what I love about cinema: It brings us closer and gives stories too rarely heard the attention they deserve. You can enter a theater knowing nothing of someone’s world and leave carrying a little of it with you.

SN: I look at you and how hard you’ve worked for so long, from our first job together. To me, it feels like you’ve achieved so much. But what does success look like for you? Do you feel you’ve achieved it?

BH: I talked to my business manager yesterday, so that made me feel a little bit more successful. But when you don’t think about the money part of it, I think success truly comes from peace within. At the end of the day, I really don’t need much. That’s why I do give back so much, because a lot of the money that I do make, I don’t feel deserving of. There are so many people in the world, whether it’s doctors or teachers, that I think deserve my pay rate a lot more than I do.

From an external space, success is about how known you are, how much you make, how many times you can be on TV, how many covers you’re on, how many fashion shows you do. And that is really important in our industry, especially when you’re building. But for me, I am different. Success, for me, can be living in peace, in a small town, with a man that I love, with my child, having my animals. I’ve always wanted to start a farm-to-table bakery, with flowers, and everything comes from the community. I want to do things that are fulfilling for me in my soul: having a greenhouse that I can garden in, having my glassblowing and ceramic studio, and having lots of children and being a mother. It wasn’t a dream that I’ve always had, but it’s something that now is so intense for me. My intuition is screaming so loudly at me about it.

I think knowing there is a reason behind everything is so important. My sister and I donated a million dollars to Palestine last year [the donation was split between HEAL Palestine, Palestine Children’s Relief Fund, World Central Kitchen, and United Nations Relief and Works Agency], which we could have never been able to do without the success that we’ve had.

God gave me the platform to be able to speak about the things that I hold dearly to me. And when it comes to Palestine or Sudan or Congo or Rwanda or places that mean so much to not only myself but the people around me—to give people a voice in any capacity, that’s success for me as well. To make my family wide and far proud, or to see themselves in me, it gives me strength.

#6665. We Come to Paul Thomas Anderson’s Cameron Winter Live at Carnegie Hall to Feel

The film, shot in 35mm, is currently “on tour” at movie theaters around the country

The Cambridge Dictionary recently announced that its selection for Word of the Year is human. Human as an adjective that denotes that something was not made by computers or A.I. (Last year’s word was parasocial.) The editor at Cambridge told the Times that they found phrases like “human governance,” “human written,” and “human need” come up often in their research.

I think about this while I’m at the movie theater watching Paul Thomas Anderson’s latest film, Cameron Winter at Carnegie Hall, which documents the 23-year-old’s performance at the legendary venue. It is the film’s release date, and all of the showings are sold out, but since I’ve come alone, I am able to find the perfect seat. Winter’s face is large on the screen, and it’s almost expressionless except for his eyebrows, barely peeking out from underneath what can truly only be called a mop of hair, which curves this way and that way as the only proof of the emotional energy he is exerting. Otherwise, he seems to simply open his mouth and the words come out, devastating and romantic, because what else would they do? What else would he do but this? For an hour and a half, we all sit here enthralled by a single man and a piano. Then I think about how my most recent moviegoing experiences required staring at people talking, or thinking, or simply staring at a very close range. (I’d just seen You Can See Everything a few weeks before.) We come to the movies to see humans was my brain-rot thought, in the style of Nicole Kidman’s famous AMC commercial. (Brain rot was Oxford’s 2024 Word of the Year.)

I think back to when it all began. The day after Valentine’s Day last year, my friend Tran casually texted me, asking, “You get into this guy?” with a Spotify link to an album called Heavy Metal by someone called Cameron Winter. I couldn’t tell what it would sound like just by looking at the cover, which was a close-up of his face, criss-crossed by silver chains, but what I soon found were songs dominated by a deep baritone voice and a sweet, jangly guitar or a kind of hazily sensual piano. Who was this guy? Well, it turned out he was the lead singer of this band called Geese—not to be confused with Goose—which he’d started when he was a teen with his high school friends. He’d made this album (jokingly rumored to have been recorded in Guitar Centers, from which he was now banned) and released it without any fanfare that previous December, against the advice of people who knew him and thought the record was too strange or would somehow interfere with the planned Geese release in the new year. (That album, Getting Killed, was released in September 2025 and turned the band from little-known Brooklyn musicians into both “saviors of rock & roll” and “music label psy-ops,” topping off numerous best-of lists and garnering multiple Grammy nominations.) But none of that had happened yet.

As I sat in the movie theater, I wondered how many of us in the audience had also been at the performance. What were we looking for in a recording of something we had experienced firsthand? That night, Winter emerged on stage and sat at the piano, his back turned to us. We watched his head sink beneath his shoulders as if he was searching for something deeper inside the instrument, or maybe he was becoming one with it. We had no choice but to completely give ourselves to the music. But Anderson’s camera was nimble and dug itself in other places: a peephole on the stage that looked out to Winter in the foreground and the ocean of people behind him, indeed turning the whole affair into a kind of landscape, swooping in from the audience to Winter at the piano like a detective looking for the culprit of an unnamed crime, or training his lens on the oversize shadow cast by Winter over the audience sitting stage left. I think, “Oh, this is like a musical Rashomon.”

During “Drinking Age,” as he sings, “Today, I met who I’m gonna be / from now on / and he’s a piece of shit,” the screen goes dark blue, with only his faint silhouette visible, the weight of his words carrying a brief silence so that you sit there in the emotion. Other times, we experience through Anderson’s POV, seeing the ushers open the backstage doors so that he can step onstage, deftly twirling the camera around Winter’s body. This time he is singing “$0.” It’s perhaps the best song on the album (or at least according to this writer), where he sings with wild abandon, “God is real / God is real / I’m not kidding, God is actually real I’m not kidding this time / I think God is actually for real …” Anderson trains his camera on one of the dramatic light fixtures at the concert hall, a round disc that resembles the sun, then he pans to the audience, then to Winter, small on stage, confessing his small miracle. Afterward, as if exhausted, he delicately plays a simple riff on the piano, while there’s a close-up of his face on screen with heavenly rays of light reflected on his face. I believed him when I first heard it on the album, and I believed him twice more as I heard him sing it live.

Winter stands up; the show is over. The lights come up, and the crowd rises to its feet. I can’t remember now if there was an encore, though I do remember we stood there applauding for a very long time, emotionally drained but joyful that we’d been witnesses. On the screen, the credits roll over images of the crowd, and the two teen girls sitting to my right have spotted themselves in the audience, elated. Winter comes back out for two more songs.

The movie ends. “Cameron Winter. Live at Carnegie Hall. Dead at 23,” flashes on the screen. Inside the movie theater, the people applaud. I’m still thinking of Winter singing of “com[ing] down to see / my private ceremonies / All on the floor of this room,” and I think, yes, we’re all here in ceremony—or are we in communion?—with one another. The human need for human connection, for human revelations.

Welcome to In Good Company, where we ring in the festive season with fashion-insider entertaining tips, expert etiquette advice, and the perfect editor-approved hostess gifts.

Mondadori’s home, dubbed Casa Cabana, is a true expression of that sensibility: a richly layered apartment designed by Renzo Mongiardino, the master of theatrical interiors and modern baroque, who was a close friend of her mother, its original owner.

Mondadori carried that more-is-more, layered sensibility into both Cabana, her lifestyle magazine turned home brand, and the way she entertains. Her Milan Fashion Week luncheons, hosted at her family home, have become a social fixture for a stalwart collective of designers, artists, and friends of the brand—from ELLE editor-in-chief Nina Garcia and La DoubleJ designer J.J. Martin to architects Roberto Peregalli and Laura Sartori Rimini—who gather over fresh pasta, delicious antipasto, and mouthwatering desserts beneath a canopy of color and pattern.

“Ambiance is not only décor; it’s how you entertain, how you bring your friends and family together, how you gather them around the table—the food, the scent, the music,” she says, noting that it can take more than two years for a house to truly feel like home. “Mongiardino was so good at [creating ambiance] because he knew how to make coziness happen.”

For Mondadori, a beautiful table or a beautifully decorated space is only the beginning. She notes that any worthwhile gathering requires a “surgical” guest list layered with people from a “mishmash of different worlds,” plus tactile, personal touches like handmade place cards (often designed by her daughter, Cosima) and a guest book to capture the evening—her favorite hostess touch. Years later, its pages become a record of the people who have gathered around her table.

You have to be surgical about it. Whatever the size of the party, you need at least one or two great conversation starters—people who can bring others together and spark interesting connections. I also love mixing crowds from different industries, which a city like Milan, New York, or London allows. When I was growing up in Milan in the ’80s, the fashion crowd and old industrial Milan were separate. Now there’s a much bigger creative industry, along with interesting visitors from abroad. I love bringing those people together.

We usually spend Christmas Eve and Christmas Day in the mountains with our extended family. We play board games, sit around the table, and exchange gifts—it’s all about creating a happy atmosphere. I ask my daughter to craft the place cards. When we moved here, she was 4, and so the first ones were all kind of scrabbled and very sweet. And now she’s very artistic, so they’re getting more creative and neat.

A guest book. Every hostess who loves entertaining loves to have a beautiful guest book where everyone can leave a little note.

#6657. Chicken Francese

Chicken Francese is a staple at your favorite Italian restaurants. But sometimes, you don't want to empty your wallet for this classic dinner favorite. That's where I come in! Francese is a preparation (much like Milanese) that can be used with all types of meat and seafood, from shrimp to veal. For chicken, it involves dredging cutlets in flour and beaten egg, creating a pseudo-batter. The cutlets are then pan-fried and made crispy, albeit temporarily. See, chicken Francese is one of those dishes that epitomizes "crispy gone soggy"—a texture sensation that is one of my absolute favorites. In this case, the chicken becomes "soggy" because you finish them by simmering it in a rich, lemony sauce that will knock your socks off.

It might seem strange, but it’s such an iconic flavor/texture combination. Also, it is very easy to make and comes together in under an hour. What could be better?!

Place 1 chicken breast on a cutting board. Using a sharp chef’s knife held parallel to the cutting board, slice into the thickest side of the breast, cutting three-quarters of the way through. Fold the top piece back (as if you were opening a book) and continue to cut until the breast is divided into 2 thinner cutlets. Repeat with the remaining chicken breast for a total of 4 cutlets. Working one piece at a time, place each cutlet between 2 sheets of plastic wrap. Using a meat mallet or the bottom of a small heavy skillet, pound the chicken to about 1/4" thick. Season all over with salt and pepper.

In a shallow dish, beat eggs, garlic powder, and a pinch of salt. Pour 1/2 cup flour into another shallow dish.

In a large, heavy skillet over medium heat, heat oil. Working in batches, coat cutlets in flour, shaking off excess, then dip into the egg mixture, turning to coat.

Place cutlets in the oil, being careful not to overcrowd the skillet, and cook, turning halfway through, until golden brown, 5 to 7 minutes.

Transfer cutlets to a paper towel-lined plate to drain and season with salt and pepper.

Wipe out the skillet, then heat over medium heat. Add five Tbsp. butter and the remaining one Tbsp. flour, stirring until just beginning to foam and the butter is melted, 3 to 5 minutes. Whisk in wine. Increase heat to medium-high and bring to a boil. Cook, stirring, until slightly reduced, 1 to 2 minutes. Add stock, lemon zest, and lemon juice and return to a boil. Reduce heat to medium and cook, whisking constantly, until the sauce is reduced by about one-third, 3 to 5 minutes. Reduce heat to medium-low. Add lemon slices and remaining 1 Tbsp. butter and cook, stirring, until the sauce is glossy, 2 to 3 minutes more.

Return chicken to the sauce and cook, turning halfway through, until heated through, 2 to 3 minutes.

Transfer chicken to a platter and pour the sauce over. Top with lemon slices and parsley.

#6653. Can 4 Minutes of Exercise a Day Really Improve Your Fitness?

Brief strength training can boost balance and leg strength.

Committing to any level of exercise isn’t easy, even whether you’re a gym regular. But a recent Penn State College of Medicine study may be music to the ears of anyone looking to get active with minimal effort—especially older adults. The findings suggest that four minutes a day of strength training helped improve leg strength and balance in adults 65 and older. The benefits are significant for study participants, but whether the results translate to younger folks is up for debate. Dr. Harry Pino, director of human performance and exercise physiology at Yunara, unpacks if four minutes of exercise can help make gains or if it’s all too good to be true.

Time may not matter as much as you think. But before diving into the details of your workout, Pino suggests figuring out your goals and assessing your current fitness level. If you’re unsure which exercises work best for your end results, Pino offers this breakdown:

“The best four-minute workout is one that challenges your body appropriately. Intensity matters, but consistency and progressive improvement are what drive long-term results,” Pino tells ELLE.

It may seem insignificant and the warm-up may take longer than the exercises themselves, but Pino says four minutes can make a measurable difference, especially for someone who’s been sedentary. “Research shows that brief daily strength workouts can improve balance, leg strength, and mobility in older adults. These improvements directly support physical function and independence as we age.”

The good news is yes, absolutely. According to Pino, exercise benefits are cumulative, and even brief sessions can stimulate positive adaptations when performed consistently. “Four minutes won’t replace a comprehensive fitness routine, but it’s an excellent starting point. Small, sustainable habits often produce the greatest long-term impact,” he says.

Focus on exercises that match your goals and fitness level. Pino’s recommendation is to start with a simple routine that might include 30 seconds each of squats, push-ups, step-ups, and resistance band rows, alternating with 30 seconds of recovery. “Prioritize proper form and gradually increase the challenge as your fitness improves,” he specifies.

Even a short workout benefits from a warm-up, particularly at higher intensities. Start with gentle movement to prepare your muscles and joints. Afterward, prioritize hydration, adequate nutrition, and quality sleep. Pino notes that exercise provides the stimulus, but recovery allows the body to adapt.

There is no one-size-fits-all workout routine, no matter your body type or how active you are. “Fitness level, strength, and mobility are much more important than body shape when designing a workout. Beginners may benefit from modified movements, while more experienced individuals can increase resistance or intensity. The goal is to challenge the body safely and progressively," says Pino.

The Penn State study only studied an older set of adults, primarily to understand how to prevent falls and balance concerns that can come with age. While Pino explained that these findings can have a positive impact for anyone looking to get active, he agrees that short workouts can be especially valuable for older adults. “They’re accessible and easier to maintain consistently. Even a few minutes of daily strength training can improve balance, mobility, and functional strength. These improvements are critical for preserving independence and quality of life as we age,” Pino says.
