IDC highlighted the findings in an October 20 analysis examining what enterprise customers expect from AI technology vendors.

#6914. Report: Global Ransomware Attacks Rise 23% in Q3

Global ransomware activity increased 23% in the third quarter of 2026, with organizations in North America and Europe accounting for the largest share of attacks, according to cybersecurity firm NCC Group.

The company's Cyber Threat Intelligence Report Review of Q3 2026 recorded 2,865 ransomware victim claims, up from 2,325 in the previous quarter.

Despite the quarterly increase, attacks declined 26% month over month in September to 821. North America recorded the most attacks that month with 390, followed by Europe with 211.

The industrial sector accounted for 29% of quarterly attacks, while Qilin remained the most active ransomware group for the sixth consecutive quarter, recording 365 victims. The Gentlemen followed with 356, and INC Ransom ranked third with 109.

The report highlighted a July cyber attack against a small UK power generation facility that forced operations to stop for approximately four days.

Although the incident did not affect the national electricity grid, it demonstrated how cyber attacks can disrupt physical infrastructure.

The security researchers said the incident was consistent with techniques previously associated with Iranian-affiliated campaigns, including attacks against internet-exposed industrial controllers.

However, the UK government has not formally attributed the attack, and the techniques used remain unconfirmed.

The report also identified growing security challenges as organizations connect traditional IT networks with operational technology (OT) systems for remote maintenance and centralized management.

The firm recommended network segmentation, stronger privileged-access controls, and continuous monitoring of OT environments.

The research found that more than 40 vulnerabilities affecting enterprise applications, VPN appliances, e-mail gateways, and management platforms were added to CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities Catalog during the quarter.

The report examined Gunra, a ransomware-as-a-service operation that has targeted 53 victims across multiple industries as of Q3.

According to the cybersecurity company, Gunra exploited vulnerabilities CVE-2024-55591 and CVE-2025-24472 in internet-facing Fortinet devices to gain access to corporate networks.

The group also used stolen credentials, session hijacking, and administrative tools to move between systems before deploying ransomware.

Other emerging ransomware groups included Deadlock and Storm, with the latter claiming 78 victims by the end of September.

Researchers also observed initial access brokers advertising compromised VPN, Remote Desktop Protocol, and SSH access, alongside access to cloud identities, source code repositories, and security management platforms.

The quarterly review also highlighted BraZetsu, an AI-enhanced Windows malware framework that evolved into a specialized initial access toolkit.

According to the report, BraZetsu uses AI models to automate victim profiling, analyze compromised environments, and identify valuable corporate systems.

The framework searches for enterprise software, financial data, digital certificates, and cloud credentials, helping operators identify compromised access that can be sold to other cyber criminals.

The cybersecurity firm also identified risks involving legitimate AI agents, including prompt injection, excessive permissions, and unintended access to external systems.

The report cited incidents involving U.S. and Australian government systems as examples of agents accessing resources beyond their intended scope.

Researchers warned that these incidents do not necessarily involve malicious intent but can create consequences resembling conventional cyber intrusions.

#6907. Microsoft Expands Protections and Controls for Sovereign Cloud

Microsoft is introducing new tools within its sovereign cloud offerings designed to give organizations tighter control over where their data resides, who can access it, and how critical systems operate, especially in light of AI becoming a bigger part of enterprise infrastructure.

In a recent announcement, Douglas Phillips, president and chief technology officer of Microsoft Specialized Clouds, outlined updates spanning public cloud, private cloud, and partner environments. The changes target governments, regulated industries, and organizations that need tighter control over sensitive workloads without abandoning cloud-based AI capabilities.

"Digital sovereignty should give organizations greater control and security over the technology they depend on, while continuing to innovate," Phillips wrote.

One of the biggest additions is Microsoft's Sovereign Control Panel, which will enter public preview at Microsoft Ignite. Available through the Azure portal, the tool will give IT administrators one place to manage data sovereignty settings, spot compliance issues, and identify potential risks that could disrupt operations.

Microsoft is also tightening controls over who can access sensitive systems. Its Data Guardian feature will require oversight by personnel based in Europe when Microsoft engineers access certain production systems in the European Union and European Free Trade Association. Another tool, Production Touch Ledger, will keep tamper-resistant records of that access, giving organizations a clearer picture of who accessed their systems and what they did.

Microsoft is also giving customers more control over encryption. External key management with Azure Managed HSM is now generally available, letting organizations keep their main encryption keys on hardware they own or manage outside Microsoft's cloud.

The company is also expanding its Sovereign Private Cloud offerings, giving organizations more options to run systems with or without an internet connection. New capabilities include GitHub Enterprise Local, GitHub Copilot with locally hosted AI models, and additional data services running on Azure Local.

The push for greater operational control comes as Microsoft is also reconsidering how enterprises should secure increasingly autonomous AI systems.

As reported by Redmond magazine, Microsoft is expanding Project Perception to address risks posed by what the company calls "agentic insiders," AI agents capable of accessing sensitive information and performing actions without constant human supervision.

That initiative introduces plans for Guardian Agents, specialized security agents designed to monitor other AI systems, evaluate their behavior and intervene when their actions become risky.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," said Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security.

Project Perception is about limiting what AI agents can do. Microsoft's sovereign cloud expansion tackles a different set of questions: where those systems run, who gets to administer them, and how the data stays protected.

Taken together, the two efforts point to a shift in Microsoft's enterprise AI strategy. The company is putting more weight on oversight, not just automation.

Phillips said AI is making these questions more pressing as organizations roll out intelligent systems across sensitive business processes and critical infrastructure.

#6903. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. Cisco says customers can extend the same access model to other infrastructure, including servers, Kubernetes clusters, and cloud resources.

#6897. Survey Finds 84% of Organizations Reported at Least One AI-Related Incident in the Past Year

Companies are running into problems as they give artificial intelligence more control over critical business applications, with 84% reporting at least one AI-related incident in the past year, according to a new survey from identity security firm Delinea.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Those gaps can make it difficult to identify segregation-of-duties conflicts, in which a person or agent has permissions across systems that should be kept separate.

Despite those issues, 95% of respondents said they were confident their access controls would withstand scrutiny in an external audit.

However, 26% reported access-related audit findings that had recurred in consecutive years, while 25% said some findings had remained unresolved for three or more years.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

Meanwhile, 60% reported an increase over the past two years in the number of critical applications operating outside the scope of access governance or Sarbanes-Oxley Act compliance.

The findings highlight the oversight challenges associated with giving AI agents authority to act within business systems, particularly when organizations cannot consistently identify who owns an agent or trace its actions.

Frank Vukovits, chief security scientist at Delinea, said organizations remain responsible for the actions AI agents take within their systems.

"When an agent can approve a transaction or change permissions, the business owns the outcome. You need to know what every agent can do, who owns it, and who approved it, before an auditor asks or your financials are misstated," Vukovits said.

#6890. Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

The findings echo a recent report about management software and AI e-mail assistants. Research from Fortra and Barracuda showed how criminals are using legitimate administrative tools and hidden AI instructions to manipulate users and compromise systems.

#6883. Study: Sign-In Problems Are Primary Drivers of Help Desk Tickets

Sign-in problems account for up to 53% of help desk tickets at individual enterprises, but network issues can take significantly longer to resolve, according to a new study from ControlUp.

Analyzing 1,069,110 help desk tickets, the IT operations company identified password resets, account lockouts, and multifactor authentication (MFA) issues as major drivers of support demand.

At one large enterprise, account-related tickets took a median of 11 minutes to resolve, compared with 50 hours for network problems. Meanwhile, 87% of network and phone tickets were reassigned between teams, compared with 20% of account tickets.

MFA enrollment and re-enrollment problems appeared at 40% of organizations, with one in five linking these issues to employees replacing or upgrading their phones. Virtual desktop problems appeared at 55% of organizations analyzed.

Traditional phone support remained a major channel for IT assistance.

Machine-generated tickets from monitoring, security, backup, and patching systems appeared at half of the organizations studied. At 15%, automated tickets represented between 25% and 50% of all requests.

The research covered enterprises across retail, healthcare, financial services, manufacturing, construction, energy, and IT services.

ControlUp said the findings highlight the need to evaluate recurring problems and their operational impact rather than relying solely on ticket volume and resolution speed.

"IT organizations have traditionally measured support performance by how quickly they close tickets, but that doesn't tell the whole story," said Simon Townsend, senior vice president of marketing and office of the CTO at ControlUp.

#6878. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

#6870. 4 Weekend Decluttering Habits That Make Small Spaces Feel Bigger

A few smart systems can keep your counters, closets, and junk drawers in check.

The easiest clutter to manage is clutter that never has a chance to form. And in small spaces like galley kitchens and studio apartments, mess piles up naturally on any flat surface. Keeping it under control is less about “magic bullet” tips and more about systems you can actually keep up over time. So when we asked the professional organizers about how to declutter small spaces, the answer was in mindset and habits. If you can limit what comes into your space in the first place, you can better control its organization.

“I could say, ‘Grab a garbage bag and pick up all of the detritus left in the wake of a busy life and then put everything else where it belongs.’ That’s obvious,” Gayle M. Gruenberg of Let’s Get Organized says. “The hard part is actually DOING it. It requires motivation, presence of mind, initiative, and the necessary tools.”

Below, find the pro-approved habits that make a difference when you have more stuff than your space can hold. You can start these routines in just one weekend, and it’s far more attainable than tackling the massive pile in the garage.

Everything needs a home in your house. That’s the power of a well-placed “drop zone.” Even in small spaces, a catchall tray, bowl, or mini basket belongs at entryways or places of transition. A hook for your keys and bags when you come through the door, a bin for loose throw blankets, or a basket for remote controls on the mantel.

Throughout the day, it can take quite a bit of mental load to always put everything back when you use it. That’s where Cathy Orr, cofounder of The Uncluttered Life, recommends having a “collection basket.” It’s just one catchall container, where you can drop miscellaneous misplaced items that you pick up throughout the day. At the end of the night, take about 10 minutes to go through the collection basket and return everything to where it belongs. It’s just one small habit, rather than something to keep up with all day.

Impulse shopping lends itself to clutter very easily. The “One In, One Out” rule can curb this. It works like this: for every new thing you want to bring into your home, one thing has to go. Greenhalgh suggests checking your closet, fridge, and pantry before you shop—going through your back stock will save you both space and money.

“In small spaces, items accumulate very quickly,” Macie Kreutzer, founder of For the Love of Simple, says. “Before shopping for new items (home goods, clothing) clear out at least one item from this category. Don't bring in more than your space can physically contain.”

Once you have everything you’re ready to get rid of, make the donation process as seamless as possible. Fill your donation bags and boxes in the hallway or in the car. This encourages you to actually finish getting rid of the clutter, rather than letting them collect dust in the garage.

“Nothing gets to wander back inside just because it once lived there,” Carroll Cartwright, professional organizer and owner of Neatly & Co., says. “And this is why I love moving everything out of the space and into the hall or a separate area first. The things you decide not to keep are already halfway out the door. Keep them moving.”

It is so tempting to take advantage of the freebies in life. But this is how linen closets and kitchen drawers get quickly overrun. Unless there’s a specific and immediate need for them, you’re better off getting rid of them.

“They are usually cheap and break easily anyway,” Diane Greenhalgh, owner of Tiny to the Max, says. “That includes airplane and hotel freebies, special event or conference swag, and extra takeout plastic utensils and condiments. Your junk drawer will thank you.”

If you already have a drawer overflowing with freebies, it’s time to start culling and evaluating. You might part with two or 10 things, or you might choose to keep it all. “The point is that you’ve looked at every single item and made a decision about it, instead of continuing to store things simply because they were already there,” Cartwright says. “More likely than not, you’ll find something that makes you say, ‘Why on earth do I still have this?’”

The advice your grandma gave you was spot-on: It’s easier if you wash the dishes as you cook. That same principle applies to keeping your small space tidy. Mess feels larger with less square footage to work with, so once you’re finished with something, put it away.

“Put away your makeup after you've applied it in the morning, stack dishes in the dishwasher and wipe down surfaces after each meal, and tuck away work or homework into a drawer once you're done for the evening,” Greenhalgh says.

For the days you can’t clean as you go, Cartwright suggests spending 10 to 15 minutes a day “closing your circles.” It’s what helps your home stay clean over time, instead of just for a few weeks. This period is when you put the scissors back in the drawer or hang up your jacket.

“Most clutter is just a collection of unfinished tasks,” Cartwright says. “This small commitment allows you to wake up to order instead of yesterday’s chaos, and that is a much better way to start the day!”

#6867. Irish Designers Share Their 7 Coziest Style Secrets That We Should All Be Stealing

Here’s how to embrace that “Green Wave” with your interiors.

Over the past few years, Americans have become notably charmed by Irish culture—even the L.A. Times coined it a “Green Wave.” From splitting the “G” when drinking a pint of Guinness and bragging about it on social media to spotlighting Irish celebrities like author Sally Rooney, musician Hozier, and actress Saoirse Ronan, we as a society—myself very much included—have become a bit obsessed with the Emerald Isle, and I think it’s high time we let this Green Wave roll into the interior design scene.

If you’ve ever had the privilege to visit a home in the Emerald Isle, you’ll know that Irish spaces are warm, welcoming, and lived-in, with many of the design elements specifically used to prioritize comfort and community, something American homeowners have begun adopting. “Irish homes have traditionally needed to be comfortable, functional, and adaptable to a climate that can be cold, damp, and unpredictable,” Galway-based designer Niamh McKenna tells House Beautiful. “Traditionally, our homes are also much smaller, so beautiful rooms must also work for everyday life.”

However, it’s not just the environment which influences their interiors. Keep reading to find out the quintessentially Irish design details that local designers love to celebrate and believe American homes should borrow.

During the Georgian era, which spanned from the early 1700s to the early 1800s, Ireland experienced a population boom. During this time, Dublin also underwent a sizable redevelopment when it comes to architecture and urban infrastructure, according to History Ireland. It’s these elegant details that designer Julianne Kelly loves so much and thinks others should adopt.

“There is so much history in these houses and the proportions are beautiful: high ceilings, ornate cornicing, original sash windows, fireplaces, and rich paneled doors,” Kelly says. “These details give a room a sense of history and permanence.”

The Merrion Square area in Dublin, she adds, is a great place to walk around to view some of these rich elements in person, specifically the iconic painted Georgian doors.

Once you leave the streets of Georgian Dublin, you’ll find the sprawling countryside with “a beautiful, varied landscape that is impossible to ignore when designing the interiors for a house,” Kelly explains. She uses many natural elements found just outside the window in her designs, like greenery, stone, timber, and natural fibers like wool and linen that feel authentic to the country and only improve with age. Colors inspired by the scenes outside help the interior feel connected to the earth as well.

The textiles specifically add much-needed warmth and coziness to a room. “I love these as they add personality, a sense of calm, and an old worldliness that doesn’t look over manufactured or brand-spanking new!”

“The fireplace has traditionally been at the heart of the Irish home, and I think that idea is still incredibly relevant,” Kelly says. Ornate or simple, stone or tiled, wood-burning or gas—the details of the fireplace don’t really matter, as long as it serves as a natural focal point and the furniture is arranged around it.

McKenna adds that centering the hearth rather than the TV promotes conversation and connection. “It’s a reminder that a room can be designed around how people gather and interact,” she explains. The energy and warmth of a fireplace, even as modern homes move away from open fires, brings comfort and beauty to any room, and that’s something worth preserving.

Step into an Irish person’s home, and you’ll likely find a lot of old mixed in with the new. “Irish people love to hold onto their items,” Kelly says. “I’m not sure if it’s due to our famine history, but on most projects I work on, people have sentimental items that I have to work with.”

The designer will always try to work with these existing pieces to come up with some sort of eclectic mix of modern and vintage. She loves how unique and personal these homes turn out to be; they tell a great story. “And the Irish love a good story,” she adds.

In Ireland’s rainy climate, a home’s interiors—especially practical, hardworking spaces—need to feel both beautiful and comfortable.

“Bathrooms and kitchens are among the hardest-working parts of our homes, and we see it important to give the most functional part of our homes the same thought and attention as our living spaces,” McKenna explains. “We love to connect the indoors to the outdoors in these spaces, by using natural materials, earthy colors, and organic textures to create an interior that is as beautiful to experience as it is practical to use.”

Much of the residential architecture in Ireland is designed to work with the chilly, wet climate. “Traditional Irish architecture features deep window reveals, thick walls, and smaller windows than what might be seen elsewhere,” McKenna explains.

These choices could easily make a home feel dark and cold, but the designer says that when oriented correctly to the outside light, these elements only add to a cozy, warm environment in the winter and a cool place for relief in the summer. “I love that these characteristics also create a depth, shadow, and texture that are difficult to replicate with decorative finishes alone,” she adds.

Similarly to the architecture, the interiors are decorated to account for the damp climate, as well. “We make comfort a priority rather than a finishing touch, by layering furnishings such as window treatments, tactile rugs and cushions, and blankets,” McKenna says. Even if someone were to decorate their home rather minimally, the layers of tactile fabrics found in Irish homes still ensure the space feels inviting and lived-in.

Just because one of your surfaces is natural white oak doesn't mean everything in the same room needs to follow suit.

“We love mixing dark wood tones with light tones and everything in between,” says Leah Hook, founder and principal designer at Gray Oak Studio.

#6810. The Kitchen Trends Designers Expect to See Everywhere in 2027

The National Kitchen & Bath Association’s 2027 forecast has arrived.

Above: the kitchen of an Athens home designed by Nashmita Rajiah.

Partial walls, hidden appliances, and pantries with room for prep are among the kitchen features expected to gain ground next year, according to the National Kitchen & Bath Association’s 2027 Kitchen Trends Report. Based on a survey of 830 kitchen-industry professionals—including designers, manufacturers, remodelers, and architects—the report looks at the layouts, materials, lighting, and technology they expect to be popular over the next three years. Here are the main takeaways.

NKBA reports growing interest in partial walls, movable partitions, and separate kitchen zones—a shift from last year’s forecast, when open layouts were still gaining ground. Dedicated prep spaces and back kitchens are also emerging features, cited by nearly half of designers.

Kitchens are still expected to get bigger, with 52 percent of designers anticipating growth. The kitchen’s surrounding rooms are growing, too. NKBA reports that mudrooms and laundry spaces are increasingly being integrated into kitchen plans. The vast majority of designers expect attached mudrooms to be popular over the next three years, with 45 percent anticipating mudrooms getting larger and 43 percent expecting laundry spaces to grow.

Appliances are increasingly being incorporated into cabinetry rather than left on display. Eighty-five percent of designers agree cabinets are being designed to hide appliances and other functional elements, and roughly two-thirds say open shelving is on its way out in favor of enclosed storage.

Pantries are taking on more work, too. Nearly 90 percent agree they are becoming multifunctional extensions of the kitchen, and three-quarters expect dedicated small-appliance stations to be popular. Separate beverage areas continue to gain support, with 90 percent anticipating their popularity, up from 85 percent in the 2026 report.

Neutrals remain the leading color of choice, followed by greens and blues. For cabinetry, light and medium tones draw equal interest, and white oak is the preferred wood for roughly half of designers. Islands don’t necessarily have to match: a little more than half expect them to feature a different color from the surrounding cabinets. Quartzite and quartz lead countertop preferences, with nearly half of designers favoring honed or matte finishes.

The lighting forecast includes under-cabinet lights, illuminated cabinet interiors, nighttime path lighting, sconces, and pendants. Eighty-four percent of designers agree kitchens will incorporate multiple layers of light, and 78 percent say lighting is increasingly being used as a design feature rather than just a functional necessity. Under-cabinet lighting leads the forecast at 87 percent, followed by interior-cabinet lighting at 76 percent.

NKBA identifies ventilation, water filtration, touchless faucets, and sound-absorbing materials among the report’s wellness features. Designers are also discussing ways to make kitchens more resilient: 65 percent cite leak-detection systems and automatic shut-off valves, 62 percent cite backup power, and a little over half point to ventilation and air filtration.

#6809. Designers Reveal the Fastest Way They’ve Updated a Living Room Under $100

A few small swaps can make your space feel brand new.

A living room is arguably the most popular spot of the house, so why not give it the tender loving care it deserves with a design refresh? Problem is, when it comes to most home projects, it’s easy to convince yourself good things only come to those who wait—or anyone willing to fork over a small fortune.

Fortunately, affordability and efficiency are no longer mutually exclusive. According to the pros, it’s possible to elevate a space swiftly and without spending much money. Want proof? Keep scrolling for the clever (and quick) ways designers have upgraded a living room for $100 or less.

Barbra Fordyce of Ginger Lemon Indigo says plants are “hands-down” one of the best low-cost, high-impact ways to spruce up your space. “Bringing something living into your living room instantly changes the mood, quite literally bringing life, nature and an organic beauty into the room,” explains the designer. While fresh foliage can look good anywhere, she loves how plants can add a “verdant richness” to neutral rooms without throwing off the entire color palette.

That said, houseplants aren’t a one-size-fits-all situation, so choose wisely.

“If you have high ceilings, you want the plant to be proportional to those dimensions,” Fordyce says. “If the plant or tree is too small in a large living room, it will feel dwarfed.”

For the perfect finishing touch, use your leftover budget to buy a beautiful planter. “The container should echo the feeling of your room,” she shares. “A basket, ceramic container or even a vintage wooden crate can all make beautiful ‘pots’ for your plants.”

Though there is such a thing as too many throw pillows, the right ones can make a difference. “A pillow and throw refresh can go a long way in bringing new life to your sofa,” explains Natalie Kaelin, an interior designer in Raleigh, North Carolina.

Of course, there’s no need to buy brand-new lumbar and square cushions on a whim: Simply purchase covers for your existing pillows and swap them out seasonally.

Neutral linen layers can conjure coastal grandmother vibes virtually anywhere, while plush, jewel-toned velvets offer an elegant, autumnal look. Come winter, embrace the holiday spirit with flannel fabric or a plaid repeat.

We might spend a lot of time talking about layered lighting or how to pick the perfect fixture, but we cannot stress it enough: Pay attention to your bulbs.

“Daylight LED bulbs are incredibly white, so they put out light akin to doctors’ offices and hospitals,” says Elizabeth Cross-Beard of Palm Henri in Baltimore. “A warmer temperate bulb gives a home a candle-light, cozy feel.” As a general rule of thumb, she recommends buying lightbulbs with a temperature of 2700K and avoiding anything above 4000K.

Cross-Beard says switching your bulbs can effectively “redesign” your space, and it’s shockingly inexpensive. “Depending on how many lights you have, it can cost as little as $50,” she adds.

If you want to upgrade your space without spending a cent, Blair Burton says to start with your furniture.

“Sometimes simply pulling furniture away from the walls and creating a more comfortable conversation area can completely change how a room feels,” the Austin-based designer explains.

After all, just because an armchair has been in the corner of your living room for years doesn’t mean it needs to go there. By doing a room rearrangement, you might end up using it more when it’s in front of a warm, sun-drenched window. Maybe you’ll even realize you need to pick up an ottoman to create the ultimate cozy space.

Another $0 hack? Burton also encourages her clients to “shop their homes.” “It’s practical, of course, but it's also about honoring the things they've collected and loved over the years,” she explains. “Sometimes finding an unexpected new home for a favorite piece is one of the most satisfying discoveries in a project.”

“Adding artwork, whether it's a gallery wall, a single large-scale piece, or something in between, makes a huge visual and personal impact in a living room,” says Molly Torres Portnof of DATE Interiors in New York.

Retailers like Minted and Society6 offer well-priced prints, but you can also reframe photographs and posters you already own. As long as you pay attention to scale and proportion, the ceiling’s really the limit. “Rules are meant to be broken,” Portnof says. “If it feels right and makes you happy, you're on the right track.”

If your living room is blessed with beautiful built-ins, you can fill them with treasures you already own, or buy a few new pieces for well under $100.

“Take the time to create vignettes,” advises Tennessee designer Katie Kiser. “More often than not, I find my clients have all the pieces for a well-choreographed space, but they're styled improperly.”

For an instant yet affordable upgrade, head over to your local antique store to pick up some art books, jars, or a trinket tray.

#6788. Go Inside Miu Miu’s Handbag Atelier In Tuscany

Twenty years in, the brand’s matelassé leather remains a luxury calling card.

At the 18th-century Left Bank restaurant Lapérouse, Miu Miu presented its first-ever show in the French capital—now its permanent runway home—signaling a crucial differentiation from its sister brand Prada in Milan. Paper-thin knits, ultra-mini dresses, peplum jackets, chunky platform wedges, and mismatched-lace oxfords walked the intimate runway, on models adorned with white eyelashes and poppy-red lips. On the handbag front, an experimental leather-making technique was introduced, one that would become Miu Miu’s signature and a staple of aughts aesthetics: matelassé.

Originating in early 18th-century Marseille, the quilting technique was traditionally used in bedding or upholstery. In Miu Miu’s collection, it was developed in the form of a leather handbag—the Coffer—a slouchy shoulder tote in chocolate brown with a twisted handle and a bold metal clasp. A counterpoint to the youthfully eccentric clothes, the bag itself was a subtle debut, with only three shown on the runway and in only one colorway. Nonetheless, it was an immediate hit, carried by Anne Hathaway, Lindsay Lohan, and Isla Fisher in its early years. For the relatively young but increasingly iconic brand, a calling card was born.

Twenty years since that collection, Miu Miu has transformed its matelassé technique into a rainbow of colorways and style offerings. The seemingly seamless Wander and boxy Arcadie styles boast prime spots in the brand’s portfolio of leather goods, especially when it comes to the matelassé iterations—just take a look at the brand’s 2026 campaign starring Gigi Hadid.

The handbags come to life in Miu Miu’s leather goods workshop in Tuscany, the architecturally award-winning headquarters called The Garden Factory, built in the Valvigna region near where Miuccia Prada’s husband Patrizio Bertelli grew up, and designed by Guido Canali in the mid-1990s. Completed in 2015, the building itself is a physical manifestation of the Prada Group’s minimalist aesthetic.

The Garden Factory’s nickname derives from the property’s one-to-one ratio of building to greenery, a complete balance of cool steel and native vines, and plenty of glass windows to bridge the two. Inside is row upon row of accessories—66,000 leather goods and 77,000 pairs of shoes—carefully filed from the collections over the years to serve as both record and inspiration. Instead of season or collection, the cabinets are organized by product category, and matelassé has its own section.

Despite its creation 20 years ago—or perhaps in part because of it—the very tactility that makes Miu Miu’s matelassé leather so appealing is hitting a peak in current luxury collections across the board. To a consumer, the in-person experience was always a factor, but in the Instagram age, brands are turning to hyper-visual signifiers of craftsmanship to keep up with the market. Be it ASMR-inducing sequins, fluffy feathers, slick latex, or impossibly buttery leather, touch and feel appeal, even through an iPhone screen, is increasingly important. To see a matelassé handbag is to wonder how it feels, and to feel one is to experience its pillowy, permanent spring, an effect somewhere between plissé and origami. That effect is intentional, yet the product itself is softer in practice. That, too, is intentional.

The quilted technique is achieved by layering elastic Lycra with thin padding and nappa lambskin leather, sourced from Miu Miu’s tannery in France. Strategic patterns are stitched onto the layers and then the Lycra is removed, allowing the leather and padding to fold back into itself without losing the valleys created by the stitches. Thus, the matelassé effect comes to life. The smaller the product—like a card holder—the harder it is to craft. 3-D modeling is never used. Leather products are always assembled by hand, an important standard for the brand, and inspected manually by experts who know every detail of the bag’s design.

How does one become an expert in Miu Miu bags, you may ask (besides simply being a loyal fan)? The building’s minimalism, it seems, isn’t a lack, but an open space for creation to take place. And like the windows bridging steel to vine, or the archives linking past and present, or even the stitched valleys of a matelassé handbag, Miu Miu has mastered the spaces in between.

Paint is often regarded as DIY-friendly, but choosing this finish probably shouldn’t involve you rolling up your sleeves. “Hire a professional,” Levy says. “High gloss involves a lot of prep work to look right.” Since the results should almost resemble a mirror, Davis echoes this advice. “If a surface isn't perfectly sanded or smooth, high-gloss will show those imperfections,” she says. “It will ultimately take away from that perfect finish you’re after.”

As far as the best location for high-gloss paint, Davis has one strong preference. “High-gloss cabinets, from a practical standpoint, are forgiving in terms of wear,” she says. “You can easily wipe fingerprints, spills, and food right off the cabinet with a microfiber cloth.” Keep in mind, though, that chips will be noticeable, so this probably isn’t the right finish for busy households with young children.

As you determine the remaining details of your kitchen with a high-gloss paint in mind, remember to keep it front and center. Everything else in this space has to complement that sheen in order for the effect to be successful. “In a rustic kitchen, or one where natural wood is prevalent, high-gloss paint may clash with the overall vibe,” Davis says. The opposite is also true: A modern kitchen with too many shiny surfaces will feel like a carnival fun house.

To pull your kitchen together, seek out countertops and flooring with a matte finish alongside lighting and hardware that has a slight sheen. The goal is to find ways to ground the room, even as you center its high-gloss boldness. “I would love to have a client who’s daring enough to do a high-gloss kitchen,” Davis says. “Nevertheless, high-gloss paint can still have a timeless quality, while still having a big impact on the room.”

#6782. These 8 Common Heating Mistakes Could Be Costing You More Than You Realize, Experts Warn

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Staying cozy is a top priority in winter—whether you're curling up on the sofa or getting snug under the covers. But turning up the heating isn’t always the answer: some everyday habits can leave your home colder while pushing your bills higher.

From setting the thermostat too high to neglecting to bleed radiators, small heating mistakes can affect how warm your home feels and how much energy you use.

To help you stay comfortable without wasting energy, we asked heating experts to share the most common mistakes—and the simple fixes that can make your home warmer and your bills easier to manage.

When the chill sets in, it’s tempting to whack the thermostat up a few notches for a quick blast of warmth. But this common habit does more harm than good. “While it won’t heat your home any faster, what it will do is waste energy and therefore waste money,” say the heating specialist experts at Heatforce. “Each degree above 66 degrees Fahrenheit can add a whopping 10 percent to your annual heating costs.”

The smarter approach is to keep your thermostat at around 66 degrees when you’re at home and awake, then drop it by four to six degrees overnight. That way, you’ll stay comfortable without sending your bills soaring.

When arranging furniture, it’s easy to prioritize style over function—but placing bulky pieces too close to your radiator can drastically reduce its efficiency.

“Make sure that furniture and curtains aren’t obstructing this area of your home, as heat needs to be able to circulate freely,” says Amthal Karim, head of design at Furniture & Choice. “Where space allows, move sofas, armchairs, and other large pieces of furniture away from radiators to help warm air circulate more effectively around the room.”

Scott Byrom, a home energy expert at The Energy Shop, adds: “A large piece of furniture placed against a radiator can absorb a significant amount of the heat it emits, meaning you're paying to warm the back of your sofa rather than the room. Pulling furniture just six inches away allows warm air to rise and move freely, and you'll feel the difference almost immediately.”

Warm air can escape through gaps around doors, windows, and letterboxes, leaving rooms colder and forcing your heating to work harder—which can push up your bills over winter.

“Many older homes with hardwood front doors will experience worse drafts in the winter as they are susceptible to expanding in heat and contracting in the cold, meaning gaps around the frame will become even wider,” warns Oliver Novakovic, head of innovation at David Wilson Homes. “To combat this, invest in a draft exclusion runner to go along the bottom of your door. You can also purchase lengths of gap sealer to run around the frame, preventing cold air from blowing in around the door frame.”

Draft blockers, letterbox brushes, and simple sealant strips are all inexpensive fixes that can help a home retain heat far more effectively.

If you’re relying on an old heated blanket to stay warm, check its age and condition. It may no longer heat as effectively, leaving you tempted to turn up the thermostat and spend more on heating.

“Older electric blankets or those without overheating protection can pose a fire hazard,” says Adele Brennan, home electrical buyer at Vonhaus. “However, there are newer, innovative electric blankets that come equipped with an automatic shut-off function.”

A newer blanket with an automatic shut-off function is a safer choice, but using one to compensate for a cold room won’t fix the underlying heating problem.

It might feel indulgent to keep the heating running from dawn until dusk, but it’s an expensive habit that can quickly inflate your energy bills. “Instead, schedule your heating for convenient times,” says Stephen Hankinson, heating expert at Electric Radiators Direct.

“For example, if you like being comfortable as you get ready in the morning, program your heating to switch on just before you wake up and then off again by the time you leave.”

This way, you’ll enjoy warmth exactly when you need it, without wasting energy. Just don’t forget to adjust your schedule when the clocks change.

It's easy to focus on simply switching radiators on and off, but regular maintenance is just as important. Bleeding your radiators is essential for keeping them running efficiently. “After a while, pockets of air can get trapped within the radiators, causing cold spots and preventing hot water from flowing through,” explains Hankinson. “The usual sign your radiator needs bleeding is that the top section is much cooler than the bottom.”

To fix this, switch off your heating system, use a radiator key to open the valve, and keep a towel handy to catch any drips of water. Doing this at least once a year will keep your heating even and effective.

Drying clothes on radiators is common in colder, wetter months, but it can stop heat from circulating around the room. Your heating may then need to stay on longer to reach a comfortable temperature, using more energy and pushing up your bills. A clothes dryer in a well-ventilated room is a better option.

“The bigger issues are its contribution to mold and dampness within your home, as well as the inability to heat your home, so you're left with a damp, cold house throughout winter, which is costing you,” says Andy Simms from MyBuilder.com. “Instead, using a clothes drying rail or taking advantage of brighter, milder days to hang your washing outside or open windows can make a real difference. And when drying clothes indoors, using a dehumidifier is effective in keeping moisture low.”

It might feel inconvenient to book in a boiler service—especially if it means rearranging your day to be at home—but skipping it can be a costly mistake, as minor issues could go unnoticed, which could reduce its efficiency.

For peace of mind, it’s best to get your service done in autumn. That way, any small problems are caught early, and you’ll avoid the risk of being left without reliable heating during the coldest months of the year.

“Having your boiler serviced annually is a great action to take to prevent boiler breakdowns, as well as improve its efficiency. It helps keep your boiler operating at maximum efficiency, consuming less fuel to generate the same amount of heat,” says Ian Palmer-Smith, appliance repair expert at Domestic & General.

Above: A hallway view of Eric Egan’s Milan apartment.

If any season has the power to inspire a complete aesthetic overhaul, it’s fall. This fall in particular, designers are urging clients to trust their guts—making sure they see themselves in the spaces they create, rather than blindly following the latest fads. “For me, a trend has to be authentic or it’s just noise,” says designer Chad Dorsey, who’s eager to ditch the spare minimalism of years past for looks with more personality. ELLE Decor A-Lister Kerry Joyce seconds the motion: “By prioritizing personalized storytelling, a space becomes evergreen, evolving as smaller objects are swapped out over time.”

But an expert nudge in the right direction never hurts. This fall, top designers are gravitating toward creative color-drenching, cleverly concealed storage, tactile upholstery, and rooms built around art. Read on for their takes on the fall 2026 trends worth investing in, plus tips on building (as Dorsey puts it) “spaces designed to be lived in, not looked at.”

Fabric and upholstery are trending ultra plush and textured for fall, but when thoughtfully integrated, they feel more timeless than seasonal. “Design is shifting toward how a space feels, not just how it looks,” says Joyce, who’s seeing an increased popularity in fabrics like mohair, velvet, cashmere, and alpaca hair. “This focus on texture does more than elevate a room's aesthetic,” he explains. “It creates a sensory experience that comforts the soul.”

Start your bedroom redesign this fall and your space will strike the perfect balance between fresh and lived-in by spring. Between client requests and a personal home build, Amber Lewis has bedrooms on the brain right now. One trend takes the lead in her eyes: “I love matching the walls to the bed upholstery and bedding so the whole room feels wrapped in one warm color family. Luckily, this cozy trend doesn’t require a complete room overhaul: “The easiest place to start is the bed itself. Reupholster your headboard in a pattern you love, or drape a vintage suzani across the foot of the bed,” Lewis says. “One move like that changes the whole mood of the room without starting over.” To bring in a little dimension and pop, she recommends adding a singular statement, like a lone striped pillow.

If your primary suite needs a more holistic update, Lewis recommends creating a small sitting area. Sourcing vintage chairs and small sofas for primary suites this fall has given Lewis a new perspective on where bedroom design is headed: “True sanctuaries that feel layered, personal, and built for actually living in.”

Color drenching isn’t going anywhere for fall 2026—or long after, according to Dorsey. When choosing a shade for a room, he finds that any color works harder when it’s layered and intentional. Color capping—a term coined by Benjamin Moore referring to painting a room in multiple tones from one color family—can help keep a space from feeling flat or overwhelmed beneath a statement hue.

According to Dorsey, the staying power of color drenching goes hand in hand with the shift away from spareness and toward “collected” spaces: “Neither is really a ‘look;’ they’re about living with things you love, which is why they’ll carry well into spring.”

Designer Regan Baker says that cleverly hidden space solutions are also on the rise. “It could be a TV that slides behind a commissioned art panel, a Murphy bed tucked into a wall of millwork or that serves as a desk when it's stored, or a peg wall that’s a place to hang a jacket one minute and a standing desk the next,” she says. These solutions are tailored to her clients’ spaces (rather than tied to a season or palette), which gives them staying power.

This concept is an easy way to turn the corner you’ve never known how to style into your favorite part of the room. “In an existing home, look at the awkward spots, like an alcove or the wall around a fireplace, and treat built-ins there as a design moment instead of an afterthought,” Baker advises.

If there’s one built-to-last fall trend most designers can agree on, it’s added eccentricity. “Homes are steering away from the sterile, overnight look of a showroom delivery,” Joyce says. “The defining philosophy now focuses on layering modern staples with antiques, secondhand finds, travel mementos, and new pieces that carry a sense of patina.”

Dorsey describes this style as “collected rather than prescribed,” where a high-low mix of vintage and custom elements gel together. And while it may feel loftier than a trending, sold-out piece, Baker says that most clients start with an artistic anchor, then build around it: “We’re designing more homes around a piece of art than around a color trend. That might be wallpaper by local designers, a commissioned piece, or even a palette pulled from a lichen-covered rock found on the property.”

One key takeaway? Curate like a collector. Your reward may be a space that never goes out of style.

#6752. I Asked Designers for Their Secrets to an Expensive-Looking Guest Bedroom. They All Said the Same Thing

Skip the fussy pillow pile and focus on the details guests will actually notice.

Pictured above: a bold aubergine double guest bedroom designed by Nicole Fisher.

The holiday hosting season is upon us, and while you’re planning your tablescape decor and seasonal trimmings, you might want to take a hard look at your guest bedroom.

“Most guest rooms get treated like overflow storage, and guests feel that the second they walk in,” says Marie Cloud, principal designer at Indigo Pruitt.

Even if you host only occasionally, your guest room should give visitors a comfortable place to unwind. No need for a guest book or fuzzy slippers: Designers say these four thoughtful touches can make the room feel luxe without costing a fortune.

Regardless of the design aesthetic, the ambiance of a room always hinges on lighting. “I always pay attention to the lighting,” says Louis Lin, founder of his eponymous New York City–based interior design studio. “A beautiful bedside lamp with a warm, flattering glow can transform the atmosphere of a room. While a pile of pillows might look inviting, designer Marie Cloud says the bedtime legwork promptly cancels out the aesthetics.

“Skip the stack of eight decorative pillows your guest has to relocate before bed,” says Cloud. Her perfect formula for a top-notch guest setup? Two sleeping pillows, plus one lumbar pillow, all set atop a good set of sheets.

“Sheets with real hand-feel, like a crisp percale or washed linen,” Cloud suggests. “Finish with a throw folded at the foot in a color pulled from the room. It looks considered, and it’s actually comfortable at 2 a.m.”

A guest room is also the lowest-risk room in the house to be bold, so take the swing.”

The best guest setups make visitors feel anticipated, even when they show up unexpectedly. To achieve the boutique-hotel feel, designers recommend investing in small luxuries that leave a big impact. A few items Cloud and Lin always recommend having on hand are a small luggage rack, real hangers, fresh towels, and a select set of toiletries reserved especially for guests.

“The real luxury is making guests feel that the room has been prepared just for them,” says Lin.

#6735. These Are the Home Trends Gaining Ground for 2027, According to Zillow

A new analysis of millions of for-sale listings reveals the features sellers are increasingly highlighting.

Above: A color-drenched den on Long Island, New York, designed by General Assembly.

Homes are getting smarter, but sellers are also advertising places to unplug. Zillow’s 2027 Home Trends Report finds growing mentions of both AI and tech-free spaces, along with color-drenched rooms, home offices, and “catios.”

The report tracks year-over-year changes in mentions of hundreds of features across millions of for-sale listings. The results reflect how sellers and agents are marketing homes—not a survey of buyers or evidence that a particular feature will help a property sell. Still, they offer a look at the amenities and design details increasingly making their way into listing descriptions. Here are the main takeaways.

Mentions of AI are up 119 percent, the largest increase in this year’s report. Zillow points to AI-powered appliances among the features sellers are advertising, while mentions of app-controlled home systems rose 34 percent.

Spaces to unplug are also appearing 34 percent more often. The report describes an interest in both technology that simplifies daily tasks and designated places to take a break from it.

“Digital detox is the new luxury,” says Amanda Pendleton, Zillow’s home trends expert, in a statement. “Homes are getting smarter, but buyers also want tech-free zones where nothing needs charging. Sellers and their real estate agents are now highlighting spaces to unplug.”

Color-drenched spaces—rooms where the same color extends across the walls, ceiling, and trim—are up 84 percent in listing mentions, making them one of the report’s biggest increases. Zillow sees the approach as part of a move toward bolder, more polished interiors. Exterior finishes are gaining ground, too: painted brick is up 27 percent, and board-and-batten siding rose 24 percent.

Color drenching takes a shade beyond the walls and onto the ceiling, trim, and other architectural details. The approach has even spawned a bathroom variation: tile drenching, which extends the same tile across surfaces for a continuous look.

The language used to describe these homes is changing alongside the features. Mentions of “elevated” rose 71 percent, while “cozy” fell 22 percent, “eclectic” dropped 19 percent, and “boho” declined 13 percent. Cozy remains one of the most common words in listings, but it’s appearing less frequently than it did a year ago.

Kitchen language is moving in the other direction. Mentions of “chef’s kitchens” fell 21 percent, and “gourmet kitchens” declined 15 percent.

Mentions of multigenerational living jumped 58 percent. Zillow connects the increase to homes accommodating the “sandwich generation,” or people caring for children and older family members.

Features that can serve different ages and needs are also appearing more frequently. Grab bars are up 18 percent, wider doorways are up 13 percent, and nurseries rose 9 percent. The report places these changes alongside the growing emphasis on flexible rooms and spaces with more than one use.

The home spa is changing. Mentions of spa-inspired bathrooms rose 60 percent, and cold plunge pools increased 44 percent. Jacuzzis and jetted tubs, meanwhile, each fell about 9 percent.

Spaces for exercise are also gaining ground. Home gyms are up 18 percent, while golf simulators rose 29 percent. Together, the findings cover both workout spaces and amenities marketed around rest and recovery, rather than a single version of the home wellness room.

Cats get a category of their own this year. “Catios”—screened-in outdoor spaces that let indoor cats spend time outside—are appearing in 32 percent more listings. A place to get some air, watch the birds, and keep an eye on the neighbors—the cat has a wish list, too.
