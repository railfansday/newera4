#6935. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. Cisco says customers can extend the same access model to other infrastructure, including servers, Kubernetes clusters, and cloud resources.

#6929. Survey Finds 84% of Organizations Reported at Least One AI-Related Incident in the Past Year

Companies are running into problems as they give artificial intelligence more control over critical business applications, with 84% reporting at least one AI-related incident in the past year, according to a new survey from identity security firm Delinea.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Those gaps can make it difficult to identify segregation-of-duties conflicts, in which a person or agent has permissions across systems that should be kept separate.

Despite those issues, 95% of respondents said they were confident their access controls would withstand scrutiny in an external audit.

However, 26% reported access-related audit findings that had recurred in consecutive years, while 25% said some findings had remained unresolved for three or more years.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

Meanwhile, 60% reported an increase over the past two years in the number of critical applications operating outside the scope of access governance or Sarbanes-Oxley Act compliance.

The findings highlight the oversight challenges associated with giving AI agents authority to act within business systems, particularly when organizations cannot consistently identify who owns an agent or trace its actions.

Frank Vukovits, chief security scientist at Delinea, said organizations remain responsible for the actions AI agents take within their systems.

"When an agent can approve a transaction or change permissions, the business owns the outcome. You need to know what every agent can do, who owns it, and who approved it, before an auditor asks or your financials are misstated," Vukovits said.

#6922. Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

The findings echo a recent report about management software and AI e-mail assistants. Research from Fortra and Barracuda showed how criminals are using legitimate administrative tools and hidden AI instructions to manipulate users and compromise systems.

#6921. The Men on 'The Gilded Age' Don't Deserve Your Praise

They may be gentlemen, but their chivalry is sorely lacking.

It feels like television shows (and real life, let’s face it) are currently overrun with men who constantly fall short when it comes to matters of the heart. In the latest season of Nobody Wants This, many fans noticed how Joanne was constantly making sacrifices for their relationship while Noah just… wasn’t. The latest season of Love Is Blind also found the guys seriously wanting, with many failing to put in a bare minimum of effort to make a love connection. And it saddens me to say that this trend of low-effort men seems to have made its way to the fourth season of The Gilded Age as well. That’s right, my friends—not even the 19th century is safe from this epidemic of male apathy.

We may only be a few episodes into the season, yet already viewers have witnessed several examples where the men in the show are found lacking when it comes to their romantic partnerships. First, there’s Larry, who appears to have zero appreciation for the tough position Marian is now in after they spent the night together. Although he does suggest they get married, Marian worries it’s only out of obligation, and he seems to not really grasp the severity of the situation she’s in, that social ruin could be waiting for her if anyone found out about their night together.

Many fans online have been quick to come to Larry’s defense, criticizing Marian for self-sabotaging or playing the part of a martyr. But I disagree. Her concerns are valid and Larry is being ambiguous about his feelings. Now would be the perfect time for a grand romantic gesture on Larry’s part to show Marian how serious he is about their relationship. Where’s the big, swoonworthy “You have bewitched me body and soul” moment? Where is the standing-outside-her-window-with-a-boombox (or 1800s equivalent) scene? I want to root for them, but Larry is making it extremely difficult with his underwhelming devotion.

Then there’s Hector, who was praised by Bertha for choosing to save Gladys’s life over their unborn child’s during an extremely difficult labor. While it is nice to know how much affection he has for his wife, it never should’ve been his choice to begin with, so applauding him for “saving” Gladys feels like a very low bar to set for these male characters. I understand this was a customary practice at the time and him making the decision was more the doctor’s doing than anything else, but now he’s also gatekeeping that she can’t have any more children. Watching a man determine when a woman should know something about her own body feels outrageous in any time period. I’m not saying that Hector is a bad guy, but I don’t think he deserves so much admiration for doing the bare minimum.

Meanwhile, George Russell continues to deal with (or rather, not deal with) his ongoing laudanum addiction, which is already growing tiresome with each passing episode. His relationship with Bertha has never been worse. She is putting in all the effort to repair their marriage while he remains cold and distant, lying to her at every turn. I understand this change in his behavior is due to his drug dependency, but should that make all of his missteps completely forgivable? Bertha assured him at the end of Sunday night’s episode, “I won’t disappoint you.” But it is he who is the disappointment right now.

Unfortunately, not even Dr. William Kirkland can get a free pass, despite his impeccable behavior leading up to his wedding day. Buying Peggy a house, standing up to his mother, and refusing to let her dictate the wedding — those are all major green flags. Yet next week’s promo indicated William might pursue a new job in Washington, which feels completely out of nowhere considering he just bought a house for the two of them. Also, this is something he should probably consult his wife about before making any public declarations. Is the honeymoon phase over already?

Bottom line: Chivalry may not be completely dead on this show, but its pulse is weak at best and if these gentlemen don’t do something about it soon we’re going to have even bigger problems than prematurely pouring milk into someone else’s tea.

#6914. Study: Sign-In Problems Are Primary Drivers of Help Desk Tickets

Sign-in problems account for up to 53% of help desk tickets at individual enterprises, but network issues can take significantly longer to resolve, according to a new study from ControlUp.

Analyzing 1,069,110 help desk tickets, the IT operations company identified password resets, account lockouts, and multifactor authentication (MFA) issues as major drivers of support demand.

At one large enterprise, account-related tickets took a median of 11 minutes to resolve, compared with 50 hours for network problems. Meanwhile, 87% of network and phone tickets were reassigned between teams, compared with 20% of account tickets.

MFA enrollment and re-enrollment problems appeared at 40% of organizations, with one in five linking these issues to employees replacing or upgrading their phones. Virtual desktop problems appeared at 55% of organizations analyzed.

Traditional phone support remained a major channel for IT assistance.

Among organizations that recorded how support requests were submitted, 57% reported that phone calls accounted for approximately two-thirds or more of contacts. At one organization, that figure reached 99%.

Machine-generated tickets from monitoring, security, backup, and patching systems appeared at half of the organizations studied. At 15%, automated tickets represented between 25% and 50% of all requests.

The research covered enterprises across retail, healthcare, financial services, manufacturing, construction, energy, and IT services.

ControlUp said the findings highlight the need to evaluate recurring problems and their operational impact rather than relying solely on ticket volume and resolution speed.

"IT organizations have traditionally measured support performance by how quickly they close tickets, but that doesn't tell the whole story," said Simon Townsend, senior vice president of marketing and office of the CTO at ControlUp.

"A password reset that takes a few minutes and an infrastructure failure that disrupts an entire location shouldn't carry the same operational weight."

#6905. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

#6899. Phishing Threats Totaled 7.3 Billion in Q3 2026

E-mail phishing threats reached 7.3 billion in Q3 2026, with September activity increasing 41% from August, according to Microsoft Threat Intelligence.

According to a report from Microsoft Threat Intelligence and the Microsoft Defender Security Research Team, phishing detections reached nearly 3 billion in September alone.

The findings also highlighted changes in attacker techniques, including business-service impersonation, credential theft, and social engineering through Microsoft Teams.

The Redmond-based firm recognized approximately 129 million malicious messages spoofing the sender domains of tracked business services during Q3.

DocuSign was the most frequently spoofed brand in September, accounting for 29% of the month's tracked spoofing activity. Adobe, SurveyMonkey, and Barracuda also experienced increases toward the end of the quarter.

The research found that some malicious messages were delivered despite failing e-mail authentication checks and being identified as threats.

In September, tenant- or user-level security overrides applied to 63% of these messages with a recorded security outcome — most involved tenant-level exceptions.

Microsoft warned that overly broad exceptions intended to support legitimate business communications can weaken protection against impersonation attacks.

The company emphasized that these findings do not establish whether recipients opened the messages or whether accounts were compromised.

Microsoft also recorded approximately 141,000 malicious Teams call attempts during Q3.

Attackers increasingly used ordinary personal names instead of explicitly impersonating IT support personnel. Such display names accounted for 46% of an observed sample in July, rising to 88% in September.

Meanwhile, QR code phishing declined 67% compared to Q2, totaling roughly 12.1 million attacks.

CAPTCHA-gated phishing activity increased toward the end of the quarter, reaching 3.1 million detected attacks in September. SVG attachments played a significant role in the increase.

Credential theft remained the primary objective of malicious attachment campaigns, with approximately 94% to 96% of payload-based attacks directing users to phishing sites or fraudulent sign-in pages.

Microsoft recommended reviewing Exchange Online Protection and Defender for Office 365 configurations, enabling Safe Links and Safe Attachments, and strengthening authentication protections.

The company also advised organizations to review security exceptions and verify the identities of external Teams contacts before responding to suspicious requests.

#6858. Timothy Dalton's 007 Advice Is the Only Guidance Robert Pattinson Should Follow

Timothy Dalton’s James Bond advice should be the only suggestion he follows.

James Bond’s greatest weakness is not megalomaniacal madmen bent on world domination. Those he can handle. The superspy is far more likely to be taken down by well-meaning committees.

That’s why Robert Pattinson’s casting as filmmaker Denis Villeneuve’s new 007 is a bold and promising choice. The two of them together have amassed enough industry clout to craft the character their own way, to make him distinctive and daring in a way, hopefully, that we haven’t seen before.

Some things shouldn’t change, like his seductiveness, the Walther PPK, and the way he likes his martinis to be shaken. But those things only make up the silhouette of James Bond. Each actor must put his own imprint on the core of Ian Fleming’s debonair secret agent, just as the author did in his original novels. Sean Connery added ruggedness to the savoir faire. George Lazenby gave him a soulful vulnerability in his solo outing as the character. Roger Moore sharpened 007’s sense of humor. Each actor leaves his fingerprints behind as he hands it off to the next.

This leads to the essential question for anyone signing up to play James Bond: Are you strong enough to stand out?

It’s not so easily answered. I’ll let Timothy Dalton, my personal favorite Bond, explain: “I have some good friends from the Bond days, some really good people, but they’re trapped. Everyone’s trapped by it in a way,” he told me. “Whoever does it has got to have a very clear sense of what they want to achieve, because everybody in the world has got a different opinion about it.”

To figure out what comes next, we should look back at what came before.

Dalton (who happens to be Christopher Nolan’s favorite Bond too) made that comment to me back in February 2025, when Amazon MGM Studios first acquired the rights to the 007 character.

When he first took on the role in 1987’s The Living Daylights, his choice was to add a renegade wolf quality. But he also recognized that James Bond must remain, ultimately, the good guy, and that movie was entirely defined by an act of mercy. Fleming’s short story “The Living Daylights” makes up the film’s opening scene: Bond has been sent to take out an enemy sniper, but when he sees that his target is a beautiful cellist (played by Maryam d’Abo), he aims his own rifle to distract her rather than kill her.

He then becomes responsible for her life, and his 007 has no problem cutting down any foes who block their paths. Dalton’s Bond was more polished than Connery and less comedic than Moore, but he was led by a kind of stormy passion. He had heart, but it pumped cold blood.

Still, Dalton had to break through that formidable wall of consensus. Bond is not a solo enterprise, of course. The director has a say, as do the producers and the screenwriters, but the danger comes from two places: the studio and the audience itself. As Dalton said, “Everybody in the world has got a different opinion about it,” and those voices have to be defied, just as his 007 refused the order to place a bullet in the enemy cellist’s heart.

Nothing makes hands wring and sweat pour in a studio conference room like something unexpected, and know-it-all supposed “fans” often resist change while paradoxically demanding something fresh and new. He had two outings as Bond, the second being 1989’s License to Kill, in which 007 goes even more rogue, defying Her Majesty’s secret service to embark on a mission of revenge. Then the character became tied up in rights lawsuits for several years, and Dalton chose not to return.

When Daniel Craig took over 007 from Pierce Brosnan in 2005, purists complained about his blue eyes and blond hair. When they finally saw him bring the character to life in 2006’s Casino Royale, those critics soon fell silent. Craig gave Bond a soldier’s mindset. He was a battlefield veteran whose new uniform happens to be a dashing suit. He was successful for so long because as he added his own spin, he capitalized on the best of what came before: He was as rough as Connery and as lethal as Dalton. The humor and playfulness that defined Moore and Brosnan were still present, but they served as a mask for the kind of wounded vulnerability that Lazenby had introduced.

Pattinson is now free to pick and choose whatever qualities he likes from this Brotherhood of Bond, but if he is going to take anyone’s advice, it should be Dalton’s: “Whoever does it has got to have a very clear sense of what they want to achieve.”

Fortunately, no one tells Villeneuve what to do. The filmmaker has spent two decades establishing himself not just as a distinctive artist but as a reliable box-office draw. Those two qualities give him a powerful weapon—clout—that can stave off the onslaught of opinions that will inevitably surround this casting choice.

Just this Saturday, I moderated a Q&A with Villeneuve at the Golden Globe Foundation before an audience of students, and one young filmmaker named Oscar Flores asked the Dune filmmaker, “When is the right time for you to listen to feedback, and when is the right time to listen to yourself?”

Villeneuve gave an answer that perfectly matches Dalton’s advice and increases my optimism about his approach to Bond: “Honestly, people say that I’m humble, but that’s bullshit,” he said, only partly joking. “If I’m asking for $200 million for a movie, what kind of humility is that?”

The suggestion he made to the students was to not take on projects that are outside their control. Start small, stay true, and build up your skill set behind the camera while establishing a reputation that gives you defining power over the movies you make. He warned them not to follow the “siren” song of a big-budget offer if it means ceding control. Instead, Villeneuve said, he found it effective to build up his status with smaller films, moving from 2010’s more intimate Incendies to 2013’s harrowing Prisoners and 2015’s even more sprawling Sicario before leveling up in scale to 2016’s Arrival and 2017’s Blade Runner 2049.

“The thing that I did when I was a young filmmaker is that I tried not to jump steps,” he told us at the Golden Globes Foundation forum. “I went step-by-step on projects. I was able to keep control of them. I was making sure that I was in control of the tools, and control of the language, and getting strong with the crew, being able to and making sure that my voice would be heard. … What I will say to you is: Be careful of the sirens.”

Only after amassing this authority, Villeneuve said, did he feel the time was right to take on his dream project of Dune. “If you made a successful first feature film that cost $200,000 and they offer you a major blockbuster, be careful,” he said this weekend. “Be careful who you are working with, and don’t listen to your ego. Do you really believe that you have the skills to bring it to the screen? Maybe you do. Some people can, by the way. I was not ready to make Dune after my second [film]. ... It took me a lot of movies before I knew that I was able to handle the pressure and making sure that I would be able to control totally, 100 percent, the film. At the end of the day when I look at the movie and the screen, it’s my movie.”

No doubt Pattinson’s casting was his decision, and Villeneuve’s status ensured it was accepted by the powers that be at Amazon MGM Studios. It probably wasn’t a difficult yes, given that Pattinson is currently in his prime. Not only is he The Batman, but he stood out as the conniving usurper Antinous in Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey and has earned accolades for his haunting performance as a predator-hunting journalist in Primetime.

Is he a heartthrob? Ask the legion of Twilight fans, and cover your ears for their deafening reply.

Pattinson forged his bond with Villeneuve with another upcoming role in Dune: Part Three, playing the Face Dancer Scytale. He’s a shape-shifting psychological assassin who seeks to undermine Timothée Chalamet’s all-seeing, all-powerful emperor by getting inside his head, and the actor and filmmaker worked closely to figure out the look, mannerisms, and motivations of a creature void of form.

As Pattinson said in a recent cover story I wrote for Empire magazine about Dune: Part Three: “I went into it being like, ‘I want to look like an alien,’ and initially Denis was saying, ‘Well, I think he’s got to fit in with everybody. I think he just looks like you. He’s normal,’ ” Pattinson said. The actor was candid about his own reaction: “I was a little bit disappointed.”

Then Villeneuve thought about it some more. As the director told those students on Saturday, he has a strong point of view, but it’s not inflexible. Villeneuve is not interested in the whims of a crowd, but he is very open to suggestions from creative partners. If their own insights open his eyes to a possibility he hadn’t considered, he will seize upon the ideas.

Shortly before shooting, Villeneuve reversed course on Scytale, and Pattinson said the filmmaker sent him an email with a new look for the Face Dancer. Pale skin, white hair, no eyebrows, which has the effect of making his expressions somewhat inscrutable. In other words, a blank page, upon which the characteristics of others can be written as he shifts his own natural shape. Pattinson said he felt relief: “I’m like, ‘Okay, this is very much not like a normal guy at all.’ ”

This anecdote is meaningful for 007 because it shows that the filmmaker and the star can share ideas, experiment and explore, and listen to each other. That’s the collaboration this character needs.

At 40, Pattinson is not the youngest possible Bond, but he brings life experience that will be vital for playing a character who needs a certain prowess and wisdom to survive. He and Villeneuve both have a kind of double-0 swagger that will allow them to ignore the cacophonous advice of the masses and devise something original.

Pattinson has been doing this for a long time. Consider his doomed Cedric Diggory in 2005’s Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire, another character—like Bond, Bruce Wayne, and Edward Cullen—who exists as an intense, preexisting image in the minds of millions of fans. But with his brooding presence, he added uncertainty to Cedric’s innate decency and fair-mindedness. The kid who dramatically hops down from a tree in his jump-scare introduction to Ron and Harry could very well have been there to bully them. That’s a sneak-attack entrance, proposed by Pattinson himself, and little details like that gave this upright, wholesome character a dose of intrigue.

Pattinson was only 17 when he filmed that. In the more than two decades that followed, he has paid his dues, learned his lessons, and no doubt gotten his share of scars. With Villeneuve backing him, he’s bulletproof. The two of them can take chances, be bold, and defy orders—just like 007 would, just as playing the character demands.

Pattinson is not a paper doll that will fold when the committees come for James Bond.

#6850. Nanofat Is Dermatologists’ Best-Kept Secret for Younger, Fresher Skin

It’s not an injectable filler, and it’s not fat transfer. It’s a secret third thing you’ll want to try.

The last few years have been unkind to our facial fat. We sliced out our buccal pads to create sunken-in, razor-sharp cheekbones. We zapped our skin with thermal devices that accidentally melted fat away in pursuit of boosting collagen. We injected our stomachs with fat-loss GLP-1 medications, leaving our faces looking hollow. Droopy. Structurally unsound. As it turns out, facial fat is critical infrastructure—not just for looking younger, but for your skin's overall health and quality. And while you can’t choose where you lose or gain fat, you can choose to have a doctor siphon it, remix it, and reinject it.

“Adipose tissue is a rich source of growth factors and progenitor cells, and re-injecting fat is probably one of the best ways to regenerate the full potential of healthy skin I've seen in the last decades,” says Patricia Ogilvie, a Munich-based dermatologist and member of the Dior Reverse Aging Board. “There are trends that are not really worth being a trend. I think the restaging of the importance of adipose tissue is very justified.”

You’re probably familiar with fat transfer, a procedure that’s been around for decades. In simple terms, fat transfer takes adipose tissue from one area of your body, like your flanks, liposuctions it, and moves it to another area, like your breasts or cheekbones. “With traditional fat transfer, it’s about restoring volume. And because it’s your own living tissue, it can integrate beautifully and give you very natural-looking results,” says dermatologist Shereene Idriss, medical director and founder of Idriss Dermatology in New York City. “But what’s interesting is that fat isn’t just fat. It also contains regenerative cells, including adipose-derived stem cells, and growth factors that may help improve the surrounding tissue over time. Nanofat is where it gets really exciting.”

Nanofat is part of a new class of regenerative aesthetics offered by dermatologists and plastic surgeons, treatments that don’t just make you look better, but rather address the signs of aging from the inside out by stimulating collagen or repairing tissue. Imagine taking that same fat from a traditional fat transfer and processing it into a fine, milky liquid rich in your own stem cells and growth factors, then injecting or microneedling it back into your face—that’s nanofat.

“Instead of using it to restore volume, you’re using it to potentially improve skin quality itself,” says Idriss. “These cells and growth factors communicate with surrounding tissue and can stimulate processes involved in collagen production, tissue repair, and blood vessel formation. So you’re not just trying to fill a wrinkle. You’re trying to improve the skin itself—its thickness, resilience, and texture—so that wrinkle potentially becomes less noticeable.”

The best areas for nanofat injections include under the eye, a notoriously risky area for hyaluronic acid fillers, and the cheekbones. “The ideal patient for nanofat is someone who's starting to notice thinner, crepier skin, fine lines, or changes in skin texture, especially around the eyes, neck, or chest, and wants to improve skin quality rather than add volume,” adds Idriss, who both has had the treatment done and offers it in her practice. Plus, Ogilvie notes, nanofat doesn’t attract water the way fillers do, so it won’t cause under-eye puffiness (a common post-filler complaint). But because nanofat doesn’t restore volume as much as it reinvigorates your skin, you can inject it almost anywhere you’re noticing signs of aging.

“We do it under tumescent local anesthesia, so you’re awake the entire time, and recovery is usually pretty quick,” says Idriss. “I personally had nanofat and fat transfer in June and was back at work the next day. The biggest downside is some soreness at the fat extraction site, which usually lasts a few days, and you’ll want to avoid strenuous exercise for about a week. Because there’s no general anesthesia, you’re also avoiding the grogginess and recovery associated with being put under.”

Like any cosmetic treatment, it has risks and downsides. Experts note that nanofat isn’t the right treatment for anyone looking to restore significant volume loss or seeking instant results. “Improvement in skin quality can vary from person to person, and it takes months to see the full results,” Idriss adds. “There are rare but serious risks, including infection, vascular complications, and even vision loss when injecting certain areas of the face.” But patients who have tried the procedure say the recovery is quick and easy, with results compounding over weeks, though they ultimately fade away faster than traditional injectables.

Nanofat injections sit somewhere between microfat and hyaluronic acid fillers. Microfat can be lumpy and unpredictable when injected, sometimes leading to lopsided results. Plus, in any fat transfer, only a small amount of fat survives. Fillers, on the other hand, offer long-lasting precision and volume restoration, but patients are tired of looking overfilled and unnaturally plump. Nanofat helps your skin look naturally healthier, fresher, and firmer, at least for a while. After all, it’s made from you.

“I look at this as something you can do periodically throughout your life, but what you do in between treatments is just as important. And that’s why I don’t believe in demonizing any one treatment, including filler,” says Idriss. “When used appropriately, for the right indication, these treatments can all work beautifully together. It’s about knowing what you need, when you need it, and when enough is enough.”

K-pop runs on a completely different model: one where the company trains the individual into being an artist through a strict, meticulous formula within a larger system of strategic control.

None of this makes the overworking of artists acceptable, nor does it erase much of the valid concern so many people feel. We are allowed to worry when our favorite artist seems to be pushed too far, whether it’s by their own drive, their label’s instructions, or by the weight of fans’ expectations. And we’re allowed to question the very systems that make that possible—in K-pop, in Hollywood, or anywhere else.

But it’s also worth asking what it might mean for a system with decades of independent history—one that grew largely outside Western norms—to navigate a global spotlight (and the equally globalized standards that come with it). Where does cultural context end and basic safety begin? And who gets to decide what “better” looks like, if not the artists living it?

#6823. The One Record in Sports That Will Never Be Broken

In an age when numbers are paramount, a single statistic from hockey stands taller than the rest.

Back in my full-time sportswriting days—and, in fact, throughout my journalism career—I managed to disguise my fundamental innumeracy. Then suddenly, quite recently, numbers became paramount in sports. Baseball led the way, as it does in so many areas where boredom is involved. Now, thanks to gambling’s explosion in popularity, statistics have become one of the primary ways to be a fan.

In this era of analytics and the tyranny of numbers, there is one statistic that remains not only my favorite but that is likely never to fall, and it comes from, of all sports, hockey. It is this, which boggles the mind: Wayne Gretzky ended his career in 1999 with a total of 2,857 points, which includes goals and assists. Factor out the goals, and he’s still at 1,963, which is 42 more points than the player with the next-highest total, Jaromir Jagr, who is retired.

The first time I ever saw Gretzky play was during the 1988 Stanley Cup playoffs, when my Boston Bruins faced his Edmonton Oilers. These were the dynastic Oilers, loaded with talent: Mark Messier. Jari Kurri. Glenn Anderson. Grant Fuhr between the pipes. All of them Hall of Famers. Amid all this clamorous talent, Gretzky was a phantom. He had this strange and wonderful ability to vanish in the middle of the action and then, suddenly, appear in exactly the right spot at exactly the right time to make exactly the right play.

Gretzky will likely be the leading point scorer in NHL history for the next several decades. In fact, you have to go all the way down to seventh place on the list to find an active player, Sidney Crosby, who is 202 short of Gretzky’s 1,963 assist-only number. Another active player, Alex Ovechkin, is in tenth place, but he’s got one skate out the door already. The next active players on the list are Evgeni Malkin and Patrick Kane, at 23rd and 24th.

Like Gretzky himself, this statistic disappears among all the more important numbers until it suddenly pops out to amaze and astound. I like Sidney Crosby a great deal as a player, but I hope he never gets there.

#6803. Designers Say "Cook's Tables" Are About to Replace Kitchen Islands in 2027

Their versatility is just part of their appeal.

Pictured Above: A kitchen designed by Amy Sklar.

For years, oversized kitchen islands have been the be-all and end-all, but lately, designers and homeowners are gravitating toward something a bit smaller: a cook’s table.

Unlike built-in islands that can sometimes dominate a kitchen and cover most of the available floor space, cook’s tables bring a lot of flexibility to the room. Because they’re freestanding, you can move them around depending on your needs (especially if they’re on wheels), whether that’s for prepping meals, hanging out with your morning coffee, or helping kids with homework after school. They also tend to be narrower, making them the perfect solution for small kitchens.

The other benefit is the big charm factor they add. They’re often wooden, with traditional-looking design elements, like turned legs and curvy silhouettes. Cooks tables can also frequently be found in vintage and antique stores, so they’re a great way to work a bit of charm and patina into an otherwise utilitarian space.

As kitchens move away from ultra-sleek and minimal, pieces with warmth and character are becoming more and more popular, and a cook’s table is the perfect example of that. If you’re considering adding one to your own home, learn what to look for—and how to style it—below.

One of the biggest reasons cook’s tables are trending right now is that they soften the overall look and feel of a kitchen. “A traditional island can dominate a kitchen,” says designer Amy Sklar. Because cook’s tables don’t have a bulky built-in base, they feel visually lighter and less imposing, especially in kitchens where space is limited.

Designers also point to the emotional shift happening in homes post-pandemic. “The pandemic reshaped the way we live at home, placing greater value on spaces designed for connection and togetherness,” says designer Becky Ducsik of The Phinery. “Nowhere is this more apparent than in the kitchen, where homeowners are reimagining how they gather and entertain.”

Unlike traditional islands that are packed with appliances and storage, cook’s tables create a more communal focal point centered around gathering and spending time together. That relaxed atmosphere is part of what makes them feel so appealing. Designer Cynthia Lananh notes that cook’s tables strike a balance between practicality and personality, functioning as prep space, dining space, and entertaining hub all at once. “I love a kitchen island that looks like a piece of furniture,” she says, adding that the look feels more casual, approachable, and timeless than a standard built-in island.

While cook’s tables can absolutely make a statement in a large kitchen, designers say they’re especially effective in kitchens with tighter footprints or galley-style layouts. Because they take up less visual and physical space than a traditional island, they can help smaller kitchens feel more open and airy.

That said, larger kitchens can benefit from them too, just in a different way. “With the right vintage find or custom-crafted piece, it can anchor the room and become a striking centerpiece that adds both function and timeless appeal,” says Ducsik.

She recommends using cook’s tables as the focal point in kitchens where storage and appliances can be concentrated along the perimeter. Since seating typically wraps around all sides, she suggests leaving at least four to five feet of clearance around the table to maintain a comfortable flow.

For homeowners who want the look but don’t have space for a freestanding table in the center of the room, Ducsik also suggests integrating one as a peninsula extension at the end of cabinetry for a similar communal feel.

Unlike built-in islands, cook’s tables offer a level of flexibility that’s hard to beat. Since they’re freestanding, they can be moved or adapted more easily over time—a feature designers say is especially useful for entertaining.

Sklar points out that cook’s tables can create a flexible landing zone within a kitchen and are ideal for holidays or large gatherings when extra room is needed. “This is perfect for holidays and large gatherings where you need even more space,” Lananh says. She recommends adding casters if the table will double as both a prep and dining space, making it easier to move around as needed.

Scale is one of the most important things to get right: The table should feel proportional to the room while still leaving enough space to comfortably move around it. Height matters, too. Sklar notes that many vintage tables sit lower than standard counter height, so homeowners may need to modify them slightly with casters or leg extenders to make them more functional for prep work.

As for materials, wood remains the go-to choice for warmth and character. “Durable woods (like oak, maple, or walnut) are popular for their warmth, strength, and character,” says Lananh. “Reclaimed wood adds rustic charm, whereas metal or butcher block tops offer a more durable, industrial aesthetic.”

Ducsik echoes those sentiments and suggests echoing the table’s wood tones throughout the kitchen with details like open shelving, cutting boards, or serving pieces to create a cohesive look.

Lastly, focus on shape. Softer corners and curves can make the space feel more intimate, while rectangular tables can maximize seating and prep space. Ultimately, the goal is for the piece to feel like furniture, not cabinetry. And that’s exactly what makes cook’s tables so appealing in the first place.

#6791. The Story Behind Peggy Scott’s Wedding Dress

The Gilded Age threw the wedding of the year for the fan-favorite character, played by Denée Benton

Every product on this page was chosen by a Harper's BAZAAR editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

It’s 88 degrees on a humid June day in Troy, New York, and Denée Benton stands in a local church’s outbuilding, posing in Peggy Scott’s wedding dress, not breaking a sweat.

It has been a long, hot day of filming, and the cast and crew are scheduled to keep going for many more hours. To get to this photo shoot, I had to walk past a cramped church hallway where Cynthia Nixon, Audra McDonald, and John Douglas Thompson sat together in various stages of Gilded Age undress, trading inside jokes. Everyone on set is in a state of elation, both because of the long-planned-for episode featuring the wedding of Peggy and Dr. William Kirkland and because it’s the day of Game 4 of the Knicks’s historic NBA run. A few hours after I leave set, Nixon will post a reel of the cast reacting to the Knicks win in their Gilded Age finery. But even amidst all of this excitement, Benton in her wedding dress steals the show.

Writers, production assistants, costume designers, and even Jordan Donica, who plays Dr. Kirkland, gather to watch the shoot. Benton, in the middle of it all, is poised in the gown of white lace, a corset underneath, dress lines crisp, makeup unsmudged, a picture of bridal serenity. Later, Kasia Walicka Maimone, the show’s costume designer, will tell me she spied Benton doing breathing exercises in the church pews, just to maintain her stamina.

Peggy has been a fan favorite since her introduction in the show’s first episode. An ambitious, upper-class Black woman in Gilded Age America, she is an anomaly in period television. Her storyline has introduced viewers to the world of the Black elite, a burgeoning class that emerged during Reconstruction and would dominate Black political and cultural life to the present day. Popular nonfiction books like Our Kind of People have documented the history of this group, but they have been largely absent from television. Even Black weddings, of any time period, are rarely portrayed on American TV. So how do you design a dress for a wedding that hasn’t been seen before?

“The Gilded Age,” as the period between the 1870s and 1890s is known, “was famous for its excitement [around fashion],” says Walicka Maimone. “How do we represent this excitement? How do we pull out from the historical research things that we, the modern audience, are going to be excited about? We have this giant library of about 50 to 60,000 images that we’ve collected since season 1 … but there’s always manipulation that I apply to it.” Peggy’s wedding dress “resonates with a lot of elements from the period, but it’s not a direct quote of any one specific dress.…What was really important to both Denée and me was the composite of the silhouette [and] the texture of the lace,” a detail that was “referred to in the script directly by her mother-in-law.”

“The speed that we had to accomplish the design and execution and put it on the screen was astronomical,” Walicka Maimone says. “Hundreds of hands altogether contribute to the making. Each dress takes about six to eight weeks to be built.” Those working on the final bridal look included the in-house costume-design team, milliners for the veil and hat, a jeweler in Rome who designed Peggy’s earrings, and on-set tailors who maintained the costume during filming.

“My wedding dress had over 75 buttons going down the back. Amber, my head dresser, buttoned each one by hand. The craftsmanship was really unbelievable,” Benton tells me a few months later. “It felt powerful to me, spiritually. It felt like we were embodying a moment for a lot of people. I have never seen a luxury Black wedding of the 1800s on any screen, on a stage, in any popular media. And so it really feels like we’re getting to be a part of a pop-culture history moment.”

Peggy is a child of post-Reconstruction America. Her father, Arthur, experienced enslavement, but she is part of the first generation of Black people born into freedom and citizenship. The thrill of watching her on screen is seeing a character who relishes pushing against the boundaries of what that might mean. She is both an avatar of modern viewers’ desires and a fantasy of what Black womanhood might have looked like in a time of dynamic social change and fabulous dressing. For Peggy, the question of a wedding and marriage takes on additional significance.

“Because the law of the land during enslavement recognized enslaved people as property, [and] therefore not protected by the law, nor were there unions, whether they were chosen unions or forced unions … they could be separated at any moment. The law did not protect the sanctity of marriage for Black people, for enslaved people,” explains historian Erica Armstrong Dunbar, who is chair of the African-American Studies department at Emory University, in addition to a consultant on The Gilded Age.

Marriage for the Gilded Age generation of Black people was an assertion of agency and an act of claiming Black humanity. The ability to choose partners and form Black families—and have those family bonds legally protected—was a radical act in the aftermath of slavery. All of this history can inform the context of Peggy Scott’s marriage, but what that wedding might look like on screen is another question.

“When we first started talking about the season and the wedding, Sonja Warfield [the episode’s writer] and Julian Fellowes [The Gilded Age’s creator] were very invested in the notion of the jumping of the broom and that being a part of African American wedding tradition,” Armstrong Dunbar explains. Jumping the broom was a wedding tradition practiced by some enslaved people, a ceremonial act to mark a bond that by definition was not legally recognized.

“I immediately was like, but that’s not really happening in the 1880s,” Armstrong Dunbar explains. “People were actually distancing themselves from many of the traditional experiences connected to the weddings of enslaved people.”

Fellowes, Warfield, and Gilded Age executive producer Salli Richardson-Whitfield and producer Davita Scarlett, however, were all adamant about including this practice in the wedding episode. Many of them had participated in broom-jumping ceremonies in their own weddings; the practice became popular again in the 1970s, ’80s, and ’90s as post–Civil Rights era couples looked to include specifically African American rituals in their wedding ceremonies.

But part of the pleasure of The Gilded Age is watching how the show plays with historical conventions and anachronisms to further draw viewers into characters’ lives. In this case, the writers and producers decided to give Peggy’s father, Arthur, who was once enslaved, the task of handing the broom he used in his wedding ceremony to his daughter, a moment that recognizes the family’s beginnings and its triumph of seeing a freeborn daughter legally wed. “It really encapsulated the way in which people try to meld traditions when they’re bringing their families together,” says Scarlett. “It just felt too delicious and too interesting and too thematically rich to pass up.”

“I knew that we were going to be doing Peggy getting married, and I claimed it. I said, ‘No one else is allowed to direct this wedding but me,’ ” says director Richardson-Whitfield. “I just wanted to make sure that it was as lush and gorgeous and special as anything else that we’ve done on the show.”

This included the decision to feature a gospel choir. “We specifically chose also to do it a cappella and not have any instruments with the music because I felt like those beautiful Black voices were enough to fill the church,” she says. An a cappella chorus singing spirituals would have been, like the broom jumping, frowned upon by many upper-class Black people in the 1880s, but that tension provided another opportunity to deepen the wedding scenes. “Just because we are African American people doesn’t mean we all think the same thing,” Richardson-Whitfield says. “It’s nice to be able to explore different kinds of Black people.”

The show’s commitment to nuance and its willingness to acknowledge the historical existence of different kinds of marginalized people means its fan base stretches far and wide. Recently, the creative agency Little Big World hosted a Gilded Age drag ball and panel on queer black history with Benton, activist and Gilded Age fan Brittany Packnett Cunningham, and queer historian Hugh Ryan. The event was packed.

“I think that people see Peggy as this sort of doll princess,” Benton tells me, “but as the actor playing her … bell hooks has this quote that’s like, ‘Queer not as in who I sleep with, but queer as in at odds with everything around me.’ That is Peggy. Period. There was not really a class of professional women [during the Gilded Age]; you were either aristocracy or you were working class. And then to be a Black professional, she really is one of one moving through this space in her time. And I find that to be very queer.”

“That’s one of the two powers of history work for me,” says Ryan. “One is seeing … ‘Oh, things actually were really different back then. So that means they could be really different in the future.’ And I think that's liberatory. But the other part, and this is where I think The Gilded Age shines … is getting people excited. I was in a panel with 60 Black queer folk in a Black queer space in Bed-Stuy talking about the Gilded Age and Black drag kings in vaudeville. That conversation only happens because beautiful art sparks our interest and allows us to see a place in the past.”

“As an artist, you can’t really ask for more than to do something that is popular and meaningful at the same time,” Benton says. “The sweet spot.”

#6753. How to Talk to Your Friends and Family About the Trans Community, According to Experts

If you get flustered whenever you speak up about trans rights, you're not alone. That's why we put together this list of expert-approved talking points.

Since Donald Trump took his second term in office, he and his administration have issued a series of discriminatory executive orders restricting access to gender-affirming care for trans youth, banning trans women and girls from participating in women’s sports, declaring that the federal government will only recognize two genders (impacting identifying documents like passports and visas), banning transgender people from serving in the military, and so much more.

Thankfully, many of these executive orders are being challenged in court. But the messaging has already infiltrated the brains of Trump supporters (and beyond) far and wide, normalizing these hateful thoughts and actions in our country.

So what do we do? We speak out and spread facts. Especially with those closest to us who might not be informed or might not understand. That’s not always easy though, especially when emotions get involved and you end up getting upset and flustered. To help, I tapped three experts to create a list of responses to common (and problematic) refrains about the trans community you might come across in future discussions.

In any discourse with someone who doesn’t think like you, it’s always important to make sure the other party knows you’re actually also listening—not just arguing. But if tensions do get high, the Q&As below can serve as a script of sorts to fall back on when you want to make sure you’re hitting all the key talking points while supporting and speaking up for the trans community.

Dr. Meredith McNamara: Actually, some people are born with neither or with genitalia that doesn’t fit binary concepts of sex. What sex is someone who is born without a vagina but has XX chromosomes (MRKH Syndrome)? What sex is someone who has XXY chromosomes and little to no detectable testosterone levels in adulthood (Klinefelter syndrome)?

But let’s take a step back and look at this from a different angle: Gender and sex are different. Gender identity develops long before the physical change of puberty. People generally know their gender long before secondary sex characteristics develop, because gender identity has its own strong biological basis rooted in the brain and it exists separately from chromosomes, gonads, and hormones.

Gillian Branstetter: And why not? Every culture in human history has had people who reject their gender assignment in one way or another, and decades of feminist legal progress has challenged the idea that biology must be our destiny. Transgender people come from all walks of life—every ethnicity, every background, every country, every political party. All people deserve the freedom to be themselves without fear, and that includes transgender people.

Shane Diamond: Everybody has a gender: an internal sense of self as it relates to the gendered world around us. For many people, that inner sense of self matches the sex they were assigned at birth—for example, someone assigned female at birth who knows herself to be a woman. This simply means they are cisgender! For other people, their inner sense of self is different from their sex assigned at birth, causing an incongruence with who they know themselves to be and how the world sees them. This person might call themselves transgender. That doesn’t make anyone’s gender more or less valid than someone else’s—it just means they’re different. #6746. In Being Heumann, Ruth Madeley Brings to Life One of America’s Most Important Disability Activists

The film, based on the Judy Heumann memoir of the same name, is now streaming on AppleTV

Being Heumann, the new film about disability activist Judy Heumann, played by Ruth Madeley, opens with one of those classic New York City street montages: the morning rush-hour commute, a close-up of feet pounding the sidewalk, yellow cabs buzzing by. Then, a wheelchair speeding down a bumpy road, splashing in puddles. Judy, a woman riding a motorized wheelchair in the middle of the road, weaves between traffic and over steaming manholes. She crosses the street, but there are no ramps on the sidewalk, so she gets back on the road, zigzagging between drivers yelling at her to get out of the way. When a giant city bus gets behind her and threateningly honks its horn, Judy simply raises her right hand, gives the driver the middle finger, and continues on her way unfazed. We are barely three minutes into the film.

Written and directed by Sian Heder, whose previous film, Coda, won Best Picture and Best Adapted Screenplay at the 2022 Academy Awards, Being Heumann chronicles the 504 Sit-In, the longest nonviolent occupation of a federal building in U.S. history. It led to the passing of Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, which, among other things, made it illegal to discriminate on the basis of disability, called for making public buildings and transportation accessible, and would set the basis for the Americans With Disabilities Act, 1990’s landmark civil-rights legislation.

“So often, disabled characters in particular—and especially women—are very softly introduced,” says Madeley a few days before the movie debuts on AppleTV+. “[But] this is who Judy is. She was doing this every day. You’re completely captivated by ‘Oh my God, where is this woman going?’ I remember her brother said there were so many times she nearly got hit by a car.” Judy’s unapologetic attitude is the driving force behind her activist group—even if it sometimes puts her at odds with others.

There is a popular saying on social media, “Well-behaved women rarely make history,” and yet so often women—even activists, or especially activists—need to fit into neat preconceived notions of how to respectfully ask for instead of demand change. “[Judy] wasn’t always likable. She was an asshole sometimes, she was stubborn, and she could railroad people sometimes … but I think that’s what makes her so compelling,” says Madeley. “The audience understands where she’s coming from because she had years of being told, ‘No, you can’t go to school,’ ‘No, you can’t be a teacher,’ ‘You can’t come in here, you’re a fire hazard.’ Just years of injustice. And she was done with it. She was tired of it.” Madeley adds, “I was so excited that Sian and Rebekah [Taussig, who cowrote the script] didn’t round off Judy’s edges, because she was a complex person. She was messy, and I love playing a messy, complicated woman.”

Being Heumann tells the story of one of the most important leaders of disability rights in American history, and it has already broken ground for its diverse cast of actors who are disabled for its main roles. Still, Madeley rejects its purpose as a mere “history lesson.” “This is what people want to go to the movies to see. They want to laugh, they want to cry, they want to go on a ride with a bunch of people storming a building when they have absolutely no idea what they’re doing,” she explains. The movie does in fact have the ebullience of the classic “us against the world” trope (my mind goes to Empire Records, a personal favorite, except with higher stakes): As the activists hunker down inside the Federal Building in San Francisco, they turn the space into a temporary utopia; the Black Panthers bring them food and weed, and they host classes and panels on topics like sexuality. (In one of the best scenes in the movie, as Judy is getting intimate with Jim Pechin, her fellow activist who’s been flirting with her since the beginning of the movie, she says, “I’m going to tell you what I want, and you’re going to do as I say.” What other movie has ever displayed this level of control and empowerment in a sex scene? “The complexity and the different nuances of that scene really interested me, and I’d never seen anything like that on screen before,” Madeley says.)

Another scene that broke ground on how we’ve seen disability on film comes early on, when Judy is in bed waiting for her home attendant, who is running late, and really has to go to the bathroom. “Movement fascinates me on screen. It always has,” Madeley reveals. “What really interested me was how Judy was so quick and forever moving and busy when she was in her chair, like she is free in her chair, and then the stark contrast to how vulnerable and dependent on other people when she’s out of her chair. That scene is really poignant because Sian didn’t make it choppy. She wanted it to be in real time, and she wanted the audience to feel the rigamarole. Judy has no core strength, so she has to be balanced, she has to move the plates on her wheelchair, then she’s transferred, then she has to move the plates back, take the locks off… There are all these different stages just to be able to use the bathroom.” She adds, “Honoring Judy’s physicality was just as important to me as honoring all the different layers of her personality.”

Madeley, who also portrayed British disability-rights advocate Barbara Lisicki in the BBC film Then Barbara Met Alan, wants people to have fun while watching Being Heumann. That said, she also hopes they come away with “a better understanding of the physical world around them.” She continues, “Every time I go down a curb cut, I’m like, ‘Thanks, Judy.’ That curb cut wasn’t just there; it didn’t just appear. It was fought for.”
