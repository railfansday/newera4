#7077. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

#7069. Sophos Launches Tool for Shadow AI Defense

Unauthorized artificial intelligence tools and agents are creating security risks for organizations as AI adoption increases, according to cybersecurity company Sophos. In a recent product announcement, the company highlighted the growing use of shadow AI, referring to AI tools and agents operating without formal organizational approval or oversight. The security firm cited research indicating that 69% of organizations suspect or have evidence of unauthorized AI use.

Separate research from Gartner and Cloud Security Alliance found that 82% of organizations discovered shadow AI agents in their environments during the previous year.

According to the security firm, organizations have struggled to identify which AI tools employees use, what information those tools access, and whether their use complies with security policies.

"Organizations are adopting AI faster than they can secure it, and most security teams can't answer three basic questions: which AI tools are running, what data is going into them, and whether that activity lines up with policy," said Kyle Falkenhagen, senior vice president of product management at Sophos.

The company also cited an IDC projection that the number of AI agents could reach 1.3 billion by 2028.

Sophos researchers reported observing attackers use AI agents within a customer's network to build and test nearly 80 malware modules.

The cybersecurity firm announced the general availability of Sophos AI Defense, a security offering designed to identify AI tools, monitor their activity, and enforce organizational security policies.

The product operates through Sophos Fusion, the company's cybersecurity platform, and uses information from devices, networks, identity systems, and browsers.

According to the company, AI Defense can inspect prompts, uploads, and other AI interactions to identify potentially risky activity and help prevent sensitive data exposure.

#7063. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#7059. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

Cisco says customers can extend the same access model to other infrastructure, including servers, Kubernetes clusters, and cloud resources.

#7008. Survey Finds 84% of Organizations Reported at Least One AI-Related Incident in the Past Year

Companies are running into problems as they give artificial intelligence more control over critical business applications, with 84% reporting at least one AI-related incident in the past year, according to a new survey from identity security firm Delinea.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Those gaps can make it difficult to identify segregation-of-duties conflicts, in which a person or agent has permissions across systems that should be kept separate.

Despite those issues, 95% of respondents said they were confident their access controls would withstand scrutiny in an external audit.

However, 26% reported access-related audit findings that had recurred in consecutive years, while 25% said some findings had remained unresolved for three or more years.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

Meanwhile, 60% reported an increase over the past two years in the number of critical applications operating outside the scope of access governance or Sarbanes-Oxley Act compliance.

The findings highlight the oversight challenges associated with giving AI agents authority to act within business systems, particularly when organizations cannot consistently identify who owns an agent or trace its actions.

Frank Vukovits, chief security scientist at Delinea, said organizations remain responsible for the actions AI agents take within their systems.

"When an agent can approve a transaction or change permissions, the business owns the outcome. You need to know what every agent can do, who owns it, and who approved it, before an auditor asks or your financials are misstated," Vukovits said.

#7001. Inside the Lavish Estates Where The Gilded Age Was Filmed

These locations are straight out of 1880s high society.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE Decor editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Why should the Brits have all the fun? The writer of Downton Abbey, Julian Fellowes, went stateside with the HBO series The Gilded Age, which tells the story of New York City high society in the 1880s amid the halcyon days of the industrial boom. The series explores the tension between old-money elites and their escalating feud with the nouveau riche neighbors who threaten the established social order. Meanwhile in real life, powerhouse families like the Vanderbilts and the Carnegies left their opulent footprints on Newport, Rhode Island, in the form of their famously ostentatious and absolutely gargantuan “summer cottages.” Circumstances which lend themselves to set-design magic.

In honor of season four of The Gilded Age, which premiered November 1, we’re revisiting the real-life settings that have helped bring the series to life. The social alliances may shift, but the architecture remains a dependable scene-stealer. Below, tour 21 properties that rank among the East Coast’s architectural crown jewels, from historic house museums to lavish mansions.

Features as: Bertha Russell’s ballroom and George Russell’s bedroom, as well as the billiard room.

These so-called dollar princesses and their families lived in Manhattan and spent their summers in Newport, Rhode Island. Of course, these industry magnates didn’t consider their infrequent visits (some only came a few weeks in a year) as a reason to exercise restraint. The mammoth Breakers mansion is one of four so-called summer “cottages” (read: castles) that were completed just blocks from each other in a building boom between 1892 and 1902. The mansion was designed by Richard Morris Hunt in the Renaissance Revival style, as a less-than-subtle symbol of the Vanderbilt family’s social and financial preeminence in the Gilded Age. It was architectural one-upmanship that is considered the grandest of all the manses, featuring ornate ballrooms, extensive art collections, manicured gardens, and the latest (then) in modern technology, including electric lights. It has 70 rooms, an interior using marble imported from Italy and Africa, and a library mantel purchased from a château in France.

“When you walk into the dining room of The Breakers, with its painting of the goddess Aurora on the ceiling 50 feet above you or see 500,000 cubic feet of marble in a variety of different hues, you can’t help but be awestruck by the incredible wealth of these Gilded Age tycoons,” says Trudy Coxe, the chief executive officer and executive director of The Preservation Society of Newport County, which has under its stewardship six of the grand mansions featured in the series.

The preservation society worked closely with the filming crew to ensure the safety of the objects and spaces. “We had long discussions about the billiard table at The Breakers,” she explains. “It could not be removed, but it was important to the scene being filmed. Rather than moving it, we worked with their props department to put an alternative covering on the table to simulate an actual game of pool.”

In the series, the Breakers’ neo–Italian Renaissance great hall and music room are featured as Bertha Russell’s (played by Carrie Coon) ballroom, which we see in the final ball scene. George Russell (Morgan Spector) also discusses business while playing billiards in the mansion’s massive billiard room.

Features as: Bertha Russell’s bedroom in the Russell residence

Marble House was built from 1888 to 1892 as a summer cottage for Alva and William Kissam Vanderbilt. It was also designed by Richard Morris Hunt, but in the Beaux-Arts style. For an American house, it was unparalleled in opulence, with a temple-front portico resembling that of the White House, which had been completed 92 years earlier in 1800 (lofty, no?).

Mrs. Russell’s bedroom, built on a soundstage in Bethpage, New York, was inspired by real-life socialite Alva Vanderbilt’s bedroom in the Marble House. The French Louis XV bed was painted and reupholstered in salmon-pink velvet, and the custom bedding used 300 yards of fabric that have been draped, cinched, and swagged.

And that’s just the textiles. Most of the room’s furniture is refurbished antique and everything in the room has been painted to match. Sometimes, Shaw notes, it was difficult to paint a lovely wooden antique piece. “The scenic artists would look at us and say, ‘You really want me to paint over the chestnut? Are you sure?’” says Shaw. “Yes.”

Consuelo Vanderbilt’s (Alva and William’s child) monochrome red bedroom in the mansion also serves as George Russell’s bedroom in the show.

Features as: The exterior of Sylvia Chamberlain’s house

Rosecliff was built for Nevada silver heiress Theresa Fair Oelrichs, whose father, James Graham Fair, was one of the four partners in the Comstock Lode. The famed architectural firm McKim, Mead & White was commissioned to design the mansion, which was modeled after the Grand Trianon, the garden retreat of French kings at Versailles. The French Baroque Revival gem was designed for entertaining at a grand scale—and it certainly fulfilled its purpose. Fair Oelrichs would become known as one of the three great hostesses of Newport.

The white front facade of this mansion, which recently got a $7.4 million renovation, is a stand-in for Sylvia Chamberlain’s (played by Jeanne Tripplehorn) house. You’ll see it in Episode 7, when Tom Raikes (Thomas Cocquerel) pays a visit.

Features as: The servant kitchen and the real-life Sarah Berwind’s bedroom as parts of the Russell residence

The Elms is a Classical Revival dwelling that was completed in 1901. Architect Horace Trumbauer designed it for the coal baron Edward Julius Berwind, taking inspiration from the 18th-century d’Asnières French château. It also houses an impressive collection of paintings and tapestries, as well as the latest technology of its age.

In The Gilded Age Seasons 1 and 2, The Elms stands in as part of the Russells’ lavish residence, including scenes in the kitchen and Bertha Russell’s bedroom. But it truly steps into the spotlight in Season 3, when it hosts a dazzling ball. And as a long-awaited confession of love looms (we won’t spoil from whom), the opulent Newport mansion proves to be the perfect setting for high drama and high society.

Each guest is granted a cinematic entrance, illuminated by a golden glow that bathes the grand façade—a new addition this season, as electricity is now becoming available. “The house’s interiors would not have been electrified yet, but people were beginning to use it for parties,” Shaw says. “That would mean they would have to have their own generator installed somewhere on the property to light up those lights.” To accomplish this, the crew went to specialty shops that make reproduction bulbs. “The original bulbs were much bigger than what we have in a household today,” Shaw adds. “Some of the lightbulbs we used were 10 inches in diameter, which is how big they were then.”

Features as: The exterior is used for Caroline Astor’s Beechwood house; the boardinghouse is used for a scene with Oscar van Rhijn; and another room serves as Agnes van Rhijn’s bedroom

Completed in 1852, Château-sur-Mer is one of the first grand Bellevue Avenue mansions of the Gilded Age in Newport, built by architect Seth C. Bradford for William Shepard Wetmore. Its interiors were designed by Ogden Codman Jr., who co-authored Edith Wharton’s Decoration of Houses. The mansion was host to many major parties, including the Fête Champêtre, an elaborate country picnic for more than 2,000 guests in 1857, as well as a debutante ball for Edith Wetmore in 1889. Wetmore’s son, George Peabody Wetmore, went on to have a distinguished political career as the governor of Rhode Island and, later, as a U.S. senator.

In The Gilded Age, the home’s exterior was used for Caroline Astor’s (Donna Murphy) Beechwood House. The hallway, dining room, and ballroom were used during Mamie Fish’s (Ashlie Atkinson) doll tea party. Several bedrooms serve as Agnes van Rhijn’s (Christine Baranski) bedroom, Oscar van Rhijn’s (Blake Ritson) bedroom, Anne Morris’s (Katie Finneran) bedroom, and Patrick Morris’s (Michel Gill) office. There’s also a scene filmed at the boardinghouse with Oscar van Rhijn, according to Patch.

Features as: The office of Brook family lawyer Tom Raikes

Hunter House was constructed in 1748 at the harborfront near the north end of the Newport Historic District at a time when the city was a bustling seaport in the British empire. The house was owned by wealthy merchants and overlooked the wharves that serviced their trading ships. It’s one of the finest examples of Colonial architecture in Newport, according to the Preservation Society. Inside, its resident families furnished the house with decorative arts produced by expert craftsmen, much of which has been preserved today. Such a luxurious lifestyle, however, depended upon the labor of enslaved people of African descent; recent research by the Preservation Society has identified at least 13 enslaved individuals who lived and worked at Hunter House. Today’s Hunter House honors their perseverance and incalculable contributions to Newport’s past.

One of the sitting rooms in Hunter House functions as the office of Brook family lawyer Tom Raikes, and is set fictitiously in Doylestown, Pennsylvania.

Features as: The Astor family summer home

Belcourt was completed in 1894 for Oliver Hazard Perry Belmont, a prominent American socialite and coachman who served in the U.S. House of Representatives. Like many of the Newport mansions, the Château-esque structure was completed by architect Richard Morris Hunt; it was designed to mimic the hunting lodge of Versailles, built for Louis XIII of France, but with a special ask to accommodate Oliver’s collection of horses and automobiles. In 1896, Oliver gave the house to his second wife, the former Alva Vanderbilt, who designed the neighboring Newport mansion Marble House. Alva used Belcourt as a gathering place for the elite of society, hosting lavish parties and balls and attracting notable figures in politics, business, and the arts.

In The Gilded Age Season 1, Belcourt shows up in Episode 8 as Mrs. Astor’s lavish new Newport home, which Mrs. Russell sneaks inside and is forced to flee to avoid a less-than-pleasant meetup.

Features as: The social gathering scene where Caroline Astor and George Russell meet

Built in 1867 by prolific Boston tea and china merchant Robert M. Cushing, the Ledges was originally the summer home of the Cushing family. This majestic oceanfront pile is perched on one of Newport’s most desirable peninsulas, overlooking Bailey’s Beach. It was built by architect John H. Sturgis, who designed a large, Stick-style cottage that was equal parts comfort and elegance. Today it features original furnishings, papered walls, and unique artwork, including a studio that was added to the side of the house for Robert’s artistic children.

Today, the estate remains in the hands of the Cushing descendants, who occasionally open the house up to film crews. It was featured a few years ago in the film Evening, starring Glenn Close and Meryl Streep. “Working at the Ledges was pretty extraordinary,” Gilded Age location manager Lauri Pitkus told The Providence Journal, “because I don't think I’ve ever been in a home with spectacular views out of every window.”

This historic house was built in 1864 for John Noble Alsop Griswold, an Old China Trade merchant and diplomat. The house is the work of Richard Morris Hunt; it was one of the earliest buildings in the American Stick style of architecture. The house has a complex roofline with a main mansarded section that is pierced by numerous gable and dormer sections. It boasts a number of balconies sheltered by deep eaves, with gable ends decorated with woodwork. Today, the house is a gallery location for the Newport Art Museum. In the series, the house features in Season 1 Episode 1, and again in Episode 5.

Features as: Mingling with the other elite scene

The International Tennis Hall of Fame and its perfectly preserved grass courts are in the Newport Casino, a National Historic Landmark property. The historic seven-acre estate was commissioned by New York Herald publisher James Gordon Bennett as a social club for the elite summer residents of Newport. The Newport News boasted three days after it opened in July 1880: “It is doubtful if a livelier place can be found.” Today, the space is recognized as the official hall of fame for the sport of tennis globally. Since 1955, 262 distinguished inductees representing 27 countries have been enshrined in Newport.

In the eighth episode of The Gilded Age Season 1, the characters are seen promenading around the piazza that overlooks what is known today as Horseshoe Court. A few characters are even playing tennis. “It was fitting to have that history depicted here, given The Gilded Age’s look into that time period,” the hall of fame’s Megan Erbes tells ELLE DECOR. “Several of our staff members got to work closely with the production team and actors, teaching everything from the history of our property to how to hit a proper forehand.” RI Monthly reported that crews returned to the courts last summer to film Season 2 of the show, so it’s possible that Newport Casino will make another appearance.

Features as: The parlor, library, entryway, and staircase of the Morris house, located on Fifth Avenue in the show; Mr. and Mrs. Morris’s bedrooms were filmed in the Newport mansions

William Smith Slater hired General William Walker to build a home as a wedding gift for his daughter. The result was this historic home, which was completed in 1877. The Victorian house is filled with treasures—the fireplace surrounds, for example, were carved by Charles Dowler, who came to the United States to produce arms for the Civil War. Today, the house is open to visitors and is known as the Clouds Hill Museum.

The Clouds Hill Museum is the only Rhode Island site outside of Newport that was used in the series. The crew scheduled filming for Season 1 in late February of 2021, when a few hundred people were on site during two days of filming, museum director Wayne Cabral told Warwick Online, noting that the taping finished around midnight on the second day of filming (after five days of work to prep the set). Almost all the parlor’s furniture was removed, and the head set designer brought in items sourced from local antiques stores in East Greenwich and North Kingstown, museum president Anne Holst added. The producers were adamant about removing the existing curtains, which were disintegrating, Holst added, so she negotiated to leave the replacements (which cost $10,000) with the museum after filming.

Features as: Visit to the home of Aurora Fane scene

The Gothic Revival–style Lyndhurst Mansion was designed in 1838 by Alexander Jackson Davis. Lyndhurst’s third owner, Jay Gould, is the real-life inspiration for the “new money” character George Russell. “He was fantastically ruthless in business, to an extent that Mrs. Astor would never let him in, he was never allowed into society, and she didn’t let his children in until he was dead,” creator, writer, and producer Julian Fellowes said in a podcast. “But he was an incredibly affectionate father and a very caring and faithful husband…and his children all had these nostalgic memories of Lyndhurst, their house on the Hudson.”

In Season 1 of The Gilded Age, Lyndhurst’s interiors are the home of characters Charles and Aurora Fane (Ward Horton and Kelli O’Hara), who are related to the van Rhijns and part of the “old money” side of society. The Lyndhurst lawns and grounds also doubled as Central Park and the Sheep’s Meadow in the opening sequence. The home boasts a bowling alley that dates back to 1894, which was re-created to be the ferry terminal that Marian Brook (Louisa Jacobson) and Peggy Scott (Denée Benton) take to Manhattan for the first time in Episode 1. And finally, the house’s carriage house was transformed into The New York Globe newspaper offices.

The Belvedere Estate, located between Lyndhurst Mansion and writer Washington Irving’s Sunnyside home, is a century-old mansion on 25 acres in historic Tarrytown. The estate was originally part of Philipsburg Manor, a 90,000-acre property built for Florence and Casper Whitney. “While the mansion dates from the 1920s, it has a much older European feel that could represent something from decades earlier,” says Belvedere Estate sales director Linda Fiorentino, who recalls the massive legwork required to film at the mansion.

“I will never forget the background actors descending from a yellow school bus, fully dressed in period costumes and felt hats,” she says. The Season 1 scene was filmed at the pond at the back of Belvedere Estate, acting as a stand-in for Central Park in the scene when Marian meets Mr. Raikes at Bethesda Fountain in Episode 1. The pond is a man-made feature, and the water level sometimes sinks to a mere foot or two, Fiorentino explains. “Before filming, they filled the pond with roughly 600,000 gallons of water to fill to capacity, giving the illusion of a much larger body of water and to allow them to have pleasure boats filled with passengers on the water,” she says. They also re-created Central Park’s Shakespeare Bridge from rough-hewn logs and brought in trees, shrubs, and foliage to cover the existing link fence because it was too modern.

Features as: The drawing room of wealthy widow Sylvia Chamberlain

Toward the end of the 19th century, Colonel Elliott Fitch Shepard, a lawyer who founded the New York State Bar Association in 1876, purchased more than 500 acres on a hill high above the Hudson River north of Tarrytown. He engaged the renowned architecture firm McKim, Mead & White to design his family’s manor house here. The result was a 75-room Victorian structure that mimicked 18th-century Italian villa architecture. Think: hand-carved mahogany paneling, a grand stairway near the front entrance, high patterned ceilings, and elaborate cornice work. Unfortunately, Shepard died before it was completed in 1893, and it passed into the hands of William Rockefeller and Frank Vanderlip. These men were part of a group (including John Jacob Astor, who died on the Titanic in 1912) who went on to convert the building to the Sleepy Hollow Country Club, which remains a private members-only club today.

Scenes in the drawing room of wealthy widow Sylvia Chamberlain were shot on location here. “The entire room was redressed by our team in the Rococo style,” set decorator Regina Graves told 1st Dibs.

Features as: Part of Mrs. Astor’s residence

Glenview was built in 1876 by John Bond Trevor, a highly successful stockbroker, who engaged New York City architect Charles W. Clinton to build his estate. The mansion was a paragon of luxury and modern comfort, featuring indoor plumbing, gas illumination, and a coal-burning furnace to circulate heat throughout its 37 rooms. The exterior had elements of Victorian Gothic and French Second Empire design, complete with a mansard roof, a gray stone facade constructed of blocks hewn from a Hastings-on-Hudson quarry, and decorative sandstone elements brought from Indiana.

The mansion makes an appearance as Caroline Astor’s residence in Season 1. “They were all so complimentary about Glenview, and of course we did a lot of polishing and waxing,” Laura Vookles, chair of the museum’s curatorial department, told The Rivertown Enterprise. “They focused on our exquisite interiors and filmed in six rooms, including the dining, library, and sitting rooms, and used all but one painting that is normally on view.”

The John Paine Mansion, also known as the Castle, was built in 1896 and was deemed the grandest house in Troy, New York. It has since served as a private residence, a college administration building, a movie set (see: Martin Scorsese’s 1993 drama The Age of Innocence), and, currently, a fraternity house. In the show, one of the bedrooms in the Castle features as Tom Raikes’s office.

Features as: The interior is used as George Russell’s workspace

Hempstead House was built for Howard Gould by architects Hunt & Hunt in 1912 on the north shore of Long Island. The mansion was eventually sold to art mogul Daniel Guggenheim (yes, that Guggenheim). Hempstead House is one of four mansions on the grounds of the Guggenheim Estate—which includes Castle Gould, Falaise, and Mille Fleur. The Tudor-style castle was the embodiment of Gold Coast opulence, a place where the esteemed family hosted grand parties, performances, and exhibitions by gatherings of the elite of the time. To say it is massive would be an understatement: 50,000 square feet, with 40 rooms and a 60-foot-tall entry foyer. In The Gilded Age, the mansion’s interior was used as George Russell’s workspace.

Features as: The late General Henry Brook’s house

Rock Hall Museum was initially an 18th-century plantation. The two-and-a-half-story Georgian-style home was built in approximately 1767 and later restored to its original appearance in the 1950s. In Season 1 Episode 1, the house’s white facade is featured within the first 15 minutes as a backdrop for the late General Henry Brook’s house. Marian, his daughter, is seen walking out of the home on her way to the train station.

The Hart Cluett Museum is a work of Federal architecture designed by Martin E. Thompson in 1827. He was hired by William Howard. Thompson brought the design of the Hart Cluett House back to New York City, where many examples of the 1827 Federal-style townhouse were copied throughout Manhattan. #6994. 6 Antique Trends Designers Are Watching for 2027

Above: The kitchen in designer Phillip Thomas’s Bellport retreat.

Do the words “antique” and “trend” really belong in the same sentence? In many ways, a great antique shop feels like the antidote to trend fatigue: a space where the true must-haves are hidden in dusty corners, with part of the fun in the thrill of the hunt. Yet, as we round out 2026, there’s no denying the current design cycle is casting off perfection for patina. Antiques are en vogue, and top designers are keeping a close eye on the periods, pieces, and yes, trends, that are poised to find new life in 2027.

“The return of interest in antiques reflects a trend towards more personal, unique interiors, and away from strict modern rooms which can often be cool and generic,” says ELLE Decor A-List designer Jamie Drake. This shift paves the way for more playful interactions between pieces, unexpected layering, and unabashed personality.

Other experts second the motion for more character. “I’m seeing clients really drawn to antiques with more to them than meets the eye: pieces that have a bit of humor or whimsy, or pieces with great provenance,” says ELLE Decor Rising Star William Cullum. (He lists a “ridiculously giant butterfly light fixture” from New Orleans, rattan chairs from Bing Crosby’s estate, and a disco ball from Cyndi Lauper’s Bonham’s sale in his list of recent acquisitions—professional and personal.)

Luckily for the amateur antiquer, not all trending vintage styles require a substantial space commitment or glamorous provenance—although an interesting backstory helps. “Antiques are always about a story: where you found them, who owned them, and how that story will continue on after us,” Cullum says.

Ahead, we asked top designers and experts to share the most inspiring antique trends of the moment. From neoclassic mirrors to hand-painted chests paired with modern art, their takes are sure to inform your next estate sale rummage, or inspire you to restyle an old heirloom.

We called mirrored surfaces a major trend to watch for fall 2026, and antique trends have followed suit. Unexpected (or better yet, artfully manipulated) reflections can add rich dimension and movement to any space, but in this category, antiques create a window to the past that’s tough to beat.

“One of the easiest ways to add a sense of history to a room is to include an antique mirror,” says Drake. “Whether you choose a sober and strict neo-classic piece or a more flamboyant baroque one, reflections of the past add depth and sparkle.”

“One trend I’m observing is an uptick in antique dowry chests,” antiques dealer Ali Mahon says. From Pennsylvania Dutch to Swedish Folk and Spanish carved, she’s noticed clients gravitating toward more delicately adorned storage over spare. With thoughtfully mixed furniture (from style to period) becoming more desirable than matching sets, Mahon sees more hand-painted antique surfaces in our future: “With their decorative tulips, leaves, birds, and hearts, dowry chests create a colorful splash in any room.”

ELLE Decor Rising Star Reagan Gail Struble agrees—although delicate designs are not a requirement of the trend, in her book: “Something about a playful wash of color on an awesome old piece of furniture is very alluring, especially when you can see the age and texture of the wood peeking through,” she says. “Hand-painted case pieces add a little punch of charm to any room.”

Luckily, these antiques can be just as functional as they are fashionable. Mahon recommends using a painted chest as a coffee table, at the foot of the bed, or in a space that could benefit from a bit more romance (and a few extra drawers). When styling your find in a modern space, Struble says that contrast is key: try a “chippy Swedish chest of drawers” with an unexpected photograph or painting above it.

According to ELLE Decor A-List designer Nick Olsen, Art Deco pieces are setting the tone for 2027 antique trends—although, if you ask him, they were never out. “I'm particularly inspired by the [art deco influence in the] Gujarat palace in India,” he says. “I'm fascinated by how every element seems to have a wide border: marble floors, carpets, walls of veneer and mohair velvet upholstery.”

According to Olsen, using Art Deco design compositions can do as much for a room as actual pieces from the period. “I like the idea of contrasting borders: solid textures punctuated by smaller graphic prints on furniture and more delicate lighting,” he explains. And while borders are easy to create with paint, paneling, and carpet seaming, Olsen challenges clients to push the Art Deco agenda further by bordering with luxe materials.

Whether you’re new to antiquing or simply trying to add more character to a space, lighting is an excellent place to start. “Lighting is the jewelry of the home, so it’s one of my favorite places to bring in something vintage,” says A-Lister Corey Damen Jenkins. “An Art Deco sconce or an antique brass lantern has a warmth to it that’s hard to find in something brand new.”

Adding to an existing space? Jenkins advises starting small: “Swap one overhead fixture or put a pair of vintage lamps on a console, and you’ll be surprised how much the whole room shifts,” he says. “It’s an easy way to add history without starting over.”

“In light of America’s 250th birthday, Americana in general is in vogue,” says Mahon. Among her clients, the trend has manifested in quilts and textiles, portraits of sea captains, trade signs, and ironstone—culminating in an aesthetic she describes as “elevated country—not corny.”

If you’ve noticed that no particular design period seems to reign supreme among designers’ top antique trend picks, it’s not an accident. Heading into 2027, there’s a strong argument to be made that mixed-period design is more influential than any singular reference.

“I love a room that doesn’t belong to any one decade,” Jenkins says. “A nineteenth-century chest with a 1970s lamp on top, or a Lucite chair pulled up to an antique desk. Those are the pairings that make people stop and look twice.” Drake adds that seemingly out-of-place seating is an easy way to add intrigue. “I love including antique chairs as sculptural notes, contrasted against modern upholstery and tables,” he says.

But when it comes to antiquing, there’s really only so much one can plan for. Letting a great find lead the way often turns out to be a better strategy than arriving to an estate sale with a rigid checklist. The best way to keep up with top designers when it comes to antiquing this year? Find a piece that has something to say, place it where it seems to belong, and then try it where it doesn’t.

You might have heard that being active is a good idea when you have osteopenia or osteoporosis, conditions marked by lower-than-normal bone density. When bone tissue is stressed via physical activity, your body responds by making more bone, helping strengthen the tissue. Exercise also lowers the activity of cells that dissolve and break down old bone. That combo helps preserve or even improve your bone mineral density, regardless of your health status. With colder weather drawing our attention indoors, many of us swap outdoor garden maintenance for caring for our favorite houseplants. But while your spider plant, peace lily, or monstera may be safely tucked away inside, their needs change significantly during autumn and winter.

Reduced daylight, cooler temperatures, and drier indoor air can all affect the health of houseplants, meaning the care routine that worked perfectly in summer could actually do more harm than good during the colder months.

It turns out many of us are unknowingly making a few common mistakes that can leave indoor plants struggling through winter. Here are the habits to avoid, plus the simple swaps that will help keep your houseplants healthy until spring.

You might assume that moving your houseplant closer to a bright window is an excellent idea in winter to allow it to drink in as much sunlight as possible. And while this isn't necessarily a bad move, it just requires a little more thought.

“It’s always a good idea to move them closer to a bright window as darker days hit, but make sure to avoid cold drafts and radiators,” Mark Sage, B&Q's horticulture expert, tells us. “Cold drafts and dry air from radiators can all take their toll on indoor plants, leading to yellowing leaves, rotten roots or even plant fatality.”

Similarly, Sage suggests thinking about where the sun actually shines in your home throughout autumn and winter.

“Where natural light may be limited, consider investing in a plant light to give an extra boost, while rotating larger plants daily can help avoid leggy, awkward growth as they stretch out looking for the sunshine,” he suggests.

When houseplants start to look a little worse for wear and growth begins to slow over winter, it's tempting to step up your care routine. You might find yourself watering more often, tending to leaves more regularly, or even feeding plants in an attempt to encourage new growth.

However, Sage warns that this is often the wrong approach. In fact, during autumn and winter, “sometimes the best thing you can do for your plants is leave them alone!”

He explains: “In a slower growth period, plants generally need less water and feeding, and winter certainly isn’t the time for regular repotting.”

Instead, Sage recommends letting the soil dry out between waterings to help prevent root rot and giving plants time to rest. “It's worth giving them a boost of plant feed ahead of the winter months, but a little goes a long way,” he says. “Give plants their final dose at the end of autumn.”

During summer, when houseplants are actively growing, it's common to spread them throughout your home so you can enjoy a touch of greenery in different rooms. However, according to Sage, this can work against them once temperatures drop.

Instead, he recommends bringing your plants together during autumn and winter to help recreate the conditions many tropical houseplants naturally prefer.

“Give your houseplants a helping hand through winter by clustering them together to create a more humid microclimate, as many of these plants originally come from much hotter parts of the world,” he recommends. “Grouping several varieties close together can help maintain humidity around them, which can be particularly useful when central heating makes indoor air drier.”

While watering may have been your main plant-care task during summer, it's a mistake to neglect cleaning your houseplants over autumn and winter. Just like the rest of your home, plants can gather dust, and that build-up can make it harder for them to make the most of limited daylight.

“During winter, leaves can gather a layer of dust,” says Sage. “Give them a wipe down every one to two weeks with a microfibre cloth to support photosynthesis, and to help them benefit from even the smallest burst of sunlight.”

In fact, Sage suggests combining cleaning and watering occasionally by giving your plants a gentle shower.

“Use lukewarm water to boost humidity and get rid of any pests, but keep an eye on the temperature as if it is too cold you can shock the roots, but too warm and you can scald the leaves.”

#6884. I’m a Run Coach. Here’s Why a Marathon Doesn't Have to Be Your Ultimate Running Goal

Women's Health may earn commission from the links on this page, but we only feature products we believe in. Why Trust Us?

This time of year, after most fall marathons have ended and our feeds have seen weeks of people crossing finish lines at some of the biggest in the world in Berlin, Chicago, and New York, it’s really hard to avoid feeling like you should run a marathon. I personally have posted countless videos of myself on the internet saying, “I’m never doing this again,” and even I’m tempted to go back on my word every November.

To be fair, I love the marathon. I’ve run 12 of them. I’ve run them very, very fast. And I’ve run them very, very slow. My first one changed my life. It gave me the courage to move to New York City and try to figure out what to do after graduating college. Six months later, I became one of the first running influencers, and I’ve spent the 12 years since building a global community, Badass Lady Gang, to help thousands of women belong and redefine their relationship with strength, power, and what it means to be a runner.

But I also feel complicated feelings about the marathon. As a running coach, I’ve watched the same conversation happen online for over a decade. Somewhere along the way, newer runners are told, or quietly absorb, that the marathon is the ultimate finish line. Which makes sense, right? Our brains love order and hierarchy. When you think about what it means to progress in running, it feels logical: going faster and farther are straightforward ways to show you’ve improved. But that’s not how it works. The marathon isn’t the objective ultimate goal. Or always the most fun one. And, it’s definitely not something that every runner needs to chase.

Every year I watch too many runners struggle through the training not because they lack commitment or drive, but because they’re chasing a goal they didn’t fully want or that their life can’t accommodate right now. And honestly, it’s just a really hard distance to master! A fellow coach and pillar in the community, Peter Bromka, put it best: “The marathon owes you nothing.” I hate that he’s right. You spend months killing yourself (metaphorically) for one huge goal, and then anything can happen on the day. It’s too hot. You get hurt despite doing everything right. You have an off day and watch your goals fly out the window.

We’re in the largest running boom the sport has ever seen, and I think it’s largely because we’re lonely. One in five Americans said they always or often felt lonely or socially isolated in a 2018 study by the Kaiser Family Foundation. And the stakes are real: the U.S. Surgeon General has warned that loneliness carries health risks comparable to smoking up to 15 cigarettes a day. Running is one of the few activities that genuinely makes a dent in the loneliness epidemic because pretty much anyone can be a runner (even though it is easier and more accessible for some more than others). You can be fast, slow, old, young—if you’re lacing up your sneakers and moving, you’re a runner. Come marathon season, we also hear stories of the incredible adaptive athletes who cross finish lines with a range of disabilities, too.

Basically, there’s a reason run clubs are hot right now. They give people a way to put their phones down and find a community. The more you show up, the more you see people of all shapes, sizes, ages, fitness levels, and backgrounds crushing distances you thought weren’t possible for someone like you. Before you know it, you’ve signed up for your first half marathon and everyone is asking when you’ll run a marathon. Once you get on that path, it’s hard to stop.

Loneliness isn’t the only thing fueling the boom. We’ve also lost the sense that we control our livelihoods, our schedules, our futures. A marathon is something you choose. It’s one place where you get to call the shots: you set the goal, decide how to train, and define what success looks like, even when curveballs come. It gives you back agency in a world that feels uncontrollable. And in an age when we produce our lives online, it looks great on a feed. It’s badass to train for a marathon, especially when you feel like you have nothing else exciting to share.

So yeah, it’s not just a feeling that everyone’s running a marathon lately. It’s real. Over 1.1 million people applied to London. Chicago saw a 12.2 percent jump in participation from 2023. New York jumped 15.1 percent in the same time period. Races sell out in hours. Getting a bib is almost as hard as finishing 26.2 miles. That’s not an accident.

I work in this industry, and I can tell you a lot of money is spent each year making you feel like you should run a marathon. Last year, New York Road Runners, the nonprofit behind the New York City Marathon, spent $3.8 million on advertising and promotion. And much of that content is built around one image: a runner crossing the finish line, arms overhead, tears in their eyes, sharing how they became the person they always dreamed of being. It works. It makes you believe transformation is possible, and it is. But the marathon industry has weaponized our deep desires to belong, to achieve, to become, and forced us on 26.2-mile slogs across our respective cities.

The result: This overwhelming pressure that everyone needs to get out there and run a marathon—and an exponentially growing number of sign-ups. Never in a million years did I think we’d get to a place where getting into a marathon was almost as hard as running it. But here we are.

I spent a decade being paid by the biggest brands in the world to promote the marathon. I built my business on being vulnerable online and sharing my own transformation through running. So believe me when I say: Not everyone needs to run a marathon.

Before I tell you why you don’t need to run a marathon, let me explain why I first decided to run one. It was never something I envisioned for myself. In my mind, “real” runners ran marathons. (Kooks, if I’m being honest.) Then, I graduated from college with a theater degree and had to start paying off my student loans. My community scattered. And I couldn’t run from the grief I’d suppressed after losing my brother my freshman year of college in a tragic accident.

On Thanksgiving morning in 2012, I started actually running from my problems. Running was brutal and wonderful in a way that left no room for my feelings. It demanded every ounce of attention just to keep putting one foot in front of the other or fall over and pray for a quick end.

I know. Dramatic. But I was the person who made the jokes about only running from the cops. Or a bear. I missed over 70 days of school my senior year ditching PE to avoid running. But desperate times! Running really did feel like a lifeline.

Running gave me structure and something to feel excited about. Becoming a runner gave me something to work toward and confidence when friends and family asked me what my plans were now that I graduated from college. Instead of saying, “I don’t know if I’ll ever work a day in my life doing the thing I love most,” I could say, “I’m training to run a marathon” and feel pride and purpose. They would look at me gobsmacked and impressed. “YOU! Running a marathon? WOAH!” Yeah, I’d think to myself beaming with pride. ME.

I went from couch to marathon in seven months. The running magazines I turned to for advice told me I shouldn’t do what I was doing. “You should run for at least a year,” “Going from couch to marathon shows you don’t respect the distance.” (Those were actual pieces of advice in articles I remember reading when I was training for my first marathon.) But I wasn’t running for a time goal. I felt like I was running for my life. I had to do it.

Training was horrible in the most fulfilling way. This was 2013 and learning to run was very different from what it is now. There were blogs written by former high school or collegiate runners or message boards with the type of runners who told you you didn’t belong if you loved to hate running like I did or ran slow. I didn’t know what I was doing. I made every single mistake you could possibly make training for and then running a marathon.

The biggest? I wasn’t ready. I grabbed a free plan from an app, ramped up my mileage way too fast, and got hurt almost immediately. I ran in shoes a size and a half too small. I didn’t know how to take energy gels (and ran the race with an absurd number of them sandwiched between my two sports bras). I wore nipple guards made for men, terrified my nipples would chafe.

And despite all the mistakes and missteps, running my first marathon saved my life. When I crossed that finish line, I immediately had this sense of if I could do that, there really isn’t anything I can’t figure out how to do.

I’ll never forget watching my first New York City Marathon from the sidelines the year I moved here. We were near the finish, at Columbus Circle, and I knew immediately that I had to run it the next year. I felt so proud to be one of the crazy people who had run a marathon, and so connected to what the runners were going through. There’s nothing like belonging to a group that has shared something so grueling and rewarding. And so, one marathon turned into two and before I knew it, I was a person who ran marathons for fun.

Coaching changed everything. I’ve watched a mom of three in her 40s break two and a half hours in the half marathon after years of believing she’d never be fast enough. I’ve watched countless women who’d been convinced they couldn’t be runners, because of what they endured in PE class, finally feel strong in their bodies while training to run a fast mile. I’ve watched women facing everything from depression to addiction pick up running as a tool and use it to get through the hardest stretch of their lives. And I realized the same joy, transformation, and belonging happen at every distance. There is no hierarchy.

But, it’s more than that. Oftentimes, when we talk about the marathon, we don’t grapple enough with the reasons it may be easier for some. Back in my 20s, the marathon was genuinely easier. Everything is easier in our 20s, even if it doesn’t feel like it at the time. We have bodies that recover much quicker, time that’s actually our own, and energy that feels endless.

I had all of that when I ran my first marathon at 24. I was braver, more naive (a gift!) and had no idea how to sit still in discomfort. I thought movement was the quickest way through, so I threw myself fully into crossing finish lines. (Funny enough, taking a break from marathons taught me what it means to sit in the hurt and actually do the work to heal.) That doesn’t mean that once you hit 30 or 40, your hopes of running a marathon are out the window, but it does mean that as you get older, you should be kinder to yourself if things feel hard or don’t look as shiny as they may seem online.

Which brings me to something we don’t say enough: a lot of the runners we look up to or love following on social media—the ones who are making us feel like we have to be a part of this—are often younger, and what we see is a story they’re producing. When you’re feeling inspired by a creator, or measuring your own training, chip times, or total races against theirs, remember who and what you’re comparing yourself to. This may be someone whose whole job is to run and work out, who has access to endless training and recovery resources, and who isn’t sharing the full story about their highs and lows. Social media literacy matters. Without it, we’re measuring ourselves against a bar that wasn’t built with us in mind.

When runners come to me and are deciding whether to throw their name into a marathon lottery or sign up, here’s what I tell them.

Not pressured. Not because you think it’s the logical next step. Compelled. That pull that screams, “I have to try this,” or “This matters to me more than anything else in the world.”

Marathon training is long and it beats you down, both physically and mentally. As a coach, my job is to help my runners uncover what’s really driving them: their real why. Because your why is the only thing that gets you out the door when you’re tired, sore, and regretting your entire decision. It’s the north star that keeps you lacing up on vacation, when your kid is sick, on a lunch break, before a wedding, on an 89-degree day, when every part of you is done. Without it, you’re just suffering for the sake of suffering.

It really is an unpaid (and expensive) part-time job. It requires hours of running and strength training every week—for months. It requires flexibility in your schedule and will leave you feeling exhausted, hangry, and spread thin for at least 16 to 20 weeks, depending on what your running looked like before you actually started marathon training.

And don’t even get me started on timing. You’re either training in the dead of winter where it’s freezing, dark, and icy when you wake up and get off work or in the summer when you’re juggling summer vacations and brutal heat.

How do you feel about running 15 miles around the Grand Canyon or in Italy? Is that doable? What about the weddings on your travel schedule? What do your weekends look like for the next four to five months? If you sign up for a marathon, they look like a whole bunch of staying in, waking up early to run double-digit miles, and obsessing over the forecast for this weekend’s long run.

Moms. Caretakers. People with busy, unpredictable lives.

I spend half my coaching time talking athletes off the ledge when life throws wrenches in their training and they feel like they’re failing. You end up traveling relentlessly for work and aren’t comfortable running in the dark in a city you don’t know. Or you hate the treadmill and have to do most of your training on one. Your kids get back-to-back-to-back sicknesses and you spend four weeks not able to run because you’re fighting off what they had. A parent ends up in the hospital. A partner gets a diagnosis no one saw coming. Extreme weather, which is becoming a new normal for many of us, makes training complicated or even inaccessible. The list goes on and on. Anything can and will happen during marathon training.

The question isn’t, “How bad do you want it?” That’s not how life works. No excuses? Tell that to a parent who doesn’t have reliable help. It's whether you have the time—real, uninterrupted time—to train the way you envision and the wiggle room to adjust to any and all of the above scenarios.

It’s about so much more than everyone posting their curated, perfect peak weeks.

Influencers know what to say to speak to whatever insecurities or fears you’re feeling. Or what to say to get just enough engagement, or outrage, to get our numbers higher. Running a marathon is big business and most of your favorite influencers are making a lot of money sharing their stories. Sometimes, it honestly feels like exploitation. Which is gross. But I wish more people understood that no one is sharing their actual lives.

The second you start filming yourself, whether you’re aware of it or not, you’re producing. You’re making choices that will resonate or earn engagement. So whether someone is sharing the highlight reel or highlighting everything that’s going wrong, remember that you will never know what’s actually happening.

Keep that in mind if you catch yourself comparing your undertrained or ordinary reality to someone else's seemingly perfect one. And keep it in mind when you relate to someone who says their training has been a train wreck too. I can tell you firsthand that making running interesting and entertaining is very, very hard.

If you struggle with perfectionism, marathon training may feel like you’re riding the struggle bus. But if you can train, show up imperfectly, and work with your perfectionism, look at social media with a grain of salt, and feel proud of what you could do, then you're in for an incredible ride.

I’m here to remind you that not everyone needs to run a marathon to transform their life, find belonging, or to have a great time being a part of something spectacular. I say this as someone who loves, reveres, and had her life changed because of the marathon: It really is just a distance. Real change doesn’t come from running 26.2 miles. It comes from specificity and intention and showing up for yourself, whatever that distance is.

There are so many other goals you can pursue with running that will give you the same result. Have you ever gone all in on a 5K? Or a 10K? Spent 16 weeks working to see how fast you can run a mile? Run the perimeter of your town with a group of friends? Can you train for a half marathon with a friend who has never done it before and be their cheerleader every step of the way? These aren’t less impressive goals. They’re your goals. And they hit just as hard.

Goals are empty without meaning. The marathon is a spectacle that feels like the ultimate test. But taking on the biggest, most difficult challenge in a sport isn't the only place where magic lives. You can find that same feeling in other distances, other challenges, and other ways of pushing yourself. The real work starts when you ask yourself one honest question: "Why does this goal matter to me?"

The marathon is an incredible accomplishment. But there is no hierarchy when it comes to distances and goals. It’s not a ladder. Just because you run a half doesn’t mean that you should run a marathon next. And, you can spend your whole life running and never even think of attempting a marathon.

You get to decide what fills your cup, what challenges you, and how you spend your time and energy. Only you have the power to choose what changes you.

So the next time FOMO creeps in and you feel like you’re missing out if you don’t run a marathon, remember this: You already have everything you need to change your life. You don't need 26.2 miles. You just need to show up for yourself.

Can you run a marathon? Yes. Should you? That’s entirely up to you.
