#6939. Sophos Launches Tool for Shadow AI Defense

Unauthorized artificial intelligence tools and agents are creating security risks for organizations as AI adoption increases, according to cybersecurity company Sophos. In a recent product announcement, the company highlighted the growing use of shadow AI, referring to AI tools and agents operating without formal organizational approval or oversight. The security firm cited research indicating that 69% of organizations suspect or have evidence of unauthorized AI use.

Separate research from Gartner and Cloud Security Alliance found that 82% of organizations discovered shadow AI agents in their environments during the previous year.

According to the security firm, organizations have struggled to identify which AI tools employees use, what information those tools access, and whether their use complies with security policies.

"Organizations are adopting AI faster than they can secure it, and most security teams can't answer three basic questions: which AI tools are running, what data is going into them, and whether that activity lines up with policy," said Kyle Falkenhagen, senior vice president of product management at Sophos.

The company also cited an IDC projection that the number of AI agents could reach 1.3 billion by 2028.

Sophos researchers reported observing attackers use AI agents within a customer's network to build and test nearly 80 malware modules.

The cybersecurity firm announced the general availability of Sophos AI Defense, a security offering designed to identify AI tools, monitor their activity, and enforce organizational security policies.

The product operates through Sophos Fusion, the company's cybersecurity platform, and uses information from devices, networks, identity systems, and browsers.

According to the company, AI Defense can inspect prompts, uploads, and other AI interactions to identify potentially risky activity and help prevent sensitive data exposure.

#6934. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

For more information, go to the Microsoft blog.

#6932. 12 12 12 Shakes Up the Tired Heist Formula with One Great Idea

Can the Anthony Mackie-led crime drama pull it off?

“A high-asset robbery, professionally conceived, is twelve months in planning, twelve hours in execution,” says Anthony Mackie’s character, FBI special agent Jack Strickland, at the start of the new Apple TV crime drama, 12 12 12. “A bank robber who is not caught within twelve days is not caught at all.”

It’s an amazing premise—honestly, one of the best I’ve heard in a long time. The first episode takes the viewer one month into planning the heist, one day into pulling off the heist, and one hour into their attempted escape. Then, episode 2 will move along to two months, two days, and two hours. Neat, right? But the big question for cocreators Jim Keeble and Dudi Appleton’s time-shifting heist series remains the same as any seemingly impossible bank job: Do they have the right team to pull it off?

From the two-episode premiere—now available to stream on Apple TV—I’d say we’re off to a very strong start. 12 12 12’s opening scene pays homage to Ocean’s 11’s Shaobo Qin when a contortionist unfolds his body and climbs out of a large safety deposit box. It’s one of the most genius ideas I’ve seen in a heist story of the last few years—I mean, talk about a man on the inside—especially in an overcrowded field of complex heist series. Remember Netflix’s Kaleidoscope, which allowed viewers to watch the story starting on any episode in the series? Well, 12 12 12 is far more than just a gimmick.

The story follows FBI special agent Jack Strickland (Mackie), who has tracked down one of the world’s best bank robbers, Paul Galvin (Jamie Dornan), to Zurich, Switzerland. Galvin and his unruly brother Bobby (Jack Kesy, who was last seen playing Robbie Pedergrast’s late brother on Task), made an enemy of Strickland when the FBI agent’s partner was killed as she attempted to arrest them during a major heist in Newark, New Jersey.

Now, Strickland has a personal vendetta against the man. He throws his philosophy that you’ll never catch a bank robber after twelve days of the robbery out the window—which is fine because the creators fabricated the whole “12 12 12” FBI rule anyway just to provide episodic framing for the series. (Maybe it resets now that Galvin is planning another heist?) Either way, 12 12 12 does a great job of making sure you never get lost in the three timelines.

Strickland’s white whale is also far from a one-note character himself. He’s a man following in his father’s footsteps to get out of a life of poverty, which would be easier if his brother Bobby wasn’t such of a wild card who can’t be trusted unattended for a single second.

As it turns out, Bobby gambled away all their money from the Newark heist, including Paul’s half. So his brother’s debtors force Paul back into a life of crime under threat of violence to his new lady love in France, his father-in-law, and their unborn child. It’s also quickly revealed that our contortionist is just one of the interesting members of Paul’s team, who are all tasked by Bobby’s mysterious benefactors with robbing a Zurich bank of 800,000 Swiss francs all owned by the Russian mafia.

From the first two episodes released so far, the 12 12 12 team seems like it has the right men for the job. The timeline-shifting format allows the series to do much more than simply break the action to provide a little backstory. As we move from months ahead to days after, and vice-versa, we also switch perspectives that allow for Paul and Jack to trade who is the hero and who is the villain.

Jack can be both the righteous FBI agent pursuing the bank robbers in the present and a man clearly far too hellbent on revenge in the past. The same goes for Paul, whose team blackmails, honeypots, and occasionally murders throughout the heist, while his backstory paints him as a good man who was forced down a dark road.

Where 12 12 12 falls short, at least so far, is in the script. Kari Skogland (who previously worked with Mackie on The Falcon and the Winter Soldier) directs the two-part premiere handily enough, but Keeble and Appleton’s writing is very formulaic. Scripts don’t always matter when you’re working with amazing characters like the contortionist lockpick. True Detective: Night Country’s Kali Reis is also on the team. Her mission is to hack into the subway system so that they can make their escape on public transit, which, of course, goes wrong. But when you have so much dialogue doubling as exposition in between the action, it feels like everyone’s conversations are more for the viewer than they are for each other.

For now, I’m hopeful that all the kinks are worked out once we move past the who, what, and why of 12 12 12’s opening episodes. Given the gimmick, and the fact that the series is only eight episodes instead of twelve, I’m excited to see just what throws the entire concept of the series into chaos.

#6930. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

According to a survey by data and AI services provider Further, enterprises are reporting benefits from artificial intelligence even as their data preparation and oversight processes remain incomplete.

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

Further found that 37% of respondents' organizations had paused, scaled back, or abandoned an underperforming AI initiative in the previous 12 months. Among that group, data quality or availability was the most frequently cited main reason, at 24%.

Meanwhile, 29% reported completing a formal data audit and remediation plan. The research also placed 69% of organizations in the exploring, experimenting, or implementing stages of adoption.

Further distinguishes data prepared for conventional reporting from data prepared for AI. Its explanation emphasizes data origins and documentation, along with access controls that support ongoing evaluation and governance.

For readers assessing their own readiness, the practical issue is whether data supports the requirements of a particular application. A collection suitable for a dashboard may need additional preparation before it can reliably support an AI system.

Seventy-seven percent reported a positive return on AI investment. However, only 3% said their organizations had a documented AI-audit process.

The findings measure different things: perceived financial benefit and the existence of a repeatable oversight process. Neither substitutes for the other.

"In reality, confidence without a documented process is exposure," said Kierra Dotson, associate director of AI strategy and governance at Further. "Closing that gap doesn't take new technology; it requires an auditable governance process."

#6925. Researchers Find AI Is Accelerating Vulnerability Discovery

AI-powered vulnerability research is contributing to a major increase in security updates, raising concerns that IT teams could fall behind as patching demands grow, according to cybersecurity company ThreatDown.

In a recent blog post, the company stated that in September 2026, vendors addressed 998 common vulnerabilities and exposures (CVEs) around Patch Tuesday, compared with 96 during the same period last year. In addition, the three-month rolling average of patched Linux kernel vulnerabilities tripled year over year, while Google's Chrome browser experienced a 36-fold increase.

The researchers point to advanced AI models, including Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a major factor behind the increase in vulnerability discovery.

Introduced in April 2026, Mythos Preview is being used through Project Glasswing, an initiative involving Microsoft, the Linux Foundation and other organizations to identify vulnerabilities in critical software.

While AI could ultimately make software more secure, the cybersecurity company warns that vulnerability discovery is accelerating faster than organizations can deploy fixes.

IT teams must still evaluate patches, test compatibility and schedule updates without disrupting critical business operations.

The challenge is particularly significant for organizations already struggling with patch management.

The cybersecurity vendor cites Verizon's 2026 Data Breach Investigations Report, which found that 35% of known actively exploited vulnerabilities remained unpatched after 28 days in 2025.

Ryan Powell, vice president of IT and security at ThreatDown, said automated patching systems can help organizations manage the growing workload. However, additional patches still require more testing and validation, increasing operational overhead and the risks associated with frequent system changes.

The report also highlights the risks of vulnerabilities in widely used software components such as OpenSSL, embedded in applications, network devices, and security products.

Unlike standalone applications, these components often depend on downstream vendors to release updates before organizations can deploy fixes.

The security firm's analysis points to CVE-2022-0778, an OpenSSL vulnerability patched in March 2022, as an example. Some affected products did not receive updates until several months later.

As AI accelerates vulnerability discovery, these dependencies could delay remediation and extend organizations' exposure to known security flaws.

ThreatDown's findings illustrate a growing challenge for enterprise cybersecurity: AI is making vulnerabilities easier to discover but fixing them remains a complex and time-consuming process.

#6919. IDC Report Finds Significant Gap Between AI Spending and Measurable Financial Returns

Nearly 70% of organizations report a significant gap between artificial intelligence spending and measurable financial returns, according to a recent survey by IDC.

The research firm's 2026 Worldwide AI Economics and Cost of Ownership Survey found that 69% reported such a gap, while only 15% had achieved an AI return on investment exceeding 100%.

In a separate May 2026 survey, 73% of respondents identified excessive AI costs as a significant risk.

IDC highlighted the findings in an October 20 analysis examining what enterprise customers expect from AI technology vendors.

Enterprise customers are placing greater emphasis on measurable business outcomes when evaluating AI products, according to IDC.

The firm identified three priorities for vendors: demonstrating financial value, addressing governance and security requirements, and building support beyond IT departments.

The analysis also highlighted the gap between initial agentic AI deployments and broader adoption. Although 80% of organizations have started deploying agentic AI, only 17% are using it across the enterprise.

#6906. Are We Doomed to Get Cheated On?

At this point, being cheated on feels like an inevitable part of the modern dating experience. Every other week, it seems, another man with a wife, a public-facing career, and absolutely no sense of self-preservation is getting caught with a woman who is decidedly not his wife. I feel as though every time I look at the news or scroll through TikTok, I find myself wondering if my own partner has a secret second phone.

As entertaining (and occasionally horrifying) as these scandals are, they've helped create a rather bleak narrative about modern relationships: Everybody cheats, men in particular, and trusting someone is basically asking to get your heart broken.

“When a cheating scandal goes viral, millions of people watch it very often for the shock and may presume everyone is doing it,” Seema Sharma, LMFT tells Cosmopolitan. “The numbers, however, don't back that fear up.”

So why does it feel like everyone is cheating?

Cheating isn't nearly as universal as the internet would have you believe.

According to a 2025 analysis of General Social Survey data by the Institute for Family Studies, 17 percent of ever-married Americans ages 25 to 54 reported having had sex outside their marriage in the 1990s, compared with 13 percent in 2021 and 2022.

Of course, there are things to consider here: Infidelity isn’t just limited to marriage, and of course, this all depends on what people are willing to share. Because the survey focuses on sexual infidelity within marriage, it doesn't capture every situationship betrayal, secret DM, or emotional affair happening in every kind of relationship.

A 2025 YouGov survey found that roughly one-quarter of American adults admitted to having engaged in sexual activities with someone other than their main romantic partner without that partner's consent.

But just because cheating happens—which we were well aware of already—doesn’t mean it's practically guaranteed when you get into a relationship.

“What I often see is the cost of the fear itself,” Sharma says. “People go into dating braced for betrayal. They check phones, they hold back, and sometimes they leave first so they can't be left.”

And this collective obsession with catching cheaters might be making it harder to actually enjoy the relationships we're in.

I don’t like to point fingers... but it’s hard not to when it comes to cheating. This is because men have traditionally reported higher rates of extramarital sex than women. But the gap is getting smaller.

According to the Institute for Family Studies analysis, approximately 21 percent of men who were ever married (ages 25 to 54) reported having cheated in the 1990s, compared with just 11 percent in 2021 and 2022. Meanwhile, women's reported infidelity rates remained relatively stable, hovering around 14 percent in the most recent period. Again, this only paints the picture for married couples.

While men and women may sometimes describe their reasons for cheating differently, the motivations aren't necessarily as gendered as we'd like to believe.

"Underneath, I hear the same things from both,” Sharma says. “They wanted to feel wanted, or seen, or authentic in their relationship, but didn't.”

Speaking as someone who has been cheated on, I know firsthand how easy it is to start wondering what you did to push your partner to betray you. But it’s important to remember that cheating isn’t always a symptom of a miserable relationship. Research shows that 56 percent of men and 34 percent of women who had affairs described their marriages as happy.

"Often an affair is more about the person and not the relationship,” Sharma explains. “Someone loves their partner and still goes looking for a version of themselves they feel they lost.”

Of course, understanding someone's motivation for cheating isn't the same thing as excusing it. Feeling unfulfilled, insecure, or disconnected might explain a person's behavior, but it doesn't make betraying their partner inevitable.

Given that Gen Z has practically turned relationship surveillance into a competitive sport—checking Instagram follows, analyzing likes, investigating suspicious Venmo transactions—you might assume younger people are particularly prone to infidelity.

But just 12 percent of Americans ages 18 to 29 reported ever engaging in sexual activities with someone else without their partner's consent, compared with 29 percent of adults ages 45 to 64. Of course, older people have had more years and relationships in which to potentially cheat, so that doesn't necessarily mean Gen Z is inherently more faithful. But it does seem that younger generations are changing how they think about infidelity.

"For older generations, cheating mostly meant sex. Younger people are dealing with DMs, likes, dating apps that never got deleted, and exes who are one click away," Sharma says.

A 2024 analysis published by the Institute for Family Studies, found that 76 percent of Americans considered a secret, in-person emotional relationship cheating, while 72 percent said the same about a secret online emotional relationship.

The upside, according to Sharma, is that younger couples may be more willing to discuss these boundaries rather than assuming they're on the same page.

"Younger couples are much more willing to sit down and define what counts," she says—which to me personally, feels like a step in the right direction despite all of the bad press relationships have been getting.

In a perfect world, our partner would never betray us. In a semi-perfect world, we would at least easily detach or leave the second that they do. But as we all know, we live in neither.

Sure, statistically speaking, infidelity isn't exactly great for a relationship's longevity. The 2025 Institute for Family Studies analysis found that 52 percent of ever-married adults ages 25 to 54 who reported having cheated were currently divorced or separated, compared with 15 percent of those who hadn't cheated. (Of course, this data doesn’t establish that infidelity directly caused those divorces.)

Five years later, 43 percent of couples whose infidelity had been revealed were divorced, compared with 80 percent of couples whose infidelity remained secret.

Those numbers sound dramatic, but the study included only 19 couples who experienced infidelity, so we can't assume the same percentages apply to all relationships. Still, the researchers found that couples who remained married after infidelity reported levels of relationship satisfaction and stability comparable to couples who hadn't experienced an affair.

Now, this isn’t me condoning you staying with a cheater. But we each have to determine for ourselves what we’re willing to work through and forgive.

Sharma says that three things matter most for couples who are able to move past infidelity: "The affair has to be over. The person who cheated has to be willing to answer hard questions for a long time without getting defensive. And both people have to want to understand how they got there."

That doesn't mean rebuilding trust is easy—or that anyone is obligated to forgive a partner who has betrayed them.

"Recovery is slow. I tell couples to expect a couple of years, not a couple of months," Sharma adds.

And while it's comforting to know that some relationships can recover, it's equally important to recognize that leaving after infidelity is a completely valid choice. Nobody owes someone a second chance simply because reconciliation is possible.

Short answer? No. Longer answer? Unfortunately, there's no magical way to guarantee that someone will never betray your trust. But that doesn't mean you should spend your entire relationship preparing for the worst.

For starters, Sharma cautions against becoming obsessed with identifying potential cheaters. Sure, sudden secrecy, emotional withdrawal, or defensiveness might warrant a conversation, but none of those behaviors automatically means your partner is being unfaithful.

"The signs that hold up are changes, not single behaviors,” she explains. “Any of those can also mean stress, depression, or trouble at work.”

And if you've reached the point of regularly checking your partner's phone, tracking their location, or conducting a full FBI investigation every time they follow someone attractive on Instagram, the relationship might already have a trust problem worth addressing.

“If you find yourself playing detective, that tells you something about the relationship no matter what you find,” Sharma says. “A direct conversation will get you further than a search through their messages.”

Ultimately, the research paints a much less apocalyptic picture of modern relationships than the internet does. Cheating is real, it can be devastating, and it's common enough that we shouldn't dismiss people's concerns. But it isn't inevitable, despite how much our algorithm may have us believe otherwise. While the headlines might make it seem like monogamy is on its deathbed, there's little evidence that we're suddenly living through an unprecedented cheating epidemic.

“Faithfulness is less about finding the one perfect person who would never do it and more about what two people build,” Sharma says.

This means communicating about boundaries, addressing resentment before it festers, and choosing partners who demonstrate honesty and accountability.

“If you walk into every relationship expecting betrayal, you'll protect yourself so well that no one can get close,” Sharma says. “That's a lonely way to live, and it can be worked on.”

So no, we're not all doomed to get cheated on. We might, however, be doomed to hear about every celebrity affair, influencer breakup, and suspicious Instagram like for the rest of eternity. And frankly, that's exhausting enough.

#6892. Enterprise AI Spending Outpaces Adoption Growth, Report Finds

Enterprise AI spending is growing faster than the number of organizations paying for it, according to a new Menlo Ventures report that points to a shift from buying access for employees to financing work performed by agents.

The venture capital firm's 2026 State of Enterprise AI report estimates deployment spending reached a $176.8 billion annualized run rate by September, up from $33 billion in 2025. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%.

A larger bill can reflect broader use inside an organization, but it does not independently establish that the additional work is useful or profitable.

Menlo's methodology describes revenue figures as annualized run rates. The $176.8 billion estimate therefore should not be read as $176.8 billion already spent during the first nine months of 2026.

The report also distinguishes unusually heavy spenders from the typical respondent. Average reported spending reached 4.7 times the prior year's level, while the median reached 2.2 times. That gap suggests a smaller group of large spenders drove the average substantially.

The practical implication is that an industry growth figure may be a poor benchmark for an individual organization's budget. Buyers need to understand their own workload growth and the results it produces.

"Agents are changing the economics of software," Menlo principal Derek Xiao said in the firm's announcement.

Menlo reports that agents account for 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, a service providing access to multiple models. That measures activity on one platform, rather than all enterprise AI use. Token consumption also measures processing volume, rather than completed business outcomes.

An agent that makes repeated attempts can consume more resources without delivering proportionately more value. For procurement teams, the relevant comparison is consequently the cost of completing acceptable work, including the review it requires.

Menlo estimates that Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google capture 69% of enterprise AI deployment spending. That measure is distinct from their shares of enterprise language-model API spending, a narrower category. The figures should not be used interchangeably.

The report draws on a Morning Consult survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers responsible for more than $4 billion in AI spending. Market totals are modeled estimates informed by surveys, public filings, and third-party reports. Menlo discloses investments in Anthropic and OpenRouter.

A separate announcement from Information Services Group describes buyers putting more emphasis on the economics and reliability of production deployments. ISG says ongoing security and observability work remains necessary as organizations scale.

That provides a useful distinction between market demand and business value. Suppliers can generate growing revenue while customers still determine which deployments justify the cost.

For enterprise leaders, the new spending estimate strengthens the case for examining usage beyond license counts. It leaves the harder purchasing question with the customer: How much worthwhile work did the increased spending actually buy?

#6817. This Unexpected French Antique Is Suddenly Making a Comeback, and You’ve Probably Seen It Before

The desire to preserve mementos from a wedding day transcends centuries and cultures. Today's brides still hang onto their wedding dresses, dry their bouquets, and spend a large portion of their budgets on photography. But in 19th- and 20th-century France, the art of preserving wedding-day memories took the form of globes de mariée, also known as marriage globes.

These ornate, shrine-like displays primarily showcased the bride's head crown and other keepsakes from the big day, and the additional pieces inside symbolized the couple's dreams for their life together. The cloche protected these special items from dust back then and still today.

Here's a closer look at the history and allure of globes de mariée, plus tips for sourcing and styling these gorgeous antiques.

Chris Featherston is the senior vice president of product acquisition at Austin Auction Gallery, and he and his family have been dealing in French antiques for more than 30 years. In the last couple of years alone, they've sold more than 150 globes de Mariée for an average of $400 to $600 each.

But this definitely wasn't always the case. Featherstone notes that the demand for these antiques has risen significantly in the past five years alone.

"In the early days of our business, most of what we sold came from house clearances in France," he recalls. "These globes were ubiquitous. At that time, we felt lucky to be able to sell them for $30 to $40 each."

Anyone with a strong appreciation for historic relics can see the appeal of these antiques. For instance, Mick and Paige Whitcomb have been collecting globes de mariée for nearly a decade, and they currently own 15. Their interest in these types of treasures is reflected in their careers. Mick creates sculptural lighting from architectural and industrial salvage dating back to the 1800s through his business, Artifacts by Nomad. Paige is a tintype photographer who captures staged scenes featuring antiques from the 1800s (including her globes de mariée) using a 19th-century large-format studio camera.

"While traveling in France for a wedding anniversary, we were first exposed to globes de mariée at the Marché aux Puces antique market in Paris," Mick says. "While we were initially caught by the beauty of the objects, after hearing their history and connection to French marriages, it felt like a beautiful way of commemorating our anniversary and trip to Europe."

Due to the highly custom nature of globes de marriée, each one is a unique find. However, Featherston notes that most contain a few similar components. For starters, the bride's headpiece often rests on a red cushion, and it typically shows off delicate beeswax orange blossoms that symbolize purity and virginity.

Most also contain small mirrors, which carried different meanings depending on the shape. For example, diamond-shaped mirrors symbolize how many children the couple wished to have, and rectangular mirrors stood for the length of time the couple knew each other before their engagement.

You'll also often find a wreath embellished with brass ornaments, usually flowers, birds and tree leaves. These symbolize love, a long-lasting marriage, and happiness.

"The globes de marriées integrate beautiful symbolism in every piece, and I find them fascinating," Paige says. "Each globe is so distinctive, and tells the story of the couple that created it. You can surmise the couple's hopes and dreams by minutiae tucked away behind the dome for safekeeping. I find very intimate details incorporated into the displays, such as a prayer card of a favorite saint, to photos of the bride and groom. I have even found hair and teeth, transforming the antique into a memento mori as well."

Mick has noticed that sourcing these antiques is considerably more difficult in the United States than in Europe. But with a little patience and determination, it is possible.

"Because of the glass domes, I think fewer importers are brave enough to bring them over," he says. "Despite this, we have found the occasional gem in the wild." In the States, he's had the most luck sourcing them from design-forward antique dealers, online auction sites and even prop houses that have just wrapped up a period film project.

Since there is a constant demand for these pieces, Featherston tries to feature several in every sale at Austin Auction Gallery.

Globes de mariée can add a formal and traditional flair to any space, and they even mix well with more modern accents.

"One of my favorite ways to showcase the globes de mariée is to mix them with a piece of contemporary art," Paige says. "I love to create a harmony between our collections of antiques and artwork from artists that are living and working today."

For example, Mick and Paige have styled two of their favorite globes de mariée with the piece "The Gardener" by working artist L.J. Eidolon. "I find collecting modern works that have a historic feeling to them helps create balance between the multiple centuries displayed together in one scene," Paige says.

Here, interior designers Paige Kelly and Lindsey Walsh share the window treatment fixes they turn to again and again, along with a few clever ideas you may not have considered.

If a room is short on vertical space, Kelly recommends mounting your curtain rod a few inches from the top of the wall, rather than right above the window. “Getting the drapery high and wide is going to make a room feel bigger,” she says.

Tall panels draw the eye up, but make sure they are long enough to skim the floor. “Otherwise, they look like high-water pants, and it just looks wrong,” warns Walsh.

TWOPAGES Liz Linen Pleated Drapes are a full-length option with a tailored pleat that gives the finished look a little extra polish.

Walsh points to “pattern drenching” as a way to make a space feel more cohesive and help soften the edges of an oddly placed window or render an awkward proportion less noticeable. When you carry the same pattern across the walls and window treatments, the window becomes part of a larger visual composition rather than a feature that stands out for the wrong reasons.

Walsh notes that TWOPAGES can work with paper-backed fabric to allow the same material to cover both the walls and windows. A wallpaper installer can apply the fabric to the walls, and a window treatment workroom will install it as drapery or shades, carefully aligning the pattern repeat so the two applications meet seamlessly.

Increasing the fullness of drapery panels can trick the eye so that windows appear more substantial. “You can make yours look wider by using the stack back required for a wider window,” says Walsh. “The panel fabric may just be covering a lot of the wall, but it will look like there’s a window there.”

If the window placement is part of the problem, Kelly suggests extending the curtain rod beyond the window and using a one-way draw. “Gathering a drapery stack to one side feels intentional, and can help balance an off-center window,” she says.

In rooms where you don’t need full blackout, like kitchens and bathrooms, cafe curtains—which hang in the middle of a window along the check rail—are a great solution. “They cover your neighbor’s dirty yard but you still get to see the sky,” says Kelly. TWOPAGES also sells a variety of ready-to-hang cafe curtains, along with the necessary hardware in a gold or black finish.

Long panels aren’t always practical, particularly when a window sits above a radiator, cabinetry, or a piece of furniture. In those situations, Walsh often turns to roman shades. They’re a streamlined way to introduce fabric and softness without taking up wall space.

For a more decorative look, Walsh loves adding a lambrequin, a fixed fabric-covered frame around the window, with a functional shade tucked beneath it. “It’s a great fix when the interior architecture gets in the way of having the kind of window treatment you want,” she says.

TWOPAGES carries a wide range of shade materials—from linen blends and embroidered cottons to bamboo and woven wood—and offers every fold and lift type possible, so you can find the exact form and function to best fit your space.

Walsh likes to use drapery beyond traditional windows, especially if a room feels stark or echoey because of finishes such as marble, glass, or tile. A portière—a curtain hung in a doorway or cased opening—can create a sense of separation between spaces, soften an entryway, or help buffer cold air near an exterior door.

“Even if they’re purely decorative, drapery and window treatments absorb sound and provide texture and visual interest,” adds Kelly.

The smallest details can make the biggest difference, even if custom treatments are not in your budget. Instead of a traditional curtain rod with finials on each end, which can look dated, Walsh advises using a French return rod, which is mounted on the wall and curves outward. This allows the end of the panels to sit flush against the wall while still creating space for a stack back.

“A French return rod is by far the most elegant way to fake it and make your drapes look professional,” says Walsh. But stay away from grommets—“they are a dead giveaway you did it yourself,” she says—and use rings with clips instead.
