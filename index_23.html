#6888. 27 Charming Christmas Towns That Look Like a Real-Life Holiday Movie

Think twinkling lights, towering trees, and Santa-themed bike rides.

The holiday season is in full swing, with many halls and houses already decked to impress in garland and fairy lights. But as you wrap gifts, prepare to host, and begin marathoning holiday movies, it can feel like the joyous season is coming to an end much too soon. We can't blame you if you're still wanting more from this merry time of year, and we've come up with a solution. There are plenty of beautiful Christmas towns across the country to fill your heart with peace, joy, and nostalgia—you just have to know where to look.

These towns are more than just beautifully decorated—they’re truly immersive, offering experiences from Santa-themed bike rides to orchestrated light shows and winter bar crawls. Below, we've rounded up the 27 most charming Christmas towns from all over the country, so no matter where you live, you'll be close enough to find a bit of extra cheer this year.

This picturesque mountain town serves as the perfect holiday backdrop for more reasons than just the ever-present blanket of fluffy white snow. Each year, the valley is transformed into a Christmas town with seasonal theatrical performances, Gunnison’s Night of Lights, a tree lighting ceremony, and even an ice sculpting event.

Ever since the formation of this city on Christmas Eve in 1741, Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, has been rooted in the holiday spirit. From Christmas markets to historical tours to horse-drawn carriage rides, there's no doubt this town won't feel magical. And make sure to stop by the historic Sayre Mansion, pictured here, to check out their elegant holiday decor.

Annapolis is known as one of the best cities during the holiday season thanks to its winter weather and historic downtown. If you're lucky enough to visit this Christmas town, make sure you spend time bargain shopping until midnight, checking out the spectacular light display in Sandy Point State Park, and viewing the Yacht Club Light Parade.

Chicago is exceptionally beautiful during the Christmas time. From the seemingly endless strings of lights to the Christkindlmarket, which is free to enter and so charming, the Windy City has many offerings during the holiday season, making it one of the best Christmas towns to visit. Plus, you don't want to miss ice skating or any Christmas-themed museum exhibits.

If you want to do something Christmas-y almost every single day of December, then Newport, Rhode Island, might just be the place to go. To create the perfect coastal Christmas, this town offers a month-long celebration that's been going strong for 54 years. From holiday bike rides to Medieval festivals to winter afternoon teas, there's so much to do in this Christmas town.

You'll feel as if you've traveled back to Charles Dickens's time when you walk through Franklin's historic Christmas festival. Characters from A Christmas Carol and Oliver Twist come to life throughout the weekend-long festivities. Roam the Victorian Village and try Victorian-era Christmas treats like sugar plums while listening to carolers.

A classic ski town, Park City offers Christmas markets, festive decorations, and a Christmas Eve parade lit by torches—plus spectacular views of the mountains are around every corner.

In the heart of Virginia horse country, you'll find one of the most festive Christmas towns. Cheer on the town's hunting hounds while sipping hard cider and wander the streets in search of vintage Christmas ornaments.

During Kennebunkport's annual Christmas Prelude, the town puts on a very New England celebration: You can watch Santa Claus arrive by lobster boat, go on seashore holiday trolley rides, and enjoy wine tastings and lobster bakes. Not to mention, the town is covered in Christmas lights and garlands that make it the perfect festive photo backdrop.

For a dreamy and unique experience, head to Santa Fe. One of the most anticipated celebrations is the Canyon Road Farolito Walk, which is when thousands of farolitos—luminaries made of paper bags, sand, and tea candles—cast a glow on the gardens and old buildings that line the road. You also can get your white Christmas fix by visiting Ski Santa Fe.

As one of the oldest towns in the country, Portsmouth is a charming New England destination that celebrates the holidays with a Vintage Christmas Celebration. Visit the historic village of Strawberry Banke or wander around the charming shopping districts in this historic Christmas town.

Get a Hallmark movie feel while experiencing a taste of Denmark in California's Solvang village, located in Santa Ynez Valley wine country. The destination hosts Julefest, a month-long holiday celebration with live entertainment, a parade, and a night market.

Koziar's Christmas Village in Bernville is one of the top attractions in Pennsylvania. With more than a million lights adorning buildings, barns, and the landscape—and a reflective lake that doubles the beauty—it's hard to beat the town's display.

This Bavarian-style village is charming any time of year! December festivities include a display of Santa costumes from all around the world, a gingerbread house competition, carolers, and plenty of photo ops.

Of course, New York City isn't a small Christmas town, but it is extra magical during the holidays. From the massive tree at Rockefeller Center to the various Christmas markets in the city parks with ice skating, shops, and more, you can't beat the Big Apple's holiday spirit.

Nicknamed the Lighted Poinsettia Capital of Texas, this town brings major holiday spirit every year. Visit during December when the lighted trail in Comanche Trail Park is aglow all month long. Check out Hotel Settles for its elegant Art Deco lodging.

During the holiday season, each home in Historic Williamsburg lights one candle, a tradition that dates back to the Revolutionary War era. The cobblestone streets and colonial holiday traditions such as caroling by torchlight make this destination a must, especially for history buffs.

Christmas without the cold? We've got you covered! In addition to abundant sunshine and festive holiday decor, you'll also have plenty of history to explore in St. Augustine, America's oldest city.

Want to take a ride on the Polar Express? Visit Durango during the holidays. The historic mining town offers a Polar Express–inspired trip that your kiddos will flip over.

#6871. The Inconvenient Truth About the Electricity AI Requires

AI companies are facing political backlash, but they’re staring down an even bigger problem as they look to take over the world.

He-Man liked to exclaim: “I have the power!” AI should be so lucky.

When it comes to the wielders of artificial intelligence, and the data centers needed to fuel it, language and numbers become inescapably extreme: trillions in investment, gigawatts of electricity, the extinction of the human race. It’s almost cartoonish, until you get down to some brass tacks: The power is not there to fuel these dreams of the would-be Masters of the Universe.

“The U.S. grid is not designed for large data-center loads,” says Anubhav Arora, senior director of global infrastructure at Fitch and a coauthor of a recent paper the ratings agency published on data centers. “Securing reliable, sufficient power is the single greatest bottleneck in data-center development,” the paper’s authors write. (Esquire and Fitch share the same parent company, Hearst.)

In the U.S.—which is by far the world leader in data centers—electricity demand from the facilities could reach up to 649 terawatt-hours (TWh) a year by 2030, according to a 2026 Berkeley Lab analysis, or roughly 12 percent of all U.S. electricity consumption. Powering New York City for an entire year requires one tenth that energy.

Meeting global demand for compute could require nearly $7 trillion in capital investment by 2030, including $5.2 trillion for AI-related data centers, according to McKinsey estimates. Estimates from Goldman Sachs Research say that Europe will need to invest more than $3.5 trillion on its grid in the next ten years to cope with increased demand. (China looks to be fine, but it is slow to the data-center boom.)

And the problem is not just a lack of juice. “The actual grid infrastructure . . . is also basically limited in terms of transmission capacity, and the interconnection process is a little bit outdated,” says Arora. That grid—the network of transmission lines, transformers, and power plants that create and distribute electricity to hundreds of millions of users—is constrained, and the interconnection backlog is massive. Put another way: Not only is the shop running low on milk, but the line to get inside is stretching around the block. “You could build a data center in one and a half to two years, but to get interconnection, it takes five to ten years,” Arora says.

According to Cleanview, a company that tracks U.S. power infrastructure, there are currently 1,454 operating data centers, as well as up to 578 under construction and 2,663 proposed. Companies like Alphabet, Meta, and Anthropic are scrambling. With that scramble, there is money to be made. PJM, the largest power-grid operator in the United States, has been trying to devise a new way to accommodate the data-center boom. Its proposed “Bring Your Own New Capacity” (or BYONC) framework would have allowed new data-center projects to buy energy in the form of a battery and add it to the grid. But in October, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission rejected that portion of PJM’s proposal, sending the grid operator back to the drawing board.

Important here is what kind of data centers we’re talking about. Of the more than 3,200 data centers proposed globally, 800 of them are hyperscalers. These are the biggies. The campuses. Sprawling centers with square footage in the millions and power demands upwards of 100 MW (which could power 100,000 homes). Collectively, some estimates put the global power usage at 200 TWh annually. (That’s juice for 11 million homes.)

How can companies even think about the grid beginning to handle that demand? Increasingly, they aren’t. Many hyperscalers are looking to “bring your own power [BYOP],” says Jonathon Smith, associate director of Sustainable Finance Research at Fitch. In other words, build their own power plants. The problem here is twofold: First, very few people on the planet know how to build something like this. You could buy the biggest army the world has ever seen; try moving it without engineers. Second, it poses very real and very dangerous environmental threats.

Recently, the journal Nature Sustainability published a study revealing that the annual carbon dioxide emissions of the largest data centers in the U.S. are equal to that of a small country, like Paraguay or Denmark. And that study looked at data centers from 2024 and 2025. Data centers have exploded since then, with as much as a 190 percent increase from 2025 to 2026.

When companies like Anthropic say, “Don’t worry, we’re not even touching the grid,” it brings up a cartoon situation more akin to The Simpsons than a Saturday-morning serial. Picture it: a giant data center, clean, cool, not an emission in sight, just humming machines and maybe a security guard. But there’s a pipe going out the back, and if you follow it as it snakes around the corner, it connects to a behemoth power plant, burning enough natural gas to power a city, with plumes of smoke assaulting the sky above. Animated, but closer to the reality of BYOP.

“From an environmental-impact perspective, most of these developers are deploying natural-gas-based assets for power, which release GHG [greenhouse gas] emissions and air pollutants,” says Smith. The cleaner alternatives are renewables (unlikely), nuclear (terrifying), and batteries (no environmental picnic, either).

Among the environmental concerns—which inevitably bleed into the social and economic ones—are these centers’ use of water, which they need in great volume to keep those humming stacks of servers cool. While many experts point to these issues as overblown, especially as data centers look to closed-loop systems (there is an initial dump of water that gets cooled and reused), the reason you won’t stop hearing about water use is because 1) it’s concerning, and 2) water is inherently political and always has been. (Traditionally, we’ve needed the stuff to feed our animals, grow our crops, and, you know, live.) Go back to the early chapters of the Bible and what’s causing strife? Water. Go back even further and some of the first wars ever recorded—4,500 years ago in ancient Sumeria—are over the stuff. And data centers have become a fast-moving political football.

A recent Gallup poll indicates that 71 percent of Americans do not want an AI data center near where they live. Those are some very big numbers. It’s important to clarify that the data centers big tech wants to build near dense populations are for cloud computing, and they make up roughly 70 percent of all data centers. While centers for AI computing and AI training are catching up, these can be built farther away, for the simple reason that we’re happy to wait a few minutes for AI to do our taxes but not for the cloud to recombobulate an email or Instagram post.

That’s still a huge number of data centers that need to be near you and me, so what will we do about it? The politicians are listening. New York State has issued a moratorium. There’s legislation limiting the centers on the table in nine other states. Gina Hinojosa, who just pummeled Greg Abbott in the Governor's race in Texas, campaigned on promises of a moratorium for permitting and construction on new data centers. Norway, the Netherlands, and Denmark are leading the charge on moratoriums and other restrictions in Europe as governments catch up with popular opinion.

Moreover, back in the U.S.A., Donald Trump has touted a “deal” with big tech on data centers, the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, supposedly protecting grids from the biggies. But never has Tip O’Neill’s maxim that “All politics is local” been more salient. More than 500 localities are forming their own moratoriums, zoning freezes, and permit pauses. Even after the midterms, how and if to move forward with data centers is a debate for states, counties, cities, townships, and any other geographic entity that makes laws, collects taxes, and breathes collective air.

As the Democrats take over Congress and data centers become every pol’s favorite new cudgel, as the European Union and member states enact regulatory frameworks (often tied to renewable energy and climate), as elected officials turn away from AI’s once sunnily vague promises, something is becoming clear: It’s humans who have the power.

#6860. Fashion Has Moved Beyond the Runway and Into the Director’s Chair

A new generation of luxury houses is stepping behind (and beyond) the camera.

Ahead of Matthieu Blazy’s first Chanel Métiers d’art show, Michel Gondry introduced the collection’s world through a surreal short starring Margaret Qualley and A$AP Rocky. Released as a prelude to the runway, the film follows the couple as they drift through New York along parallel paths before finding each other again on a downtown sidewalk, where Rocky proposes moments before they disappear into the subway station together.

When guests, including Rocky and Qualley, arrived at the show, the story continued where the film had left off. Bhavitha Mandava emerged in silk “jeans,” followed later by Mona Tougaard, Anok Yai, and Alex Consani, each embodying a different New York archetype, from the sharply tailored power executive to the impeccably dressed society woman. As they moved through the decommissioned station at Bowery and Kenmare Street in Blazy’s reimagined tweeds, the everyday commute took on a cinematic quality. “Designers are directors themselves, except we’re all their actors,” says Jane Rosenthal, an Academy Award-nominated producer, co-founder of the Tribeca Festival, and co-chair of the board of Tribeca Enterprises. “When they create characters this vivid, they’re creating not just a dress, but a feeling people want to inhabit.”

Fashion’s love affair with the silver screen stretches across decades. Coco Chanel designed costumes for two Jean Renoir films; Hubert de Givenchy created Audrey Hepburn’s black sheath dress for Breakfast at Tiffany’s. Today, the relationship runs in both directions. Julian Klausner’s spring/summer 2027 collection for Dries Van Noten borrowed the language of cinema through imagined movie posters splashed on slips and party skirts, while the Angelina Jolie-starring Couture turned its attention to the women behind the seams.

And as film culture enjoys a renaissance of its own, fashion is deepening its relationship with the medium. Letterboxd has turned cinephilia into a social, gamified experience, while celebrities now revel in the chance to highlight their most obscure Criterion Collection favorites. Fashion, ever attuned to shifts in culture, has followed suit, moving beyond the familiar fields of costume design and cinematic campaigns to support and sometimes produce filmmaking itself.

Since 2015, Through Her Lens: The Tribeca Chanel Women’s Filmmaker Program, co-founded by Rosenthal, has paired mentorship with financial support for emerging female and nonbinary filmmakers. This year’s program brought together mentors such as Celine Song and Gina Prince-Bythewood, who worked directly with participants.

Rosenthal points out that the number of female-directed films has, unfortunately, dipped in recent years. After over a decade of the program, “What we’re finding is that the community is really intentional,” she says. “Our advisory board wants to be here. They come back year after year. They want to be part of it, because they want to help support the next generation.” Miu Miu’s Women’s Tales, established by Miuccia Prada in 2011, has similarly become a platform for female filmmakers—including Ava DuVernay, Claire Denis, and Mona Fastvold—to tell original stories. Those initiatives were early signs of a broader shift.

In September, Dior announced a two-year creative partnership with A24, the studio behind auteur-driven hits like Marty Supreme and Everything Everywhere All at Once, spanning film, theater, and live events at the Cherry Lane Theatre in Manhattan’s West Village. The collaboration was introduced online with a tongue-in-cheek short directed by Aidan Zamiri, in which Sabrina Carpenter, Josh O’Connor, and Drew Starkey auditioned to play Dior creative director Jonathan Anderson. (The announcement kicked off with a companion teaser, featuring Carpenter dressed as Anderson himself, complete with his now instantly recognizable formula of a plaid shirt, jeans, and Salomons.)

Under its former creative director Anthony Vaccarello, Saint Laurent launched Saint Laurent Productions in 2023, backing acclaimed films including Pedro Almodóvar’s Strange Way of Life and Jacques Audiard’s Oscar-winning melodrama Emilia Pérez. And Rolex has also invested in cinema’s future, supporting the Student Academy Awards, which platform emerging filmmakers, partnering with festivals like the New York Film Festival and the Sundance Institute as an official partner and exclusive timepiece, and serving as a founding supporter of the Academy Museum of Motion Pictures.

Tara Maria Gonzalez, a fashion writer and author of the Substack newsletter Cult Classic, notes that film allows brands room to build worlds rather than simply present collections or have their designs live in the endless scroll of social media. “People are growing tired of seeing the same trends,” she explains. “It’s like, ‘Well, why do I need to buy that if everybody else has it?’ When designers align themselves with directors, that adds a level of depth that can also connect to a wider audience.”

Look no further than Balenciaga, which enlisted Past Lives and Materialists director Song to helm the short film A New York Minute. Song built a character study around Sarah Pidgeon, using Pierpaolo Piccioli’s designs as a narrative thread rather than the main attraction. It leaves the viewer to focus on a mood before a product, an inversion of what fashion advertising has traditionally asked us to do. “Brands are [showing] that fashion isn’t just for people who self-identify as ‘fashion people,’” Gonzalez notes. “Fashion has the mass appeal that can speak to anybody who’s interested in storytelling. And one of the most accessible forms of storytellingis film.”

Like Gondry’s lovers disappearing into the subway station, the most memorable fashion stories don’t end when the collection arrives on the runway or on the shop floor. They only become more interesting out on the streets, where clothes become characters and the city becomes the set.

#6833. Burgundy Cat-Eye Nails Are the Most Mesmerizing Mani of the Season

There are a few nail colors I consider non-negotiable on my fingertips once fall rolls around: chocolate brown, black cherry, and, of course, burgundy. The latter is a rich, wine-like shade that’s classic, sophisticated, and something I find myself coming back to year after year. For 2026, the color has gotten a seriously mesmerizing makeover in the form of burgundy cat-eye nails. It’s not only a shimmery, dimensional manicure that’ll have you staring at your fingertips all day long, but it’s also a certified trend with searches for the look up more than 300 percent on Google. “Burgundy cat-eye nails have been one of my favorite looks to create behind the nail station,” says celebrity nail artist Erica De Los Santos.

Unlike a traditional glossy burgundy manicure, cat-eye nails use magnetic polish to create a light-catching effect that shifts and moves depending on the angle. Basically, it scratches a really specific itch in your brain that you’ll definitely become obsessed with (if you aren’t already). With the help of De Los Santos, I have all the deets on how to get burgundy cat-eye nails, plus plenty of photo inspo.

Burgundy cat-eye nails are exactly what they sound like: a moody and sophisticated rich, wine-red manicure created with polish that contains magnetic particles. This delivers a shifting shimmer that looks like velvet or polished gemstones—just enough sparkle to make these perfect for the holiday season.

“What makes this look so beautiful is how the depth and color shift change depending on the undertone of the base and the magnetic shade layered over it,” says De Los Santos. “The result is a multidimensional finish that catches the light beautifully, giving the nails a luxurious, almost velvet-like appearance.”

And the best part? There are so many ways to make the look your own. The magnetic effect can be customized depending on how much shimmer you want, and you can work a cat-eye into all types of nail art. “One of my favorite ways to wear burgundy cat-eye nails is as a solid color because the magnetic effect is already so beautiful on its own,” says De Los Santos. “But if you want to have a little fun with it, you can pair it with chrome starburst accents, playful polka dots, or even animal prints. The beauty of a burgundy cat-eye is how versatile it is.”

If you tell your nail artist you want a burgundy cat-eye manicure, they’ll have no problem picking the perfect deep red shade. However, you’ll need to specify whether you want a soft velvet cat-eye finish or a more defined cat-eye look. Bringing a reference photo is always helpful (gotcha covered below), since the placement and intensity of the shimmer can completely change the finished look.

After prep (“I always start by gently pushing back the cuticles, carefully removing excess dead cuticle, and shaping the nails”), De Los Santos applies one coat of a deep plum or burgundy gel polish and lets it cure.

Next, “I apply one coat of magnetic cat-eye gel, preferably in a shade similar to the base color, such as deep red, plum, or purple,” says De Los Santos. “Keeping the shades within the same color family really enhances the depth and makes the magnetic effect pop. Before curing, I use a magnet to create the desired light-reflecting effect.” She flash cures (a short, incomplete cure that’s a few seconds long) each nail as she applies the polish and magnet, and then fully cures all of them at the same time once every nail is painted. From there, leave your cat eye as-is or add chrome powder, designs, or any other nail art you love.

You can also create the look at home with magnetic nail polish and a compatible nail magnet (talk about an easy DIY nail art moment!). De Los Santos explains that gel gives you more time to manipulate the magnetic particles, but she also says there are lots of traditional polish options out there to get the cat-eye effect. With regular polish, prep is exactly the same, but the only thing that’s different is the application. “Apply a coat, immediately use your magnet while the polish is still wet, and move on to the next nail,” says De Los Santos (working with the polish while it’s still wet is key to get the magnetic particles to sit where you’d like them to). “Once you’ve completed all ten nails, you can repeat the process with a second coat if you’d like to build more depth and dimension.” Finish with the appropriate top coat.

Instead, incorporating layers of lighting to add dimension and create a warm atmosphere is much better for your home. Sconces, picture lights, table lamps, and thoughtfully placed accent lighting at various heights throughout the room, Mendoza adds, give a space a more intimate touch.

“I also recommend using warm 2700K light sources on dimmers to flatter people, highlight architectural details, and bring out the natural beauty of materials,” she explains. “Lighting should be considered early in the design and construction process, not treated as an afterthought.”

Part of the appeal of “grandma chic” decor is that it’s been collected over time. The pieces show their age, the interior is imperfect, and it’s very clearly reflective of the homeowner’s personality and life. However, “there is a difference between a room that feels ‘collected over time’ and one that feels inherited all at once,” designer Stephanie Kraus explains, and she’s tired of seeing this false presentation on display in people’s homes.

“Heavy Roman shades with lots of fabric, chintz on top of chintz on top of chintz, fringe on every edge, etc.,” she adds. “One of these details in a room can be charming, but if you use them all together, the room can start to feel like a period movie set.”

It’s often clear when this eccentric aesthetic is manufactured rather than effortless. Instead of trying to make the “ideal” interior in the span of a month, be patient and work on your home over a greater span of time. That way, the end product will look authentic.

Though curved sofas are massively popular right now and look gorgeous in a photograph, Kraus is ready to leave them in 2026. “A curved sofa rarely seats more than two people comfortably,” she says. “Once guests settle in, everyone ends up sitting awkwardly, and conversation feels harder than it should.”

She’s all for a sofa or couch being something that invites people in to linger over conversations and stay late into the night, and a kidney-shaped seat isn’t going to do that.

While designer Maggie Vollrath of Provision Studios loved seeing the return of decorative, playful details like ruffles and scalloped edges, she thinks we’ve gone a bit overboard in 2026. These touches are being applied to just about everything: upholstery, pillows, lampshades, bedding, furniture—the list could go on.

“These details can be incredibly charming, but when they're repeated throughout an entire room, they begin to lose the very character that makes them special,” she says.

Rather than treat every piece of a room with one of these details, Vollrath suggests opting for a scalloped lampshade or a beautifully tailored ruffled pillow instead to still capture that charm without drowning the space in them.

“Limiting these details to one or two thoughtful applications allows them to feel intentional rather than overly thematic,” she adds. “Sometimes, the most interesting interiors are as much about what you choose to leave out as what you include.”

“I'd love to see us move away from selecting materials simply because they imitate the appearance of something more expensive,” Vollrath explains. This goes beyond marble-printed contact paper and burl wood veneer; the designer means on a larger scale, like mass-produced tile that’s made to replicate the imperfections of handmade zellige.

“Part of what makes authentic zellige so beautiful is the craftsmanship and natural variation that comes from its production,” she says. “When those qualities are artificially reproduced, I think we sometimes lose the very thing that made the original material special.”

She would rather see a greater appreciation for beautiful, honest materials at every price point, and if the exact thing a homeowner wants is out of budget, then her solution would be to pivot the idea altogether rather than find something cheaply made that looks like what they desire.

“A classic Carrara marble paired with beautifully considered cabinetry can create an incredibly sophisticated interior, even when something as dramatic as Calacatta Viola isn't within the budget,” Vollrath explains.

We could probably say this every year, but it bears repeating: Don’t solely follow trends! “Just because something is trending doesn't mean it's right for your home,” designer Drew Michael Scott of Lone Fox says. “Polka dots are having a moment right now, but they don't make sense in many homes, or dare I say, most homes.”

He suggests thinking of home trends as prompts that should get you to brainstorm new ideas for your space that actually fit your style. Take polka dots again. If you want to introduce a new pattern to your home, but you don’t particularly mesh with polka dots, try a stripe instead.

“If you try to force something you don't love into your home just because it's trending, you're going to drive yourself crazy,” he adds. Let’s leave this line of thinking back in 2026 once and for all.

While we love a good built-in bookshelf and wooden ceiling beam, those types of architectural details don’t always make sense in every single home. In 2026, as personality-filled interiors became more popular for everyone, a lot of homeowners looked into adding this type of millwork to their spaces as a way to add more visual interest and texture.

“Decorative beams, arches, and elaborate millwork can feel forced when they don't make sense with the architecture of the home,” designer Dani Flax of Pearson Design Group argues. “I think there's a tendency to add these elements to make a space feel more interesting, but the result can actually take away from the architecture itself.”

A home in a modern farmhouse style, for example, would look a bit odd with an intricate ceiling medallion and excessive picture frame molding. Design your interior around the existing architecture and the style of your home, don’t incorporate these elements simply because they’re everywhere right now.

The next year is not about playing it safe with your interiors—designers are tired of the stark white home. While ubiquitous gray and white color schemes had their time, they can often make a home feel generic and devoid of character.

“In their place, we're embracing more intentional, tonal palettes and color drenching,” Mendoza says.

You can still have a mostly white house, of course, but take a little risk and mix in some pops of color and pattern here and there, or choose a beautiful neutral wood tone for your cabinetry instead of a bright white paint.

“Taking a considered color, whether it's a deep green, warm clay, or earthy mushroom, and carrying it across walls, trim, and cabinetry creates a cohesive, enveloping effect,” Mendoza adds. “The goal isn't necessarily to introduce more color, but to use it with greater confidence and purpose.”

This is definitely a hot take, but designer John Maloney McNamara of Coosa Interiors is ready to leave velvet upholstery behind. “I’ve tried, but velvet almost always reads as either a 1990s prom limo or a hotel lobby trying very hard,” he explains. “The sheen shows every crush and bruise, and it ages into something that looks tired rather than loved.”

If you love the look of velvet, he suggests going with a mohair fabric instead, saying it has all of velvet’s depth and none of its shine. It also only gets better with age. And though he knows creamy bouclé has been overdone, Maloney McNamara thinks it deserves a reprise, but only in a rich color that can replace velvet still.

#6808. Van Cleef & Arpels Explores the Secrets and Mysteries of Ancient Egypt

The maison’s latest high jewelry collection taps into the mystique of the Valley of the Kings

After archaeologist Howard Carter and his team discovered King Tutankhamun’s tomb in 1922, regal diadems, golden collars, and bold cuffs brought a new wave of Egyptian Revival into the Art Deco movement. Van Cleef & Arpels took note and added Egyptian-inspired calligraphy, hieroglyphs, and semiprecious stones into its lexicon of deco bracelets, sautoirs, and brooches, bringing a historic feel to the frostbitten glamour that defined the roaring ’20s. Now, over a century later, the maison’s new Fascinating Egypt high jewelry collection explores more avenues of the region’s mystical splendor.

From opulent collars to sculptural rings, each design harkens back to the arts, values, and beliefs that defined the New Kingdom. But just as Egyptologists explored hidden messages beneath the grandeur of the 18th dynasty, Van Cleef conveys the mystique beneath its history. Shape-shifting necklaces function like secret passageways to new realms of expression, and Mysterioux brooches reincarnate sacred deities in gold and gemstones. Just take the Bennou, a terrestrial vessel for the sun god Ra, whose feathers of emeralds, marine-blue sapphires, lapis, cerulean slips of turquoise, mother of pearl, and drops of diamonds represent the colors of the earth. A blood-orange spessartite garnet with rays of diamonds hangs from a red spinel, like a drop of sunshine.

“There are a lot of real animals that were fashioned into amulets and small statues, likely to imbue the owners with some of their traits, but also to revere the creature and the benefits it provided,” archaeologist Randall Hixenbaugh tells Bazaar. His namesake gallery in Manhattan’s Design District is home to artifacts sourced in accordance with the ethical standards set by the International Association of Dealers in Ancient Arts.

Talismans were worn like supplements to enhance the human experience—cats to spark joy, mongooses to offer protection from snakes, and frogs for fertility. Stones of certain colors were valued for their connotations with abundance and immortality. “The Egyptians prized bright blue and green materials, which were associated with the Nile, the sky, and life itself. Lapis lazuli was rare and valuable and therefore associated with the pharaohs. Its extensive use on King Tut’s gold mask shows how highly regarded it was,” Hixenbaugh explains.

The cobalt stone finds its way into all kinds of Van Cleef’s latest creations. It’s used generously in the “Ornement de Saphir” necklace, whose central totem resembles an evil eye, with an oval-cut sapphire pupil plopped cleverly in an iris of pavé diamonds. Suspended from a panel of lapis lazuli interspersed with slivers of gold and baguette-cut rubies, the pendant is detachable and can be worn like a brooch, while its Corde chain incorporates clasps concealed beneath colorful rondelles that allow it to transform into a pair of bracelets. A concept conceived in the 1940s, the versatile signature speaks to the ongoing conversation between the house’s heritage and Egypt’s mystique.

Some of Van Cleef’s most striking interpretations of the ancient kingdom come through in Art Deco bracelets. The articulated cuff style was a staple of the 1920s, with ample real estate for planes of illustrious motifs to flourish. Instead of a painter’s palette, Van Cleef brought diamonds, emeralds, and rubies together like dots on a pointillist painting through the “mystery set” technique (a manner of setting gemstones sans prongs).

The house patented the method in the 1930s and has continued to enlist it to craft seamless gemscapes, extending into a series of “Paysage” bracelets. Clad in fields of gemstones, the deco-style pieces bring Egyptian royals, celestial skies, and monuments to life. Openwork, a technique that minimizes the use of solid metals, enables light to pass through each bejeweled mural, amplifying the high shine of each cuff. Other inconspicuous elements, like golden hieroglyphic nuggets, rest beneath articulated columns, like artifacts awaiting discovery.

The house’s bold fonts of design can be equally understood through the juxtaposition of lolly-like cabochons and sugar-white diamonds. Amid the sea of platinum and flashy Asscher cuts that defined Gatsby-era decadence, Van Cleef & Arpels brought teardrop emeralds into the mix. Suspended from pillars of diamonds set in platinum, the plump stones hung like enticing lozenges throughout a collaret necklace, circa 1929. The spellbinding masterpiece drew the eye of Egyptian royalty, Her Royal Highness Princess Faiza, who purchased the necklace in 1937. It was a convergence of brilliant facets and vivid, dome-shaped gems that bridged the gradual shift into midcentury modern shapes and matte stones. The provenance of the design influenced the maison’s updated rendition of the crown jewel, featuring luscious emeralds and slender pearls suspended from elongated pendants of white gold and diamonds.

By the 1950s, Van Cleef’s interpretations of Egyptomania embraced organic forms, space-age ellipses, and plots of semiprecious stones. Smooth, matte surfaces of turquoise, malachite, and coral enhanced the realism of biomorphic designs, like a scarab clip with wings of turquoise, circa 1957. (The house would lend the brooch to the Petit Palais for the debut of “Tutankhamun and His Time” nearly a decade later.) Such vibrant stones extended into contemporary aesthetics of the 1980s, such as a collar necklace slathered in slices of jade, stripes of onyx, and birds carved out of coral.

This collection revisited the era through the language of Ettore Sottsass and the Memphis Group, a 1980s decorative arts firm, for spherical shapes and elements of cubism to take flight. Some of Sottsass’s most recognizable pieces include a sterling silver fruit stand with zigzag pillars and a colorblocked bookstand named “The Carlton.” Van Cleef translates this friction between elements smooth and sharp, glossy and matte into pieces like a saucer-shaped Mythe Celeste clip, coated in sheets of lapis and malachite with buttons of coral—elements beheld by the Egyptians as modalities of abundance and immortality. By contrast, a Frise Divine ring juxtaposes an oval-shaped Sri Lankan sapphire with a rigid tile of coral, and rows of diamonds set in zigzag streams of gold.

All creations in the Fascinating Egypt œuvre are united by tradition, a preservation of heritage echoed in the foundations of other art forms, like ballet. In fact, the house’s seamless dances of emeralds, rubies, and diamonds compelled George Balanchine to choreograph Jewels for the New York City Ballet in 1931—a moment that came full circle when the jewelers debuted a collection of ballerina clips and brooches a couple of years later. The nature of their anatomically sound arms, gilded in gold and ribbons of gemstones, extends into the collection’s Muse Éternelle brooches. The fantasy of falling in love in a small town has surprisingly little to do with the small town itself.

I’ll find any excuse to cozy up with a holiday rom-com, so when Guarding Stars dropped on Netflix, it was a no-brainer. Gossip Girl and Supernatural alumni Leighton Meester and Jared Padalecki are embracing one of my favorite tropes: the good old “small-town holiday romance.” Leighton’s character Hannah goes with Jared’s character Jack to his hometown pretending to be his girlfriend, while actually serving as his bodyguard. It got me thinking: Whether we’re die-hard city dwellers or seasoned travelers, why do we still love the home-for-the-holidays romance trope?

For me, the answer lies in how this specific genre taps into our own deep-rooted desire for escapism. We’ve seen these kinds of fake girlfriend or friends-to-lovers storylines play out a million times before, so their predictability is incredibly comforting to watch. We have, quite literally, seen this film before. And during the heightened chaos of the holiday season, it places viewers in the safe bubble many people crave.

Plus, the small-town setting in Guarding Stars is perfectly cozy. The two head to Jack’s obnoxiously large family home in Montana that’s been pristinely decorated in Nancy Meyers–inspired aesthetics. And much like in one of my favorite holiday movies, The Family Stone, the house itself is just part of the fantasy. The idea of being welcomed into a close-knit community with acceptance might be exactly what we dream of the holidays being like. Being loved by a family that, in the case of Hannah, is not even your own is really intoxicating.

Even the dating looks more fun. Hannah and Jack spend the whole movie dating their way around town, doing cutesy romantic things like attending the local rodeo, getting caught on a kiss cam, and mucking out horses together in the family stables. These wouldn’t be your go-to date locations in a more urban, city-based setting, so their dating experience feels playful and different from everyday life.

And Guarding Stars finds a way to elevate this trope. Hannah isn’t actively looking for a man when all of this unfolds. She’s just doing her job as a bodyguard when she inadvertently falls into a snowball of festive shenanigans. Hannah is doing fine on her own, but there’s something sweet about watching her let her guard down. Guarding Stars gently suggests that by Hannah making herself a little more vulnerable, she’s making room for more people to love and support her. As someone who tends to handle the hard stuff alone (eldest daughter syndrome, anyone?), that really resonated with me.

So often, this genre leans toward women going to the small town and being “fixed” or changed by the relationship they encounter. Their lives in the big city are unsatisfying, actually, and they really would rather work at the Christmas tree farm. That doesn’t happen here. She ends up back in New York City, and that’s where the final action set piece of the movie happens.

Guarding Stars shows that the small-town holiday romance trope can change and evolve without losing its festive magic, because it’s about comfort and connection. We can be cozy and keep our apartments in New York City, and I’m thankful for that this holiday season.

Moving has traditionally been a stressful experience that you were expected to figure out on your own—but that may be changing. Allison Flinn, owner of Reclaim Professional Organizing, says that more people are taking advantage of organizing services when they move.

“You can pack and unpack, but you won't truly have a fully functional and an organized new home if you haven't decluttered prior to moving or aren't intentional during the unpacking process by implementing a system that truly works for your lifestyle,” she says.

Instead of doing what a lot of us do—devising storage systems for each individual space—2027 may welcome the idea that you should treat your home as an entire unit to be organized.

Michelle Urban, owner and founder of The Organized House, says that people are increasingly not treating each room as its own one-off organizing project. She says that instead, they’re looking at how belongings move from one space to another and where they need to be throughout the day. For instance, items like backpacks, shoes, and sports gear may land in the entry, get stored in the garage, and need to be back by the door the next morning.

“When those spaces are planned together, the system supports the family’s routine instead of just moving things from one pile to another,” Urban says.

Viewed more holistically, experts are also seeing that organizing isn’t just an occasional task, but something to stay on top of each week. “I think more homeowners will begin to view organization as ongoing home maintenance rather than a one-time project,” says Ashley Bailey, founder and professional organizer at Everything Orderly. “Just like other aspects of maintaining a home, organizing requires occasional attention as our lives and needs change.”

Flinn says that more and more people are outsourcing household tasks to a home manager or home concierge. In addition to handling things like doing laundry, returning packages, and making doctor’s appointments, these professionals can also help you organize your home in just a few hours a week.

Eco-friendly everything is still as popular as ever, and it even applies to how we organize our homes. While O’Keith says that sustainability never really goes out of style, homeowners are increasingly turning to eco-friendly organizing products—specifically, by avoiding plastic and embracing natural materials like bamboo, rattan, and sea grass for baskets and bins.

But she says that there is a exception for basements and attics. “I always recommend heavy-duty plastic storage [for these areas],” O’Keith says. “Since those spaces deal with dampness, temperature changes, and pests, other materials could get damaged easily.”

While it can be lovely to live in a well-designed, beautiful home, if there isn’t function, you might make your daily life more difficult. That’s why people are going beyond Instagram-ready interiors and are wrapping in plenty of function.

Urban believes that progressively more people are bringing functional storage into a room’s design, and it isn’t just an add-on.

“A credenza can hold games in the living room; a dining room buffet can store wrapping paper and party decor between celebrations,” Urban points out. “As rooms take on more jobs, people want storage that looks like it belongs and does a useful job.”

As we head into 2027, people are thinking about their budgets more and more, which is why using items you already have as storage solutions is cost-effective.

“I'm seeing more clients interested in making better use of what they already own rather than automatically purchasing new organizing products,” Bailey says. For instance, she recently took on a project that transformed a playroom, but used items the family already had.

“Sometimes the best solution isn't another organizing product. It's rethinking how you're using the space and what you already have,” she says. “I think we'll see more emphasis on thoughtful editing and repurposing before purchasing something new.”

#6752. “Two Girls Who Speak All the Time About Politics”: Tao Okamoto and Virginie Efira Take On Capitalism in All of a Sudden

Okamoto and Efira shared the Best Actress Award at Cannes, where All of a Sudden premiered this summer

We’re all seeking comfort as the world ends. Environmental, economic, and political pressures weigh heavily on our minds as tangible solutions feel farther and farther out of reach. Solace is a rare commodity in a world controlled by unrelenting capitalist forces. In All of a Sudden, filmmaker Ryusuke Hamaguchi suggests that as we face tragedy, we must remember our vital need for human connection.

All of a Sudden tells the story of two women who forge a formidable friendship as they face personal and professional hardship. Marie-Lou Fontaine (Virginie Efira) runs an elder-care facility near Paris, where she introduces a “Humanitude” methodology designed to treat patients with dignity and introduce listening-heavy caretaking practice. She befriends Mari (Tao Okamoto), a terminally ill theater director from Japan. Ryusuke chronicles this relationship through extended, emotional conversations, spoken in both Japanese and French, and covering topics from identity to mortality.

The film is an adaptation of a nonfiction book, When Life Suddenly Takes a Turn: Twenty Letters Between a Philosopher With Terminal Cancer and a Medical Anthropologist, which collects correspondence from two Japanese-speaking academics as they reflect on health care and death during the last few months of philosopher Makiko Miyano’s life (who inspired Okamoto’s character). Instead of keeping the fact that the two pen pals spoke the same language, he introduced French and moved the story to Paris. This small change forced the story to confront language gaps, exploring the power of conversation and listening and asserting that so much can be achieved through this mediation.

Efira and Okamoto understand their characters’ simmering anxieties and outlooks on the world, providing the bedrock for these impressively naturalistic performances. The two actresses sustain long, philosophical conversation with such intensity throughout the entire three hours and 16 minutes. In a rare occurrence, the two shared the Best Actress Award at the Cannes Film Festival, where the movie premiered this summer. In the film, their budding friendship serves as a sounding board for each other, as Marie-Lou grapples with the pressure of capitalism on her medical staff and Mari comes to terms with the end of her life. In typical fashion, Ryusuke lets the camera linger on these two actresses during these prolonged philosophical interactions, forcing the audience to sit with the gravity of their situations.

Ahead of the theatrical release of Ryusuke’s latest feature film, Bazaar spoke to Efira and Okamoto about the first time they read the script, how they built an intimate bond despite speaking two different languages, and the punk, anti-capitalist edge fueling this intimate, conversation-heavy film.

Tao Okamoto: It’s the scene where she talks about capitalism while writing on the whiteboard. That’s one of the strangest scenes in the movie. I felt connected to the character because I am also a climate activist myself. I’ve been spreading the word about climate crises on podcasts, and I talk about it daily. When I read the part, I felt, “I need to play this character.”

Virginie Efira: It’s the same for me, because this scene is so special. When I read it, the movie felt like one of the most profound, special, and radical readings. You have two girls who speak all the time about politics. We don’t see that. I never see that in other movies. Sometimes, when a director wants to put something philosophical, political, or intellectual, they say, “Maybe we’ll do three minutes,” otherwise the audience will be bored. No, this movie spent 15 minutes [discussing capitalism], and I said, “Oh yes, that’s so punk!” The film has its own language. When I read All of a Sudden, I could hear the language of the movie, and I wanted to approach it. When I met Tao, I thought, “Oh, we are so lucky to be an actress and work with [Ryusuke], but also to share this material with him.”

VE: I only knew one word in Japanese before making the movie. Then, to be able to speak Japanese at a high level, because the character studied in Japan, I must first learn to read, then learn to embody the language. Finding the language of the movie was strange. There’s a flat and natural way to play [in one of Ryusuke’s movies]. He spoke to me about Cassavetes and Gena Rowlands, but also about Bresson. Neutral performances. You find it when you open up, and when I opened to Tao, we discovered the right language for the movie. We did a lot of flat readings before shooting. We never rehearsed in motion, so when we finally performed for the first time, we awakened the text.

TO: I’m going to add that because I started my career in America, finding a middle ground by using a language that’s not my mother tongue is normal for me. On the surface, that part wasn’t a challenge for me. Finding the language for this film and respecting Hamaguchi’s way of working were priorities. We were naturally drawn to his talent and wanted to make his dream happen and be part of it.

TO: I joined this project way after Virginie. I luckily had months to prepare with him, and during rehearsals, you drop all emotion and read the text as if you were reading numbers. On top of that, I got to visit some institutes, such as the Cancer Research Center and places where people work with disabled people, to build up my understanding of my role’s point of view. Reading the text over and over was a unique experience for me. [Ryusuke] can really listen to the actor’s voice and tell the difference between someone who is simply reading and someone really concentrating. Even though you’re not supposed to put any emotions, you have to be present; you have to be there. I’ve never experienced such rich, profound preparation for a role.

VE: We shot in a real nursing home. There was a mix between fiction and reality. Sometimes filmmaking becomes standardized, with no room left to breathe, but with Hamaguchi, you’re sure he will leave room for the unexpected and for uncertainty. It allows you to embrace the unpredictability of reality. He’s not looking for an accident, but for the ability to be in the present. A viewer of Hamaguchi’s work will always feel a sensation of documentation, but it’s still always cinema. To be an actor in his movies is to be naturalistic, to be in the present, to capture some details and really embrace this unpredictability. The filming process becomes an adventure he wants you to have. One day, he told me, “I hope that you will find the light.” He said a lot of cryptic things. He said to me, “Don’t use your text like a tool; just go inside the text.”

TO: The script is already amazing. And I could read it over and over again, and that was a joy. But he mentioned that I should read the original book. It’s about two women exchanging letters—or emails. They are real people who the characters are modeled after. I wanted to please the people who know these women, not to make them too different from them. I first put pressure on myself to portray this woman as close to the real person, but it was impossible for me because this person passed away in 2019. I couldn’t do much research, so I interviewed her mother and the co-writer. That provided me with a lot of clues, but reading their exchanged letters already gave me the soul of this movie.

VE: He gives the book to the actresses, but also to the entire team. I never felt something so collective, and you will feel it in the movie, from the sound engineer to someone who only stayed two days on set. I also met the anthropologist. When you read this book, it’s full of life. It gives you a perceptible beauty of things. The book has a kind of energy; it helps you keep a level of optimism even when you know the situation is hopeless. In this book, it speaks about what it’s like to be alive. Maybe there’s beauty in life because we are going to die.

TO: For me, I came on this project at the last minute, only a month before. Virginie was so dedicated to studying Japanese from scratch; it was so impressive how she studied all this writing, reading, and lines. Seeing that, I had a huge admiration, like “Who can do this!?” As you can see, Virginie’s so charming; nobody would hate her. It was so easy for me to be drawn to her as a person, as the characters in the movie were discovering each other.

VE: We got to know each other through the shoot. I was happy not to know much at the beginning and to progressively get deeper into our relationship. We lived all that together. I conflate Marie-Lou’s feelings for Mari with Virginie’s feelings for Tao, because we share that. It will be an end, not a tragic one like in the movie, but it will be an end. But I know Tao will carry this experience in her heart forever, and I will too. He let us feel what we want to feel.

TO: I study environmental issues, and I always thought capitalism is responsible for our world’s issues. Something I didn’t know until I read this script was that capitalism doesn’t only exploit our environment, but also our human bodies—human labor and our time. When I connected those dots, I realized all the problems could be blamed on capitalism. We’re looking for time and looking for happiness—or security—and all these things can be related to a capitalistic way of living. I think it’s great that Hamaguchi connected that fundamental issue to Marie-Lou’s situation in the elderly home. She’s frustrated, as is Marie. She’s battling her cancer, and it can be connected to this economic issue.

VE: Cinema can show bodies, fatigue, and with Marie-Lou, you see a certain fatigue because capitalism creates it. How do we deal with that? We say, “Oh, capitalism’s not good. We don’t have time. And we want to be richer and richer.” But how do we fight that, because everything is already there? I come from France, and you see all the young people outside. The real question is, what can cinema do with that? It’s not a theoretical thing, but Hamaguchi can find a solution. It’s speaking, listening to the other, sharing ideas; it’s movement. If I look for a solution to this problem with money, I’ll remain a prisoner of this way of functioning. Even if you think you’re not in a stage of capitalism, you are.

#6740. What Is GTA VI Trying to Say About the Manosphere?

What’s a man allowed to like these days? Not the Super Bowl, or Christopher Nolan movies, it seems, each of them guilty of sins against masculinity. Now there is a new offender: Grand Theft Auto VI. Over one brief weekend, Rockstar’s long-in-the-works, exorbitantly expensive opus has given us a rare monocultural moment and elicited a wounded reaction from a slice of gamers steeped in the streams and podcasts of right-wing figures like Steven Crowder, Zack “Asmongold” Hoyt, and Braden Eric “Clavicular” Peters.

We asked a physical therapist, developers, and other gaming obsessives for the best tips to help you highjack garbage trucks in Leonida for as long as humanly possible.

Grand Theft Auto VI, these men assert, is emasculating because it asks players to role-play a man that respects his partner and do mundane tasks she would appreciate, like repaint their bedroom or get her a nice necklace. For others, it is less that the game is offensively woke and more that its wokeness is getting in the way of their fun. “You can’t kiss anyone other than your strong female Latina partner,” complains Crowder, his cohosts all lamenting that video games ought to be a vehicle for their every base fantasy. These men object to what they see as politics encroaching on what they think ought to be a politics-free space, “shut up and dribble” but for violent video games. But there’s a delicious irony to this because the game genuinely has a bone to pick with these unfortunate figureheads of the manosphere.

The manosphere, it should be noted, is a (shitty) state of mind. Less a formal designation than a collection of people who promote similar sexist, racist, and/or fascist ideals, a loose web of podcasters, streamers, and influencers who all preach a gospel of grievance in which men are victims of women withholding sex, liberals withholding power, and other men for not performing masculinity correctly. They can run the gamut from disgraced influencer Andrew Tate (currently in jail awaiting trial for a litany of alleged crimes) to the comedians and podcasters orbiting Joe Rogan’s massive audience. But they all have one thing in common: These men and the people who pay rapt attention to them are the targets of Grand Theft Auto VI’s satire.

Some background here: While yes, Grand Theft Auto VI casts players as modern Bonnie and Clyde Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos, their story is only one reason to play the game. Another is the incredibly dense setting of Leonida, a feverish and astonishingly lifelike take on Florida, chock-full of weirdos and wildlife to take in. Layered on top of that is a whole media ecosystem: a fake Instagram to scroll named Snapmatic, a news feed app, a suite of radio stations with robust programming including two talk radio stations, three TV networks with entire shows, and magazines and books you can read in excerpts.

It’s in all this background material that Grand Theft Auto VI really tears into the modern grievance grift of the manosphere. The game’s decade-plus gestation means it’s had a lot of time to amass a shooting gallery of noxious conservative male influencers that the algorithm has elevated and buried, ranging from flat-earth conspiracy theorists to MAHA-adjacent cronies. On the Weazel TV network, you can find these views as the punchline of entire Adult Swim–esque cartoons like Right Wing, about a patriotic superhero horrified to learn that a straight pride parade has been transformed by a gay superhero called the Flamer. “All these years suppressing who I was, but now I’m free!” shouts Right Wing’s colleague Great Uncle Sam after the Flamer has liberated him. “Isn’t that what America is all about?” “No,” Right Wing retorts. “America is about guns, and lots of them!”

Scroll through Snapmatic, and you can find a clear analogue of Liver King—the content creator who promoted an “ancestral lifestyle” that involved consuming raw animal organs—posting under @paleoprimal. In one of his faux influencer videos, he advises his followers to “start beating your meat,” before dropkicking a hunk of raw flesh. “I don’t eat anything unless I’ve conquered it first.” Ashes of Glory, a sendup of historical war fiction that sits by Jason’s bed, is drowning in overly reverent prose that also happens to note that its war heroes are psychopaths. One passage: “'You’ve been charged with murdering civilians, violating every law known to God and man, calling your enemies rude names, and uncontrollable teabagging,’ said the General. 'Which is exactly why we need your help.’”

A lot of these blows are glancing, more about attacking attitudes than people, give or take a Liver King. But turn the in-game radio dial to WKTT, Grand Theft Auto VI’s conservative talk radio station, and the targets sharpen. Hosts Richard Bastion (Jason Sudeikis) and Dominic “The Brick” Brizzolato (Stavros Halkias) do pastiches of Alex Jones and Nick Fuentes, spouting racist and sexist takes. Ponytails are “the hairstyle of someone who’s about to suck your dick,” per Brizzolato, who also maintains local football team the Manatees have problems to do with “culture” and “not race.” Every so often they are undercut by callers who ask how performance-enhancing drugs and hair implants don’t count as gender-affirming care. Of the many items they hawk—Flaming O’s snacks, Sprunk soda, and Patriot beer—the most absurd and on-the-nose is a special playing card set with “no queens.”

In its blunt re-creation of the conservative male media ecosystem, Grand Theft Auto VI plainly states what Rockstar thinks of these guys: They’re really fucking stupid. They are stupid in ways that are true to life and moronic in ways that Rockstar exaggerates, but only just so. They drive home this point by underlining the pathetic loneliness subtextual in all these real-life broadcasts, with Richard Bastion asserting that he doesn’t have friends but is building “a fraternity with a mission to save America” and that friends “let you down” and “tell the papers that your wife sleeps in a separate bed and sometimes house.”

After 13 years, and a budget rumored to rival that of Dubai’s Burj Khalifa skyscraper, the new Grand Theft Auto is finally here. Our critic's verdict? It's a tour de force that reimagines what a video game can be.

The message hits cleaner than past iterations of the game not because Grand Theft Auto VI is any less lowbrow than before—it is still awash in dick jokes, memes about shitting your pants, and South Park–esque lambasting of White America—but because this more richly detailed, lifelike version of Grand Theft Auto means the cartoonish aspects stand out all the more. What’s a joke and what is not is clearer than it has ever been.

Despite the protests from fans who say the latest iteration of Grand Theft Auto betrays the spirit of the franchise, there was always evidence that the game hated these hypermasculinity-obsessed tryhards, even before they had a manosphere to inhabit. While early games in the series did not have much in the way of plot or characters, they were tongue-in-cheek caricatures of gangster epics and the ambitions of men within them, a touch of arch Scottish humor (Rockstar’s lead Grand Theft Auto team is based in Edinburgh) keeping them from being sincere homages.

Nearly 20 years ago in Grand Theft Auto IV—a series turning point, where the games started to have stories with intentional thematic interests—Rockstar Games incessantly lambasted the pursuit of the hypermasculine. Its protagonist Niko Bellic served as a stoic counterpoint to many of the jacked-up weirdos of the faux New York dubbed Liberty City, arriving in the United States during the game’s opening credits with nothing but the clothes on his back and his cousin Roman’s lies about his own success in his ear. Through Niko’s journey, Grand Theft Auto IV posed the arguments that this country’s obsession with the unfettered pursuit of power and wealth makes people unwell and that culture afflicts men in ways that render them uniquely delusional. It gives his cousin Roman a fantasy of a success that will never come, causing him to submit to the intimidations of two-bit enforcers. It makes men like Brucie Kibbutz, a supplement-obsessed fitness freak, deny his own obvious interest in men in favor of a nonstop stream of homophobic jokes and crude sexual banter. It makes Niko, a soldier who thought he was escaping his criminal past, find himself immediately becoming a different kind of criminal at the behest of men who believe the world is theirs to take. And that’s just in the first few hours.

Grand Theft Auto IV’s skewering came at the expense of others—namely the women and queer people who are the butt of nearly every joke in the game. Seven years later, Grand Theft Auto V would course-correct by being hateful to everyone in its fictional version of Los Angeles: the acai-and-granola liberals and the asshole conservatives alike. It mires itself in the desperation of male entitlement, as three characters—the middle-aged Michael De Santa, the young striver Franklin Clinton, and Trevor Philips, the lewd loose cannon—adjust poorly to a world they cannot dominate. De Santa, arguably the protagonist of the three characters the player can inhabit, sees the world as out to emasculate him, either through his wife’s affair, his deadbeat son, or his daughter, who he must rescue from TV producers that he assumes are predatory. Michael’s world is much simpler when he’s holding a gun, and Grand Theft Auto V is eager to get one in his hands. In this, it feels like the game wants you to agree: Isn’t this more fun than dealing with all that? And it is, because Rockstar made it that way.

Yes, there are missions. But have you seen the kicks?

There has always been a have-it-both-ways nature to Grand Theft Auto’s satire. Rockstar is happy to facilitate the same American male id it’s lampooning, and the staggering number of copies sold suggests that even conservative commentators out there appealing to incel logic are logging right onto their PlayStations the moment they stop posting. But despite what critics may claim to their audiences, Grand Theft Auto VI’s manosphere parodies are not a huge departure for the series, because the problems with toxic masculinity remain woefully consistent, as do the grifters who seek to profit from it. Insecurity is both a big business and a renewable energy source.

What’s left out of all these complaints about Grand Theft Auto VI being a game where you embody a simp man is that you do not have to do any of the loving behavior these clowns rail against in the video game. You can ignore Lucia when playing as Jason, let your relationship grow distant and decay. You can leave the walls unpainted, never pick up flowers or groceries, not press the button to hold Lucia’s hand, keep your distance on the dance floor. The game just makes clear what the real world does not: That’s a good way to end up alone. And that’s the one thing the parodied blowhards of GTA VI’s in-game manosphere broadcast loud and clear: that they’re alone.

Plant-based foods are eliminated entirely.

While eliminating foods like fruits, vegetables, and whole grains is extreme, Pouria Sefidmooye Azar, PhD, RD, a registered dietitian and assistant professor at the University of Central Arkansas, says the carnivore diet isn’t all bad. He says that short term, many people lose weight eating this way.

“Protein is more satiating than carbs and fats, so people following the carnivore diet often stay full for a longer amount of time, which means they reduce their caloric intake,” he says. Logically, it makes sense. If you’re eating foods like beef, chicken, and salmon, you’ll be less likely to dig into the pantry for snacks than if you ate a bowl of noodles.

Dr. Azar says it’s important not to conflate weight loss with getting healthier. He says that by eliminating plants, you’re going to miss out on a lot of important nutrients the body needs. You can probably guess the big one. Yep, fiber. “"If you’re cutting out every plant food, you're not getting fiber or phytonutrients, which are protective compounds found only in plants,” says Chris Vogliano, PhD, RD, a registered dietitian and global food systems expert.

While you might not suffer any consequences of this immediately, both dietitians say that continuing to eat this way can mess up your digestion. You need fiber for good poops, plain and simple. Dr. Azar says that we also need fiber to feed the healthy gut bacteria in the microbiome. This is important for supporting mental health, immune health, and minimizing the risk of chronic diseases and cancers.

Dr. Azar specifically calls out the connection between a high-meat, low-fiber diet and the risk of colorectal cancer. For example, a 2015 Oncology Reviews study shows a connection between red meat consumption and colorectal cancer risk, while a 2026 European Journal of Cancer study called low fiber consumption a major risk factor for colorectal cancer.

Considering that men are at increased risk for heart disease (the number one cause of death in the U.S.), it’s important to know how the carnivore diet can impact your cardiovascular health. Spoiler alert: It’s not good. “I would not recommend the carnivore diet as a healthy, long-term eating pattern,” says Sunny Intwala, MD, MBA, preventive cardiologist and the director of Prevention & Wellness at Northwell Health.

Dr. Intwala explains that this is because meat doesn’t just contain protein; it has a lot of saturated fat too. He says that diets centering around red meat increase LDL cholesterol and blood pressure in a few short weeks. This, he says, can contribute to inflammation and plaque buildup that increases the risk of heart attack and stroke.

As someone who studies how the way we eat impacts the planet, Dr. Vogliano points out that the carnivore diet is pretty horrible for the environment. “From a food systems lens, a meat-only diet on a global or even national level would carry massive environmental costs from land, water, and greenhouse gas emissions. This is regardless of whether the meat is conventionally raised, organic, or even regenerative. There’s just not enough land to produce that much meat,” he says.

Okay, we need plants. But what about the Brian Johnsons and Paul Salidinos of the world preaching the benefits of eating raw meat? If you are going to eat meat, are there benefits to eating it raw?

Both dietitians say no, emphasizing that there is no scientific evidence showing that raw meat is more beneficial in any way than eating cooked meat. “Liver is a nutrient-rich food and a great way to get vitamin A, vitamin B12, folate, and iron. But you can get that from cooked liver; you don’t have to eat it raw,” Dr. Vogliano says. In fact, he emphasizes that eating any meat raw increases the risk of foodborne illnesses like Salmonella and E.coli.

Dr. Azar warns against pulling a Rocky and eating raw eggs either. “The protein in raw eggs is between 50 and 60% digestible. But the protein digestibility in cooked eggs is over 90%,” he says. A study from the ‘90s in The Journal of Nutrition backs this up.

It’s worth noting that this isn’t the same for meat. A 2015 study in the same journal found that the cooking time for meat didn’t impact protein digestibility very much. So while you may get the same amount of digestible protein from raw meat as you would cooked meat, your risk of foodborne illness is greater.

Decades of scientific research make it clear that a plant-free diet is not a healthy diet. If you’re a meat lover, you might be wondering just how much meat you can get away with eating without compromising your health.

Dr. Azar’s advice is this: Meat can still be your primary protein source. Just eat some plants along with it. “If you love meat, you can still eat foods like beef and chicken, but you also need to eat fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and nuts and seeds because there are some nutrients you just can’t get from meat,” he says, adding that only eating meat will lead to nutrient deficiencies.

Dr. Vogliano delivers a dietary message you’ve likely heard many times before: The healthiest diet is a diverse one. “We know from scientific research that the more plant foods our diets consist of, the more insurance we have that we’re getting the nutrients the body needs,” he says. Because of this, he advocates for a primarily plant-based diet and only eating meat occasionally.

Different governing bodies have slightly different recommendations when it comes to how much meat you can eat while still maintaining a healthy diet. The American Institute for Cancer Research says to keep red meat under 18 ounces a week, or roughly three portions a week. The American Heart Association doesn’t give a specific serving recommendation for meat, but says to mostly get protein from plants, fish, and seafood.

Dr. Azar emphasizes that there isn’t one universal way of eating that works for everyone. One diet may make one person feel energized and leave someone else feeling exhausted. What is important, he says, is making sure you’re getting all the nutrients your body needs. And what we know for sure is that you can’t do that by just eating meat.

#6715. Two Menswear Darlings Just Made the Most Covetable Boots of 2026

Say you wanted to create the perfect pair of vintage-style military boots. First, you’d need a designer with impeccable taste and encyclopedic knowledge of vintage workwear; then you'd need a bootmaker with the requisite skills to produce it at the highest level. If everything went right, the result would be something like the Modèle 1917, a new collaboration between famed Parisian creative director Gauthier Borsarello and renowned Canadian bootmaker Viberg.

In basic terms, the Modèle 1917 is a pair of boots based on the French Army’s 1917 “brodequin,” a design used by soldiers in the trenches of WWI, available in soft brown suede (Mud) and glossy textured black leather (Pavement). But there’s nothing basic about them. The design comes courtesy of Borsarello, a top-tier collector of vintage workwear and the creative mind behind Gersaint Paris and L’Etiquette magazine. Viberg, a third-generation, family-owned boot specialist, got its start making footwear for forestry crews in British Columbia, and it still builds every pair in its Vancouver Island workshop. While it doesn’t make boots for smokejumpers and loggers anymore, old-school shoemaking techniques like a channeled insole Goodyear welt and stitchdown construction ensure their footwear retains its heirloom-level durability.

The backstory of the “brodequin” begins with the Mdl. 1893, a hobnail-soled marching boot created for French infantrymen in the late 1800s that remained in use until the 1950s. In the First World War, the brodequin’s iron-studded soles and sturdy uppers proved ideally suited to the muddy conditions of the trenches, and American troops soon adopted them in place of their inferior M1904 Marching Shoes. “To me, it’s the blueprint for many combat boots across different countries after WWI,” says Borsarello. “I love the idea that France inspired others after fighting side by side in the trenches. They’re very simple, yet instantly recognizable at the same time.”

For all of its strengths in the field, the original brodequin—whose nickname pays tongue-in-cheek homage to a medieval torture device—wasn’t what you’d call a comfortable boot, especially by modern standards. “They were so stiff that they were almost impossible to walk in,” Borsarello admits. This created something of a design challenge.

To stay as true as possible to the original, Borsarello and Viberg designed the boots on a period-correct “pied haricot” (bean-shaped) last, with a cotton twill lining, solid brass hardware, and reinforced panels that support the midfoot so the forefoot can move freely. To make the Modèle M1917 something you’d actually want to wear, they replaced the hobnailed soles with rubber ones and swapped the original roughout leather for supple American-tanned Horween. The result is a pair of boots that are as comfortable as they are durable, not to mention surprisingly versatile. In other words, exactly what you’d expect from a meeting of these formidable talents.
