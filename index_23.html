#7057. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#7053. SailPoint Intros New Capabilities to Address Agentic AI Identity Security Challenge

AI agents are moving from experiments into everyday enterprise environments, but the security controls around them are not keeping pace, according to enterprise identity security company SailPoint. In its latest Horizons of Identity Security report, SailPoint found that 79% of enterprises already have AI agents running in production, while just 2% have deployed identity security tools specifically designed to govern them.

That gap could become harder to ignore as organizations give agents more autonomy. Agents can increasingly access applications, sensitive data, and credentials, and in some cases take actions on a user's behalf, effectively creating a new class of identity that IT and security teams need to manage.

SailPoint, which develops identity security and access governance technology for enterprises, is responding with a collection of new identity security capabilities announced at its Navigate 2026 conference. The company is expanding its Agentic Fabric to discover "shadow AI" agents and provide runtime authorization and controls capable of immediately disabling an agent.

The company also introduced what it calls SailPoint Autonomous Agents, specialized AI agents designed to govern other agents. The security agents can monitor runtime activity, intercept threats, and adjust permissions while allowing legitimate agents to continue operating, SailPoint said.

The idea reflects one of the challenges created by increasingly autonomous AI systems: Traditional human approval processes may not be fast enough to govern software capable of taking large numbers of actions on its own.

"The idea that a security admin is going to review and approve access for a group of autonomous agents making 10,000 tool calls a second isn't just outdated — it's a fantasy," SailPoint CTO Chandra Gnanasambandam said.

Another part of SailPoint's approach focuses on visibility.

New endpoint and browser sensors, along with SIEM and XDR telemetry and non-human identity discovery capabilities, are designed to uncover agents, MCP servers, credentials, tools, and data stores that haven't been brought under an organization's identity program.

The company is also introducing Autonomous Identity Security Posture Management, or A-ISPM, which uses identity information from its Atlas platform to identify dormant accounts, orphaned access, shadow administrators, and other potentially risky access paths. Organizations can then revoke access, disable an identity or AI agent, assign an owner or automate enforcement.

Just-in-Time provisioning is another part of the strategy. Rather than giving people, service accounts, and AI agents permanent access, permissions can be granted when needed and subjected to conditions such as reauthentication or a business justification.

Capabilities across SailPoint's Agentic Fabric, Human Fabric, and Atlas platforms are beginning to roll out globally. A-ISPM and several other announced features are expected in the company's fourth quarter of fiscal 2027.

"Autonomous agents are no longer just experimental copilots; they are becoming the primary execution layer of modern enterprises," SailPoint President Matt Mills said.

#7049. Survey: Most Organizations Are Not Consistently Measuring AI Value Against Costs

A new study from consulting firm KPMG found that while AI investment continues to grow, relatively few organizations consistently measure whether the technology's benefits justify its costs. The company's Global AI Pulse Q3 2026 report found that 89% of organizations report measurable value from AI, while just 12% consistently assess that value against costs across the organization.

Average planned AI investment for the next 12 months has risen to $210 million, compared with $186 million in the first quarter of 2026.

The findings are based on responses from 2,131 senior leaders across 20 countries and jurisdictions, surveyed between July 23 and August 26, 2026.

Most organizations have established ways to track AI spending: 61% review costs during project approval, and 59% monitor them once systems are operating.

Consistently connecting those costs to value remains less common. Beyond the 12% that do so across the organization, another 26% assess value against costs for most initiatives.

Organizations reporting established AI returns have stronger measurement practices. Among this group, 48% consistently assess value against costs, four times the proportion in the overall survey group.

Cost-monitoring dashboards are also more common among organizations reporting established returns: 77% use them, compared with 43% of those still experimenting.

"The organizations seeing a return did the implementation and the transformation properly: guardrails in place, workforce ready, data cleaned up," said Priya Emmanuel, global head of aIQ at KPMG International. "That is a sequence, not a secret."

Organizations are also developing more formal systems to manage AI deployment.

Fifty-five percent operate a formal AI management layer, which KPMG calls a "harness layer," comprising controls and tools between AI models and business applications.

Security and identity controls, along with data access controls, were the most common capabilities, each reported by 43% of respondents. Model routing, which determines which model handles a particular task, was reported by 23%.

Meanwhile, 53% assign accountability for AI-informed decisions to a named executive, the CEO, or an executive committee.

The proportion reporting significant employee adoption of AI agents rose from 25% in the first quarter to 34% in the third quarter.

Cybersecurity concerns were more common among organizations with mature AI programs.

Half of those reporting established AI returns identified cybersecurity as a barrier, compared with roughly one-third of those still experimenting.

Overall, 86% of respondents said their organizations are adapting their cybersecurity operating models to address AI-accelerated threats.

The report identifies accountability, operational resilience, and cost management as priorities as AI becomes more embedded in business operations.

"Many organizations have made AI models widely available and track their use," said Swaminathan Chandrasekaran, head of the US AI Center of Excellence at KPMG. "But consumption alone is not a strategy."

#7044. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

#7031. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#7028. Survey: Organizations Report Positive ROI on AI Investments, Yet Data Readiness and Oversight Lag Behind

The company's recent State of Enterprise AI Maturity research surveyed 515 U.S.-based AI decision-makers. Just 5% believed their organizations' data was largely ready for AI.

The findings suggest that gaining value from individual AI applications and preparing to operate them more broadly are separate challenges. Reported returns alone do not establish that an organization has the foundations to sustain or expand those results.

But it happened nonetheless.

“This was the first one that had the bones that Katherine wanted,” recalls Gauthier, who runs the firm GAuthier Architects. “It follows the classic real estate rule of buying the worst house on the best block.”

The strategy paid off: Once their offer was accepted, the couple embarked on an 18-month-long odyssey that saw them jump through a series of hoops (preservationists; concerned neighbors; skeptical engineers) to completely transform the moldering pile into a creative haven for them to live, work, and raise their daughter.

Yet, Keltner and Gauthier saw an opportunity, not to recreate the past, but rather forge their own set of rules.

“The house wasn’t burdened with pristine historic details—our moldings were trashed,” says Gauthier. “There were a few things that we kept and celebrated, but to me, it was like a tabula rasa. We can just go in and make it ours.” Katherine agreed: “I feel like sometimes having to preserve things is more expensive than just starting clean,” she says. “And so I thought that was an advantage for us.”

Not having to be too precious with ornamentation allowed the couple to make a bold statement about crown moldings, eschewing tradition and installing curving corners and coved ceilings instead. Nowhere does the effect have more impact than in the parlor, where Gauthier devised a ceiling light in the shape of a giant oval that casts light upward rather than down. “I really like indirect lighting,” he says.

Crafted out of laser-cut plywood, the elliptical fixture is an abstracted homage to the ceiling medallions found in neighboring historic brownstones. “Some of them are incredibly ornate and beautiful, but they don't really have any use—they’re just decoration,” says Gauthier. “Adding ambient lighting to the living space takes that historical reference in pocket.”

Once they were through the process of changing the structure’s zoning from an SRO to a single-family home, Gauthier and Keltner had to contend with its landmarked status, meaning a series of approvals were required for any additions or exterior modifications.

The couple originally planned to construct a three-story extension in the back, but they ended up compromising on two floors. This expansion includes a bump-out for the parlor floor’s kitchen, leading to a rear deck that steps down to the garden. On top, a private terrace connects to the primary bath. “Instead of having that third story, we now have a deck off of our primary suite,” says Keltner. “It’s kind of amazing,” adds Gauthier.

One decision the couple wrestled with mightily was whether to replace the original, 150-year-old pine flooring with ebony-stained white oak. “The floors were part of the reason we liked the house so much,” says Gauthier. But their contractor talked them out of keeping them. “It’s a lot of extra work for them,” says Keltner. “They have to take it up, save it, put something else down, and then put it back. I think we reached a point in time and money where we were like, Okay, fine.”

Another flooring regret was not adding underfoot heating. “I was pushing the mechanical engineers to do radiant heating and radiant cooling,” says Gauthier. “Radiant heat is really lovely—you have warmth under your feet all the time.” But the mechanics behind the system proved complicated and technically redundant, as the home was already configured with a forced-air HVAC system.

“In doing research and other projects, it’s clear that the cost-to-pleasure ratio would have been worth it,” he says now. “We regret not putting the warm floors in.”

While windows might sometimes be an afterthought, Keltner and Gauthier have advice: Go with triple-glazed. “Windows provide heat and cool insulation as well as security,” explains Keltner. “And they can define both the outside and the inside of a space.” The couple went with triple-glazed windows from Austrian-based firm Bewiso that come pre-wired for easy connection to alarm systems. “They’re almost like hurricane windows,” she says.

“You can't break through three panes of glass,” says Gauthier. Due to the building’s landmark status, they had to use historic-style windows on the front facade. “We were able to get these old-fashioned type of windows that when you close them, they feel like you're closing a Mercedes,” says Gauthier. “They completely silence the outdoors.”

The final piece of advice is perhaps the most crucial: “Hire the best contractor you can afford,” says Gauthier.

“The typical advice is to get a few bids and throw out the lowest and the highest,” says Keltner. “People usually take the second lowest.” However, that could be the determining factor between an on-time project and one that drags. “You’ll end up paying the difference both in money and time,” she says. “It’s often hard for people to bite the bullet and go with the slightly higher price, but it really pays off.”

#7008. Inside the Lavish Estates Where The Gilded Age Was Filmed

These locations are straight out of 1880s high society.

Why should the Brits have all the fun? The writer of Downton Abbey, Julian Fellowes, went stateside with the HBO series The Gilded Age, which tells the story of New York City high society in the 1880s amid the halcyon days of the industrial boom. The series explores the tension between old-money elites and their escalating feud with the nouveau riche neighbors who threaten the established social order. Meanwhile in real life, powerhouse families like the Vanderbilts and the Carnegies left their opulent footprints on Newport, Rhode Island, in the form of their famously ostentatious and absolutely gargantuan “summer cottages.” Circumstances which lend themselves to set-design magic.

In honor of season four of The Gilded Age, which premiered November 1, we’re revisiting the real-life settings that have helped bring the series to life. The social alliances may shift, but the architecture remains a dependable scene-stealer. Below, tour 21 properties that rank among the East Coast’s architectural crown jewels, from historic house museums to lavish mansions.

Features as: Bertha Russell’s ballroom and George Russell’s bedroom, as well as the billiard room.

These so-called dollar princesses and their families lived in Manhattan and spent their summers in Newport, Rhode Island. Of course, these industry magnates didn’t consider their infrequent visits (some only came a few weeks in a year) as a reason to exercise restraint. The mammoth Breakers mansion is one of four so-called summer “cottages” (read: castles) that were completed just blocks from each other in a building boom between 1892 and 1902. The mansion was designed by Richard Morris Hunt in the Renaissance Revival style, as a less-than-subtle symbol of the Vanderbilt family’s social and financial preeminence in the Gilded Age. It was architectural one-upmanship that is considered the grandest of all the manses, featuring ornate ballrooms, extensive art collections, manicured gardens, and the latest (then) in modern technology, including electric lights. It has 70 rooms, an interior using marble imported from Italy and Africa, and a library mantel purchased from a château in France.

“When you walk into the dining room of The Breakers, with its painting of the goddess Aurora on the ceiling 50 feet above you or see 500,000 cubic feet of marble in a variety of different hues, you can’t help but be awestruck by the incredible wealth of these Gilded Age tycoons,” says Trudy Coxe, the chief executive officer and executive director of The Preservation Society of Newport County, which has under its stewardship six of the grand mansions featured in the series.

The preservation society worked closely with the filming crew to ensure the safety of the objects and spaces. “We had long discussions about the billiard table at The Breakers,” she explains. “It could not be removed, but it was important to the scene being filmed. Rather than moving it, we worked with their props department to put an alternative covering on the table to simulate an actual game of pool.”

In the series, the Breakers’ neo–Italian Renaissance great hall and music room are featured as Bertha Russell’s (played by Carrie Coon) ballroom, which we see in the final ball scene. George Russell (Morgan Spector) also discusses business while playing billiards in the mansion’s massive billiard room.

For an American house, it was unparalleled in opulence, with a temple-front portico resembling that of the White House, which had been completed 92 years earlier in 1800 (lofty, no?).

Mrs. Russell’s bedroom, built on a soundstage in Bethpage, New York, was inspired by real-life socialite Alva Vanderbilt’s bedroom in the Marble House. #7001. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. What to Look for in the Most Comfortable Mattress

There are several qualities to take note of when you're shopping for a comfortable mattress. They are:

Mattress Type: You will notice that the mattresses on our list are either foam or a hybrid. Foam mattresses are made of memory foam and other layers of foam without any springs or internal structures. Meanwhile, hybrid mattresses typically have coils or an innerspring along with a comfortable layer on top. This layer can be made from various materials, from polyfoam or latex to memory foam. One type isn't better than the other since personal preference will play a role here.

Memory foam is known for conforming to your body's shape and cradling you while providing support. You don't have to worry about squeaky springs (there are none), and the dense foam layers make it hypoallergenic because dust and mites will find it hard to penetrate the deep layers. Some drawbacks of memory foam, though, include trapping heat while you sleep and less edge support. This means you sink in if you sit on the edge of the mattress since memory foam doesn't have coils for support.

On the flip side, since a hybrid has a coil support system, it will feel sturdy and supportive. There's less chance of your mattress sagging over time, and hybrids can support heavier weights. The coils, too, allow for more airflow, so your mattress holds in less heat. Some downsides to hybrids: they are often more expensive than foam mattresses, and they are usually heavier to maneuver.

Firmness: Everyone has a different tolerance for firmness; some prefer a softer feel, while others like a rock-hard mattress. Firmness is calculated by how far one sinks into a mattress. And remember: firmness does not equate to support. The latter is determined by how the mattress helps a sleeper maintain spinal alignment. So, you can have a firm bed but not have it be supportive for your back. To test for support in person, lie on the mattress for at least 10 to 15 minutes—the mattress should push back slightly to support your body. You will know if the mattress supports your body or not after sleeping on it for a few nights. If you get aches and pains anywhere, that means the mattress is not supportive.

Temperature Regulation: If you're a hot sleeper, you know how important it is to find a mattress that is more cooling, which often comes in the form of a hybrid model. (The coils don't trap as much heat as multiple layers of an all-foam mattress.) Look for a mattress with elements like a breathable cover made of cotton or natural latex that regulates body temperature. Mattresses with gel-infused memory foam are also good indicators that they'll cool you as you sleep.

Motion Isolation: If you sleep alone, this probably isn't important to you, but if you sleep with a partner or pet, pay attention to this category. A mattress that can reduce motion transfer will help you get a good night's rest, whether your partner is getting out of bed early for a workout (and you want to sleep in) or your pet is jumping on/off the bed throughout the night. All the picks on our list have excellent motion isolation, but a simple test you can do at home is to put a cup of water on the bed. Press your hands down on one side or even dramatically plop onto the bed—if the glass of water doesn't spill or the liquid barely moves in the cup, the mattress has good motion isolation.

Trial Period: Almost all mattress brands will offer a lengthy trial period for testing out your new piece. You can't sleep on a mattress for a week and expect it to still feel the same months down the line! We love brands that offer a year-long trial, but chances are, when you buy a mattress and it works, you likely won't go through the hassle of returning it (unless it is really terrible!). Make sure to check the return policy and how to return your mattress if needed, as this can affect whether or not you want to buy it.

If you're a heavier sleeper, you may want to opt for a hybrid since the coils will provide more support. If you like your mattress to feel very stiff, then an all-foam option may not be the best for you. Body Type: Similar to how your height affects the type of mattress you buy (you don't want your feet dangling off!), your weight will also factor in the purchase decision. Sleepers who weigh 130 pounds or less can handle a softer mattress because they don't sink too deep into the mattress. If you weigh between 130 and 230 pounds, go for a medium-soft to medium-firm mattress. For those who weigh more than 230 pounds, a firmer mattress with extra support is ideal.

Chances are, if you're looking for a comfortable mattress, it's because you've been sleeping on a mattress that gives you back pain, or you have a pre-existing condition that makes picking a mattress trickier. Here, we break down what to look for if you have:

#6968. 4 Weekend Decluttering Habits That Make Small Spaces Feel Bigger

A few smart systems can keep your counters, closets, and junk drawers in check.

The easiest clutter to manage is clutter that never has a chance to form. And in small spaces like galley kitchens and studio apartments, mess piles up naturally on any flat surface. Keeping it under control is less about “magic bullet” tips and more about systems you can actually keep up over time. So when we asked the professional organizers about how to declutter small spaces, the answer was in mindset and habits. If you can limit what comes into your space in the first place, you can better control its organization.

“I could say, ‘Grab a garbage bag and pick up all of the detritus left in the wake of a busy life and then put everything else where it belongs.’ That’s obvious,” Gayle M. Gruenberg of Let’s Get Organized says. “The hard part is actually DOING it. It requires motivation, presence of mind, initiative, and the necessary tools.”

Below, find the pro-approved habits that make a difference when you have more stuff than your space can hold. You can start these routines in just one weekend, and it’s far more attainable than tackling the massive pile in the garage.

Everything needs a home in your house. That’s the power of a well-placed “drop zone.” Even in small spaces, a catchall tray, bowl, or mini basket belongs at entryways or places of transition. A hook for your keys and bags when you come through the door, a bin for loose throw blankets, or a basket for remote controls on the mantel.

Throughout the day, it can take quite a bit of mental load to always put everything back when you use it. That’s where Cathy Orr, cofounder of The Uncluttered Life, recommends having a “collection basket.” It’s just one catchall container, where you can drop miscellaneous misplaced items that you pick up throughout the day. At the end of the night, take about 10 minutes to go through the collection basket and return everything to where it belongs. It’s just one small habit, rather than something to keep up with all day.

Impulse shopping lends itself to clutter very easily. The “One In, One Out” rule can curb this. It works like this: for every new thing you want to bring into your home, one thing has to go. Greenhalgh suggests checking your closet, fridge, and pantry before you shop—going through your back stock will save you both space and money.

“In small spaces, items accumulate very quickly,” Macie Kreutzer, founder of For the Love of Simple, says. “Before shopping for new items (home goods, clothing) clear out at least one item from this category. Don't bring in more than your space can physically contain.”

Once you have everything you’re ready to get rid of, make the donation process as seamless as possible. Fill your donation bags and boxes in the hallway or in the car. This encourages you to actually finish getting rid of the clutter, rather than letting them collect dust in the garage.

“Nothing gets to wander back inside just because it once lived there,” Carroll Cartwright, professional organizer and owner of Neatly & Co., says. “And this is why I love moving everything out of the space and into the hall or a separate area first. The things you decide not to keep are already halfway out the door. Keep them moving.”

It is so tempting to take advantage of the freebies in life. But this is how linen closets and kitchen drawers get quickly overrun. Unless there’s a specific and immediate need for them, you’re better off getting rid of them.

“They are usually cheap and break easily anyway,” Diane Greenhalgh, owner of Tiny to the Max, says. “That includes airplane and hotel freebies, special event or conference swag, and extra takeout plastic utensils and condiments. Your junk drawer will thank you.”

If you already have a drawer overflowing with freebies, it’s time to start culling and evaluating. You might part with two or 10 things, or you might choose to keep it all. “The point is that you’ve looked at every single item and made a decision about it, instead of continuing to store things simply because they were already there,” Cartwright says. “More likely than not, you’ll find something that makes you say, ‘Why on earth do I still have this?’”

The advice your grandma gave you was spot-on: It’s easier if you wash the dishes as you cook. That same principle applies to keeping your small space tidy. Mess feels larger with less square footage to work with, so once you’re finished with something, put it away.

“Put away your makeup after you've applied it in the morning, stack dishes in the dishwasher and wipe down surfaces after each meal, and tuck away work or homework into a drawer once you're done for the evening,” Greenhalgh says.

For the days you can’t clean as you go, Cartwright suggests spending 10 to 15 minutes a day “closing your circles.” It’s what helps your home stay clean over time, instead of just for a few weeks. This period is when you put the scissors back in the drawer or hang up your jacket.

“Most clutter is just a collection of unfinished tasks,” Cartwright says. “This small commitment allows you to wake up to order instead of yesterday’s chaos, and that is a much better way to start the day!”

#6965. Irish Designers Share Their 7 Coziest Style Secrets That We Should All Be Stealing

Here’s how to embrace that “Green Wave” with your interiors.

Over the past few years, Americans have become notably charmed by Irish culture—even the L.A. Times coined it a “Green Wave.” From splitting the “G” when drinking a pint of Guinness and bragging about it on social media to spotlighting Irish celebrities like author Sally Rooney, musician Hozier, and actress Saoirse Ronan, we as a society—myself very much included—have become a bit obsessed with the Emerald Isle, and I think it’s high time we let this Green Wave roll into the interior design scene.

If you’ve ever had the privilege to visit a home in the Emerald Isle, you’ll know that Irish spaces are warm, welcoming, and lived-in, with many of the design elements specifically used to prioritize comfort and community, something American homeowners have begun adopting. “Irish homes have traditionally needed to be comfortable, functional, and adaptable to a climate that can be cold, damp, and unpredictable,” Galway-based designer Niamh McKenna tells House Beautiful. “Traditionally, our homes are also much smaller, so beautiful rooms must also work for everyday life.”

However, it’s not just the environment which influences their interiors. Keep reading to find out the quintessentially Irish design details that local designers love to celebrate and believe American homes should borrow.

During the Georgian era, which spanned from the early 1700s to the early 1800s, Ireland experienced a population boom. During this time, Dublin also underwent a sizable redevelopment when it comes to architecture and urban infrastructure, according to History Ireland. It’s these elegant details that designer Julianne Kelly loves so much and thinks others should adopt.

“There is so much history in these houses and the proportions are beautiful: high ceilings, ornate cornicing, original sash windows, fireplaces, and rich paneled doors,” Kelly says. “These details give a room a sense of history and permanence.”

The Merrion Square area in Dublin, she adds, is a great place to walk around to view some of these rich elements in person, specifically the iconic painted Georgian doors.

Once you leave the streets of Georgian Dublin, you’ll find the sprawling countryside with “a beautiful, varied landscape that is impossible to ignore when designing the interiors for a house,” Kelly explains. She uses many natural elements found just outside the window in her designs, like greenery, stone, timber, and natural fibers like wool and linen that feel authentic to the country and only improve with age. Colors inspired by the scenes outside help the interior feel connected to the earth as well.

The textiles specifically add much-needed warmth and coziness to a room. “I love these as they add personality, a sense of calm, and an old worldliness that doesn’t look over manufactured or brand-spanking new!”

“The fireplace has traditionally been at the heart of the Irish home, and I think that idea is still incredibly relevant,” Kelly says. Ornate or simple, stone or tiled, wood-burning or gas—the details of the fireplace don’t really matter, as long as it serves as a natural focal point and the furniture is arranged around it.

McKenna adds that centering the hearth rather than the TV promotes conversation and connection. “It’s a reminder that a room can be designed around how people gather and interact,” she explains. The energy and warmth of a fireplace, even as modern homes move away from open fires, brings comfort and beauty to any room, and that’s something worth preserving.

Step into an Irish person’s home, and you’ll likely find a lot of old mixed in with the new. “Irish people love to hold onto their items,” Kelly says. “I’m not sure if it’s due to our famine history, but on most projects I work on, people have sentimental items that I have to work with.”

The designer will always try to work with these existing pieces to come up with some sort of eclectic mix of modern and vintage. She loves how unique and personal these homes turn out to be; they tell a great story. “And the Irish love a good story,” she adds.

In Ireland’s rainy climate, a home’s interiors—especially practical, hardworking spaces—need to feel both beautiful and comfortable.

“Bathrooms and kitchens are among the hardest-working parts of our homes, and we see it important to give the most functional part of our homes the same thought and attention as our living spaces,” McKenna explains. “We love to connect the indoors to the outdoors in these spaces, by using natural materials, earthy colors, and organic textures to create an interior that is as beautiful to experience as it is practical to use.”

Much of the residential architecture in Ireland is designed to work with the chilly, wet climate. “Traditional Irish architecture features deep window reveals, thick walls, and smaller windows than what might be seen elsewhere,” McKenna explains.

These choices could easily make a home feel dark and cold, but the designer says that when oriented correctly to the outside light, these elements only add to a cozy, warm environment in the winter and a cool place for relief in the summer. “I love that these characteristics also create a depth, shadow, and texture that are difficult to replicate with decorative finishes alone,” she adds.

Similarly to the architecture, the interiors are decorated to account for the damp climate, as well. “We make comfort a priority rather than a finishing touch, by layering furnishings such as window treatments, tactile rugs and cushions, and blankets,” McKenna says. Even if someone were to decorate their home rather minimally, the layers of tactile fabrics found in Irish homes still ensure the space feels inviting and lived-in.

#6961. 5 Common Mistakes That Are Making Your Vintage Decor Look All Wrong—and How Designers Get It Right

Experts say the best rooms mix eras, styles, and stories.

Every item on this page was hand-picked by a House Beautiful editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Pictured above: This space, designed by Sarah West, strikes the right balance of vintage and contemporary decor, proving that a mix is always better than sticking to one design era.

It’s not just the Instagram algorithm playing tricks on you: Everyone really is looking to antique and vintage decor. Homeowners are growing tired of rooms that look as though everything was bought from a single brand in one fell swoop. We’re now at a point where a collected, curated, personalized feel has replaced quiet luxury.

“I think it’s important to add at least some kind of antique or vintage piece in every room,” says Sarah West, founder of Sarah West & Associates and a designer for over 20 years. “Even if you’re doing a more modern or transitional room, a sense of history adds weight to a space.”

It’s not necessarily as easy as plopping one antique into a room and calling it a day, however, and that can feel intimidating. Even once you learn to shop for antiques and vintage like a pro, what do you actually do with those finds when you’re home?

While the designers we spoke with are quick to point out there really are no strict rules here—it’s about your style and what you love—there are a few key mistakes that pop up most often when people start styling with antique and vintage decor. These designers have the scoop on how to avoid those missteps and make your treasures, and your entire room, shine.

Committing to a massive piece of furniture or tasking yourself with overhauling an entire room with antique or vintage pieces can get overwhelming fast. You may make decisions you don’t love down the line.

If you decide you’re going to decorate a whole room in a limited amount of time, and you’re set on sourcing antique or vintage items, you’ll likely end up settling for things just because you need them, says Nancy Cavaliere, a New York–based interior decorator, stylist, and vintage collector. Go slow and start small.

“I’d rather live with an empty corner while I wait for the perfect piece than fill it with something I don’t love,” she notes. “The most interesting homes evolve over time. I think a room should tell the story of the person living in it, not the story of one shopping trip.”

Ease your way into decorating with vintage and antiques by starting small. Think one piece of art, one mirror, one vase.

“Don’t overthink it,” West advises. “Take a small piece, try it out in the room for a couple of days, see how it feels.” Do you like the piece in that spot? Does it work with other elements of the room? Does it inspire other styling choices for the space? Try moving it around and interacting with it to find its perfect positioning and see where that takes you.

As you start out getting a feel for that smaller piece, remember that just because it’s unique doesn’t mean it has to be the focal point of the whole room.

“Don’t treat vintage pieces like they have to be the star of the room simply because they’re old,” says Cavaliere. “Not every antique needs a pedestal. Sometimes the most beautiful thing you can do is let an extraordinary piece sit comfortably alongside something completely ordinary.”

Fully incorporating vintage or antique pieces into a space imbues the room with that richness and intrigue.

“These pieces give feelings to a room,” West says. “If you put something in a corner, as you transition throughout the room, it gives this element of surprise.”

This includes creating high-low mixes, says Cavaliere, who points out, “a $20 flea market find can look incredible next to an expensive designer piece. It’s all about how you put things together.”

In fact, that mix is something to strive for, says Alex Bass, founder of Salon 21, Art Advisory & Interior Design Firm.

“I love a contrast, like a room with modern architecture that leans heavily into antiques or vice versa. If you want to incorporate pieces from Pottery Barn, CB2, that’s fine, but there are also so many affordable vintage finds. And something like an antique light fixture can make all of these pieces work.”

Speaking of contrasts, all of our designer sources say a very common misconception they encounter is that you can only decorate a room with pieces from the same era.

“Just because you have a Victorian chair doesn’t mean you need a Victorian living room,” Cavaliere says. “My own home is a mix of different periods, styles, colors, and patterns, and that's what gives it personality. I love the tension between pieces that seemingly have nothing in common. And it's fun!”

Bass cites the example of Art Deco being popular right now, while articles are claiming midcentury modern is “out.” She encourages people to think of pieces less rigidly, assuming they have to commit fully to one style, or toss another style because someone declared a new one the next big thing.

“These are not ‘trends,’ these are design periods that already existed,” she explains. “It’s about having the eye to pull pieces from different periods that speak to you and make sense in your room.” You can mix Art Deco and midcentury modern, for instance, by using elements characteristic of them both, like marble, warm woods, mixed metals, and bold color pops.

There can be a fear with vintage and antique pieces that changing anything “ruins” them. But unless it’s a significantly valuable piece you’re investing in or something you plan on having appraised, Bass says, embrace the ability to make special pieces work for your space.

“Don’t be afraid to reupholster,” she says—and not just if the fabric is in poor shape. “If I buy a piece and the structure is beautiful but I want to add a contemporary fabric and print, that can make for a fun contrast with the frame.”

For example, Bass fell in love with a modular midcentury sectional, but its fabric didn’t quite complement the space she was designing, so she chose a contemporary fabric that worked with its aesthetic.

This, Cavaliere points out, frees you up from obsessing over condition when shopping for pieces. If something has great bones but its fabric or finish has seen better days—and if you love it—don’t pass it up. Remember you can reupholster, strip and stain or paint, and repair.

Just factor the cost of those fixes into the price of the item to help make your decision. Especially because, as Bass advises, it’s better to consult a professional unless you’re well-versed in certain projects. Stripping and staining a wood piece is fairly easy, but when it comes to reupholstering and other repairs, you’ll likely actually save money by outsourcing to a pro rather than potentially damaging the piece yourself.

Similarly, it’s typical for people to feel they can’t actually make use of vintage and antique pieces, because they think they’re too precious. But especially considering how well-made many older pieces are, they’re begging to be put to work. Again, it’s actually incorporating them into your everyday life that gives your room and entire home that sense of personalization, curation, and storytelling.

“One hundred years ago, people were using their silver,” West says. “In my kitchen, you’re eating with hotel silver.”

This includes getting creative with how you use antique and vintage pieces—don’t feel bound by what something is “supposed to” be for.

“One of my favorite tricks is turning vintage drapes and valances into bed canopies,” Cavaliere says. “I did this in my daughter’s room and I absolutely love it.” Cavaliere also finds unexpected uses for small vessels and decorative objects: a creamer and sugar set in the bathroom to hold Q-tips and cotton balls; dishes, trays, and crystal bowls to sort accessories in her closet.

“A vintage china cabinet doesn't have to live in a dining room just because it was designed to hold dishes,” she says. “One of my favorite tricks is using china cabinets to display and store shoes and handbags in a dressing room or closet. Why spend a ton of money on custom cabinetry when you can repurpose a beautiful vintage piece that has so much more character?”

West agrees. “Purchase an armoire, flip the doors, add mirrors, and make it a bar,” West suggests. “Find an old door knocker you love? Hang a towel off of it. Use a tablecloth to make a pillow. Tell a story.”

You start with an unlikely hero. This time around, it’s an Italian entrepreneur with a big, bold, brazen idea. He wants to make a watch, something unlike anyone has ever seen. It’ll be gold, with an angular case shaped like the porthole on a ship. Not so crazy yet. But wait. Because, breaking entirely with the conventions of the day, the strap won’t be made of exotic leather or precious metal but rubber. Humble, industrial rubber.

This is exactly what Hublot founder Carlo Crocco envisioned nearly half a century ago, and what he brought to the Basel Watch Fair—pretty much the place to be if you wanted to be a part of the business of timekeeping—in 1980. He spent three years developing the idea, all without doing a bit of market research. He just believed in his vision. And then the fair began.

“I was very anxious, because for the first two days, no one came into our booth,” he says. But the anxiety didn’t have long to fester. Word was spreading. And then? People galore. “On the third day, we realized that everyone was speaking about us because it was really something different from all the other watches.”

That first watch—36mm in diameter, with a quartz movement and a black rubber strap—quickly became a hit. Industry buzz gave way to the kind of clientele most brands can only imagine. The King of Greece bought one in Gstaad for himself and another for his brother-in-law, the King of Spain. More royals followed. (“By the way, they’re all related,” Crocco quips.)

But the biggest thing wasn’t getting insiders to a booth at a trade fair or convincing kings to strap Hublot watches—named, if you’re curious, for the French word for porthole—on their wrists. It was changing how the industry thought about the way watches are made.

“At that time, the mentality of watchmaking was very classical,” Crocco says. “They couldn’t think, normal people, of putting together rubber and gold. I personally thought it was a really good idea because it was against the current. It was very risky, but if you don’t risk, you have no result.”

The result was a watch industry that completely recalibrated itself. Crocco says that within four or five years of the original Hublot’s release, the brand was already dealing with imitators. Now, the idea of combining disparate materials is firmly embedded in the minds of both consumers and designers. You can see it on watches from brands that sell at the local department store and brands that only sell to folks who haven’t seen the inside of one of those since the first time their private jet took off. Crocco’s big idea changed the whole game.

Which means it’s a pretty good time to go back to the beginning of this thing. Last month in Milan, Hublot debuted a new take on that first, groundbreaking watch. Dubbed the Hublot Classic Fusion Tributo, it’s an homage but not a nuts-and-bolts recreation. Instead of the original 36mm case, it’s been upsized to a more 2026-appropriate 38mm. And instead of a quartz movement—Crocco thought it was “more practical” at the time but has come around to the enduring appeal of mechanical construction—a self-winding HUB1711 movement with a 50-hour power reserve keeps things ticking along. Oh, and now you can get it in white gold, too.

Then there’s that rubber strap. When Crocco designed the original Hublot, he was insistent that the strap was entirely straight; he felt it was crucial to the aesthetic proposition. On the Tributo, it’s ever-so-slightly, almost imperceptibly tapered, a necessary concession to keep the proportions intact after increasing the case size (which, handily, lets that automatic movement sit comfortably inside).

But, as with the first Hublot, it’s not all about tech specs and watch-nerd particulars. It’s about the spirit of the thing. And the spirit of this watch stays true to that pioneering design. There are twelve screws around the bezel, so you can keep an eye on the hour despite the dial’s lack of indices—something Crocco says was a non-negotiable when he started consulting on the new release. The yellow gold version keeps the abyss-like black lacquer dial and the white gold throws us a tiny (and welcome) curveball by using onyx. The combo of precious metal and rubber still hits, 46 years later.

If you prod him how he feels about the way that he shifted the watch industry, Crocco won’t brag. He left Hublot nearly 20 years ago, when it sold to LVMH. He knows his idea changed things; he’s happy about it. But this isn’t his day job anymore. Still, when he’s asked if he could be considered the godfather of fusing high and low in watchmaking—and you give him a minute to ponder it—he’ll tell you the truth.

#6892. Here’s What It Takes to Keep a Pool Running During the Winter, According to Experts

Before you dive in, it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved.

When most people think of backyard swimming pools, relaxing outdoors in the summertime immediately comes to mind. But some homeowners don’t want the swimming season to end when temperatures drop. Fans of cold-water swimming are starting to consider ways to enjoy their pools throughout the winter as a part of an at-home wellness experience.

“Ten years ago, most people didn’t think winter swims were possible, especially in colder climates,” says Karen Larson, cofounder of Soake Pools. “Times have changed because these days, they’re seeing neighbors, friends, and others do it and post the proof on social media.”

The increasing interest in cold-water experiences is part of a broader wellness trend. In American College of Sports Medicine's 2026 Worldwide Fitness Trends report, cold therapies, including cold plunges, cryotherapy, and cold showers, earned the 17th spot among fitness trends for the year, up from number 20 in its 2025 report.

If you’ve previously closed your swimming pool around Labor Day but are interested in making cold-water swimming a part of your daily wellness routine, there’s a lot to keep in mind. Not every existing pool is a good candidate for keeping open for winter swimming, and it’s important to understand the risks and expenses involved. But if you’re curious, here’s what to consider about keeping a pool open year-round.

Why is cold-water swimming becoming more common? There are numerous purported mental and wellness benefits, from athletic recovery to stress relief and better sleep. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis published in PLOS ONE found that cold-water immersion led to a reduction in stress for 12 hours afterward as well as improvements in sleep quality and overall quality of life.

“Winter swimming often creates a time where friends and family can come together without distractions,” adds Larson.

That said, while swimming during the colder months has its perks, there are also several risks to keep in mind. A 2020 review in the International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health found that cold-water immersion can trigger an immediate shock response, leading to involuntary gasping, hyperventilation, and a rapid increase in heart rate and blood pressure. These reactions can make it difficult to control your breathing and increase your risk of drowning. Cold temps can also impair your muscle strength and nerve function, making it challenging to swim safely.

It’s a good idea to acclimate to the cold water gradually and avoid prolonged exposure. You’ll also want to make sure there’s someone else home while you take your cold-water plunge in the pool.

In addition, if you have underlying conditions like heart disease, consult your doctor before all else, as the cold water may place additional strain on your heart.

If you’re trying to consider whether your pool is a good candidate to keep open during the colder months, these are the things to look into.

The cost of keeping a swimming pool open for winter swimming will depend on your location, size, desired water temperature, and the type of heater you use.

“Routine pool maintenance generally remains about the same year-round, although chemical consumption may decrease depending on how frequently the pool is used,” explains Jim Eisch, Jr., vice president of operations at Pinch A Penny Pool Patio Spa. “The biggest expense to consider in the winter is heating the pool.”

For this reason, prospective winter swimmers may want to consider an upgrade to their heating system. Instead of using pricey natural gas heating or a traditional heat pump, Eisch says that inverter technology is one of the most affordable ways to maintain a swimmable pool temperature year-round.

For example, if you live in Florida and have a 10,000-gallon swimming pool, Eisch estimates your annual heating costs will be $2,467 with a natural gas heater, $741 with a traditional heat pump, and $434 for a heat/cool pump that uses inverter technology.

As aforementioned, the goal with wintertime swimming is not to be soaking in bathwater-like temperatures while it’s freezing outside. For safety purposes, winter swims are quick, and the heating system is to keep your chlorinated pool water well above the 19-degree Fahrenheit freezing point.

Unsurprisingly, a standard 14-by-28-foot swimming pool is going to be one of the hardest to use effectively year-round, unless you live in a region with a mild wintertime climate.

“You can technically use a large pool year-round, but it’s difficult to cover all its surface area well enough to hold in the heat, so heating bills can climb fast,” says Larson.

Therefore, smaller sizes like a 10-by-16-foot cocktail pool or plunge pool are better candidates for keeping open. Dedicated winter swimmers should also invest in an easy on-and-off cover—or a motorized auto cover—for added protection.

Safe pool access is just as important as any other aspect of the pool’s design. That’s why a nonslip surface, excellent drainage, and ample lighting are key.

Maybe you’ll even realize you need to pick up an ottoman to create the ultimate cozy space.

Another $0 hack? Burton also encourages her clients to “shop their homes.” “It’s practical, of course, but it's also about honoring the things they've collected and loved over the years,” she explains. “Sometimes finding an unexpected new home for a favorite piece is one of the most satisfying discoveries in a project.”

“Adding artwork, whether it's a gallery wall, a single large-scale piece, or something in between, makes a huge visual and personal impact in a living room,” says Molly Torres Portnof of DATE Interiors in New York.

Retailers like Minted and Society6 offer well-priced prints, but you can also reframe photographs and posters you already own. As long as you pay attention to scale and proportion, the ceiling’s really the limit. “Rules are meant to be broken,” Portnof says. “If it feels right and makes you happy, you're on the right track.”

If your living room is blessed with beautiful built-ins, you can fill them with treasures you already own, or buy a few new pieces for well under $100.

“Take the time to create vignettes,” advises Tennessee designer Katie Kiser. “More often than not, I find my clients have all the pieces for a well-choreographed space, but they're styled improperly.”

For an instant yet affordable upgrade, head over to your local antique store to pick up some art books, jars, or a trinket tray.
