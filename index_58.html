#7. Russia-bound ship still on fire after attack in Gulf of Finland

Finland says work is continuing on the Sparta II ship on Sunday

Rescue and firefighting efforts are continuing for a Russian cargo vessel hit by a drone attack in the eastern Gulf of Finland, authorities have said.

The ship - the Sparta II - was bound for Russia's Baltic exclave Kaliningrad when it was hit on Saturday morning, before drifting into Finnish waters. Finnish defence forces said Ukraine was likely behind the attack. Kyiv has yet to comment.

Finland's Border Guard said on Sunday that work was ongoing to make sure the cargo ship did not sink but it was not aware of any danger to surrounding maritime traffic.

Water was still being used to douse the ship on Sunday, and Finland said Estonian and Russian assets were also involved in recovery work.

Kaliningrad Governor Alexei Besprozvannykh said two drones had been used in the strike, based on preliminary information, and there was "no threat" to the territory's supplies.

"War is spilling over into our immediate vicinity," Finnish Prime Minister Petteri Orpo said. "Ukraine has the right to fight back against the aggressor."

The Sparta II was sanctioned by the US in 2022 and Ukraine earlier this year, and is run by a Russian company, according to the Ukrainian and US governments.

The company says on its website that it was established within the structure of Russia's defence ministry in 2011 and since 2015 has been operating within the military construction complex.

Finland's coast guard said it had been alerted to the attack at around 08:50 local time (06:50 GMT) on Saturday and that the cargo vessel had been located approximately 50km west of the island of Suursaari in the Gulf of Finland.

This prompted "temporary aviation and maritime restrictions" around the area, Finnish border officials said.

Shortly after the incident, Finland's defence ministry said a Russian helicopter was suspected of violating the country's airspace, potentially linked to "rescue or tracking operations".

A Russian rescue vessel approached the ship and was told by the crew that they did not need to be evacuated, Kaliningrad's governor wrote on Telegram, adding: "The crew is currently carrying out the necessary work and plans to continue on its way."

The Finnish Border Guard later said the crew had been evacuated to another Russian vessel that was now already en route to Russia. The Russian rescue vessel had remained at the location of the stricken ship.

It added that no environmental damage had been detected and that there was no danger to maritime traffic or people.

Kaliningrad is a Russian exclave that sits between Poland and Lithuania.

#5. Race to replant five South African baobabs after export blocked

The trees, intended for an unnamed buyer in Georgia, were reportedly dug up without the necessary permits

Environmentalists in South Africa are racing to replant five centuries-old baobabs after the 20-tonne trees were dug up by an exporter, who tried to ship them thousands of miles to Georgia.

The baobabs arrived at a reserve in Timbavati, in north-eastern South Africa, on Wednesday, where they will be replanted and supported by cables to help them recover. The trees had been stranded at a warehouse in Durban since July, when they were intercepted by authorities who said they had been moved without the necessary permissions.

The department of forestry, fisheries and the environment confirmed the trees had been destined for Georgia but did not name the buyer.

The case echoes an episode in Kenya four years ago, when eight baobabs were uprooted to be exported to Shekvetili Dendrological Park, owned by Bidzina Ivanishvili, an influential Georgian billionaire and the country’s former prime minister. Ivanishvili has filled the park with ancient, giant trees from Georgia, transporting them by boat along the Black Sea.

The export of the Kenyan baobabs was initially blocked after an outcry by environmentalists, who condemned their export as biopiracy, but later allowed to go ahead.

Otherwise, such exports are very rare, say experts. David Newton, programme office director for east and southern Africa for Traffic, an NGO that monitors the trade in wild animals and plants, said: “It is very unusual to see baobabs being used as ornamental plants … mainly because they are exceptionally difficult to dig out of the ground and transport, and then to successfully re-establish.”

Sarah Venter, founder of the Baobab Foundation, who has led efforts to replant the South African trees, said she believes they have a good chance of surviving if they are replanted quickly. The winter months of May to October, when the trees lose their leaves and are dormant, is a good time to move them.

“As soon as spring starts [in November], the trees use their water to flush their new leaves, and that’s when we need to get them into the ground as soon as possible,” she said.

The rescue mission has faced delays because of the challenges associated with moving such large trees, which are transported on special trucks capable of carrying abnormal loads. The five baobabs weigh between 19 and 29 tonnes, have a diameter of up to 4.5 metres (15ft) and a height of up to 12.5 metres.

Baobabs, known as the tree of life, are protected under South African law, which means a permit is required to cut or disturb them.

“A baobab isn’t just part of an ecosystem, it is an ecosystem in itself,” said Wambui Ippolito, a landscape designer, lecturer and horticulturist from Kenya. “These trees are biodiversity anchors.”

Animals ranging from bats and bush babies, to grey-headed parrots and the southern ground hornbill shelter in its cavities, while elephants, monkeys and warthogs feast on its fruit.

The trees have huge cultural importance, added Ippolito. “It has always been a sacred tree in Africa. It doesn’t matter where you go: Senegal, Mali, Ghana – wherever you go, if there is a community around a baobab tree, that tree is sacred,” she said. “Somebody’s great-great-great-great-great-grandparents sat under that tree, and it is part of human history in that area.”

Baobabs have shallow roots, which means they do not need to be planted very deeply, and these regrow fairly quickly, said Venter. Once replanted, the trees will be stabilised with cables and protected by electric fences to prevent damage from elephants.

The trees are thought to be between 200 and 300 years old. “Baobabs easily reach 1,000 years old, so they’ve got a few hundred years ahead of them if they survive,” said Venter.

A criminal case has been opened against the owner of a landscaping company allegedly responsible for the illegal harvesting and transportation of the trees.

#3. South Korea education minister resigns over exam cheating scandal

How South Korea tackles cheating on its college entrance exam

South Korea's Education Minister Choi Kyo-jin has resigned over an exam cheating scandal that shocked the nation.

He said he took full responsibility for the confusion caused by last-minute measures aimed at stopping AI-assisted cheating during Thursday's annual college entrance exam, known as Suneung.

A week ago, the top scorer in the national tax accountancy exam admitted using a mini-camera, mobile phone and AI tools. This prompted officials to act urgently to detect hidden devices, including sending emergency alerts to every single mobile phone in the country.

The Suneung takes up to nine hours to complete, and it plays a big role in career trajectories of millions of South Koreans.

South Korean high school pupils in Daegu check their answers, a day after the annual college entrance exam

"As the minister in charge, I believe I cannot escape criticism that we failed to prepare in advance," Choi was quoted as saying on Sunday by South Korea's Yonhap's news agency.

"Now that the exam has concluded, I feel a deep sense of responsibility and am offering my resignation," the minister added.

South Korean President Lee Jae Myung - who has been calling for an overhaul of the current exam system - later accepted Choi's resignation.

The education ministry earlier said 111 candidates had been found with unauthorised smart devices during this year's Suneung, which was taken by about 500,000 students.

Concerns over academic dishonesty during the fiercely competitive exam had been brewing in recent weeks, after reports of candidates cheating with the help of AI devices.

These fears came to a boil last weekend, when Han Won-seok, the top tax accountancy exam scorer in April, admitted to cheating.

His confession came amid speculation over his unusually high score, with just one out of 160 questions wrong.

"I used a microcamera to photograph the test questions and sent them to an AI, then checked my answers on my smartphone to commit the cheating," the 34-year-old said in a video message.

This prompted the authorities to introduce urgent measures, with just days ahead of the Suneung.

This included sending nationwide emergency alerts on the exam day.

Three alerts were sent between 08:00 and 15:00 local time on Thursday, causing phones across the country to blare out in unison - even if they were on silent mode, and even if they belonged to tourists using data roaming.

The phones did not ring out if they were switched off or on airplane mode.

It was the first time that South Korea used its national disaster alert system to catch exam cheaters.

Candidates also had to cover up the buttons on their shirts and jackets to block any hidden cameras, and authorities conducted inspections of eyewear.

All this made even honest test takers paranoid.

Ahead of the Suneung, one high school senior told the Korea Times he was worried his horn-rimmed spectacles would be mistaken for smart glasses.

Another told Yonhap he usually wore glasses but made an effort to put on contact lenses for the Suneung to avoid raising suspicions.

Nearly 72,000 people have been evacuated from areas of Odisha in eastern India as Tropical Cyclone Akvan approaches land. Peak wind gusts are expected to reach 70mph, with rainfall totals through Friday and Saturday potentially exceeding 200mm for northern coastal Andhra Pradesh and Odisha.
