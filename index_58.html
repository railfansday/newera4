#59. Argentinian lawyer mocks UK’s sovereignty claim in Falklands oil case

Lawyer says Britain wrong to believe in historic doctrine that crown has sovereignty over parts of oceans

Britain should not be permitted to act as a 17th-century colonial power treating the waters around the Falkland Islands as a Dominium maris Britannici, Argentina has told an international tribunal.

Marcelo Kohen, representing Argentina at a hearing in Hamburg, mocked Britain for apparently believing in the historic doctrine that the British crown has sovereignty over expansive parts of the oceans.

Argentina has applied to the international tribunal for the law of the sea (Itlos) for a ruling that would force Britain to suspend the drilling for oil in the North Basin of the Falkland Islands ahead of further arbitration.

The Sea Lion oilfield is about 130 miles north of the Falkland Islands, a British overseas territory over which Argentina has a longstanding sovereignty claim.

Argentina’s president, Javier Milei, reportedly shared among his cabinet on Tuesday a photograph of the Argentinian legal team in Hamburg, describing it as “tremendous”, adding that “we are working on the Falklands issue like no one else in history”.

Addressing the tribunal on the second day of hearings, Kohen said that the dispute over the archipelago of 700 islands in the south Atlantic – known in Argentina as the Malvinas – did not mean that the area was a “legal black hole” and that the UK could do as it wished with its natural resources.

“The fact that the parties dispute sovereignty does not turn the areas surrounding the Malvinas into some kind of Dominium maris Britannici,” he said. “We are not in the 17th century … We are in an era where we should put an end to the last vestiges of colonialism.”

Ellie Reeves, the attorney general for England and Wales, told the tribunal’s panel of judges on Tuesday that Argentina was seeking to further its sovereignty ambitions by applying for suspension of the drilling.

She claimed that such a move would kill the Sea Lion project, with “disastrous consequences” for the Falkland Islanders and the companies involved. The UK claimed that the tribunal, which adjudicates over maritime matters, does not have jurisdiction to wade into matters of sovereignty.

Kohen, switching from French to English, said the UK had failed to mention in its submissions the “elephant in the room” or the “whale in the bathtub”. The UN regarded the Falkland Islands as a non-self-governing territory, he said, and had called in 1976 for both sides to refrain from taking decisions that would unilaterally change the situation before the sovereignty dispute was settled.

The exploitation of hydrocarbons would put the islands among the “highest ranks of wealth just below the principality of Monaco” while depriving Argentina of its rights to those resources, Kohen told the tribunal.

Argentina’s legal team claimed that suspension of the drilling, which is due to start in March next year and which could double the GDP of the islands, was urgent given the irreparable damage to Argentina’s rights.

In his closing remarks to the panel of 22 judges, Mario Oyarzábal, a legal adviser to Argentina’s ministry of foreign affairs, said they rejected the UK’s attempt to characterise the proceedings as an attempt to litigate the sovereignty dispute.

He said: “The question before this tribunal is a much narrower one: whether, while the underlying dispute remains unresolved, the UK may unilaterally authorise or carry out new drilling for the exploitation of hydrocarbons in the disputed maritime area.”

Sam Wordsworth KC, responding for the UK, said there was nothing in the convention on the law of the seas, over which Itlos has jurisdiction, which obliged a state not to “aggravate” a sovereignty dispute and that the UN resolution cited by Kohen as the “elephant in the room” was not legally binding.

The UK further claims there is no need for urgent suspension of drilling as oil extraction would not take place until 2028, before which an arbitration panel would have come to a conclusion on Argentina’s wider claim that its rights have been breached.

During his recent speech at the UN general assembly, Milei described the reclaiming of the Falklands as a “national cause”. Argentina invaded the islands in 1982 on the orders of the dictator Gen Leopoldo Galtieri. The 74-day conflict ended with the surrender of the Argentinian forces and cost the lives of 649 of its military personnel, 255 British military personnel and three Falkland Islanders.

Itlos is expected to make its ruling on Argentina’s case on 28 November.

#57. Indonesia's vice-president resigns amid signs ruling alliance is fraying

President Prabowo (left) said he was sorry to lose Gibran Rakabuming Raka (right), who has been mired in a growing controversy over his high school credentials

Indonesia's Vice-President Gibran Rakabuming Raka tendered his resignation to parliament on Thursday, triggering the process of finding his replacement amid growing turmoil for the government.

Gibran had been facing impeachment calls after a Supreme Court ruling that a high-school document used during his 2024 election campaign was invalid.

His departure comes amid a faltering political alliance between his father, ex-president Joko Widodo, and the incumbent President Prabowo Subianto.

Standing alongside Gibran, Prabowo told reporters he had accepted his resignation with "a heavy heart". Upper house lawmakers will have 60 days to fill the vacancy from two candidates nominated by the president.

"[Gibran] has demonstrated a chivalrous attitude, a strong sense of responsibility, and the qualities of a statesman," Prabowo said, adding that the resignation was "for the benefit of the nation, the state, and the people of Indonesia".

"He certainly believes that he completed his education through a proper and structured process. However, we must understand that educational systems abroad differ from those in Indonesia," the president said.

Gibran has long faced controversy over whether his high school qualifications met legal requirements to run for office.

Having studied in Singapore and Australia, he had used an Indonesian equivalence certificate to demonstrate his educational credentials.

Indonesian law requires vice-presidents to have completed senior secondary school or its equivalent, but the Supreme Court found no evidence to prove Gibran had graduated.

"No evidence was found to show Gibran had a foreign diploma or certificate that could serve as the basis for equivalency with a senior high school or equivalent qualification," it said.

It also considered a Constitutional Court ruling from earlier in October which came to the same conclusion.

The Supreme Court ruling prompted more than 100 lawmakers to support a parliamentary initiative that could have paved the way for proceedings to impeach Gibran.

Gibran previously argued that undertaking university education was sufficient proof that he had completed senior high school.

On Tuesday, he asked for the chance to challenge the ruling and be given the opportunity to prove the legitimacy of his education credentials, State Secretariat Prasetyo Hadi said.

Gibran's ascension to Prabowo's ticket in the 2024 election was part of an alliance formed between his father, outgoing president Joko Widodo - known as Jokowi - and Prabowo, his erstwhile bitter rival.

Gibran was 36 at the time, and only became eligible for candidacy after a controversial ruling by the Constitutional Court, which exempted him from the minimum age requirement of 40.

But tensions between Jokowi and Prabowo appear to have flared.

Since taking the presidency, Prabowo has shown little interest in pushing ahead with Jokowi's legacy project: the construction of Nusantara, a new capital. Instead, he has focused much of the state's resources on his own flagship programme to provide free school meals across the country.

In a series of personnel reshuffles over the past year, Prabowo has also shifted many Jokowi loyalists into new roles, including long-serving finance minister Sri Mulyani Indrawati.

Last week, authorities arrested several senior officials, including presidential chief of staff Listyo Sigit Prabowo - no relation to Prabowo - on suspicions of wiretapping the president.

Prabowo sacked Listyo a day later, in what many pointed to as a sign of a crumbling alliance: Listyo, who formerly served as the national police chief, is widely considered a long-time ally of Jokowi.

Within the government, allegiances seem to be shifting. On Monday, three members of Prabowo's cabinet collectively resigned from the party led by Kaesang Pangarep, Jokowi's youngest son, citing their desire to focus on their duties in Prabowo's administration.

But neither man has confirmed the rift. Speaking to reporters on Thursday, Jokowi said he still supported Prabowo's leadership.

Jokowi also said Gibran's resignation was part of "political dynamics" and he had told his son "the position was temporary".

All this is unfolding at a particularly turbulent period in Prabowo's presidency. The free school meals programme is struggling for funding, parts of Indonesia have recently battled the country's worst wildfires in a decade, and the national stock market faces crisis amid allegations of widespread market manipulation.

Across the country, students and activists have staged demonstrations to protest against the government's handling of a range of issues, including the environmental crisis and corruption.

#36. Five new miniature frog species found in eastern Himalayas

A snail-eating horned frog discovered in India has been named Jingophrys tanogogo

Scientists have discovered five new species of tiny horned frogs in India's eastern Himalayas, more than doubling the number of known species in the group.

A team of scientists from University of Delhi and Harvard University made the discoveries after five years of fieldwork, identifying the frogs as members of the Jingophrys group.

Among them is Jingophrys tanogogo - or the snail-eating horned frog - the first frog known in India to feed on snails and the first member of its family recorded with such a diet.

The research suggest the Himalayas have played a much larger role in frog evolution than previously thought and highlight the region as an overlooked hotspot for biodiversity.

Some discoveries, such as the Jingophrys taniitatii, were named to honour the local tribes that helped the researchers

Jingophrys hrdy or the Hrdy's horned frog is among the miniature frogs discovered in India's Arunachal Pradesh state

The Himalayas, stretching from tropical forests to alpine meadows, are among the world's richest biodiversity regions.

Despite drawing interest from scientists and researchers, many parts here have not been fully explored.

The state of Arunachal Pradesh particularly has emerged as one of its least-studied regions.

Dr Sonali Garg, who led the study at Harvard University's Museum of Comparative Zoology, said the research highlights how significant evolutionary changes can go unnoticed in little-studied species.

The study - called two-fold hidden diversity of diminutive horned frogs (Megophryinae: Jingophrys) - was conducted by Garg along with her colleague James Hanken and Delhi University's SD Biju, Tage Tajo and Radhakrishna Upadhyaya K.

It was published on 11 November in the Royal Society Open Science journal.

Researchers say they will have to carry out more studies to better understand the biology of the new species, including Jingophrys siangensis (Siang horned frog)

"Some of the new species are extremely secretive and found in remote forests only during a short breeding season," says Biju.

Researchers say further studies will have to done to better understand their biology and conservation needs.

DNA analysis showed the horned frogs rapidly diversified into multiple species in the eastern Himalayas as the mountain range rose and the Asian monsoon took shape over millions of years, helping drive the evolution of the region's plants and wildlife.

The researchers said in a statement that local tribes gave them essential field support and knowledge that helped make the discoveries possible. Some of the species were named after these communities.

The Tanii horned frog (Jingophrys taniitatii), for instance, is named by combining the name of the Tanii tribe in the region with the word 'tatti' - frog in the local Apatani language.

The new species have been named Jingophrys taniitatii (Tanii horned frog), Jingophrys tanogogo (snail-eating horned frog), Jingophrys siangensis (Siang horned frog), Jingophrys hrdy (Hrdy's horned frog) and Jingophrys wularsoni (Wu-Larson's horned frog).

“It’s very important to restore the dignity of these people, which was taken from them in life and in death,” said Carolina Dias dos Reis, a government official who travelled to Boston to sign the agreement with Harvard.

The existence of the skulls was revealed in 2022, and Brazilian researchers sought their repatriation from Harvard, but said they faced a drawn-out process that only moved forward after the Brazilian government became involved in late 2024.

In recent months, concerns grew that Donald Trump’s sometimes hostile behaviour towards his Brazilian counterpart, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, could derail the repatriation. But everything worked out.

His accomplices threatened and beat victims to ensure they followed his orders. The emaciated bodies were found scattered in shallow graves and dumped in latrines. Some had been buried alive.

Mackenzie was arrested in 2023 after police received a tipoff, and weeks later authorities began uncovering bodies in shallow graves in the Shakahola forest near the coast in south-eastern Kenya.

In total, more than 400 bodies have been recovered. Many were severely decomposed and only 26 could be identified.

Mackenzie denied murder charges, saying he did not force the victims to starve, but the court rejected that argument.

The Shakahola tragedy has gripped Kenya since the discovery of mass graves in the forest more than three years ago.

The festival is part of See My World’s year-round programme showcasing pan-African arts, culture and events.

It reflects the determination among creatives and activists in Manchester to build on the legacy of the 1945 Pan-African Congress in Manchester – which connected luminaries of postwar independence movements with a shared vision of liberation – after last year’s 80th anniversary commemorations.

See My World’s CEO and artistic director, Tunde Adekoya, said this month’s film festival was taking inspiration from across the spectrum of pan-African thought, including the works of Kenyan writers John Mbiti, an Anglican theologian who died in 2019, and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o, the author of the groundbreaking 1986 book Decolonising the Mind who died last year.

Both writers rejected the colonial conceit that African thought was inferior, stressed the importance of community and saw reclaiming African ways of knowing as essential to a people’s self-understanding.

While Mbiti sought common ground between African religions and Christianity, Ngũgĩ was a Marxist. He argued colonialism was motivated by cultural and economic conquest, and that European powers used language as their sharpest instrument. Ngũgĩ famously abandoned English to write in Gikuyu, a Bantu language mainly spoken in Kenya and criticised the role missionary Christianity played in colonial rule.

Adekoya said: “It’s a day dedicated to pan-African and global majority films from the north of England with exclusive premieres of documentaries we’ve been working on, which weave together expressions of liberation through art forms like poetry and dance.
