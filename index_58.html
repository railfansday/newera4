#93. NSW passes Mount Pleasant coalmine law as Greens claim change an attempt to head off One Nation

Labor says allowing mine to increase production reflects ‘gravity of situation facing hundreds of workers’ – but critics call it ‘unprecedented and dangerous’

The New South Wales government has passed special legislation to allow a coalmine in the Hunter Valley to increase production by 32% in its final act of parliament before the March state election, in which the region will be a key battleground for One Nation.

After a marathon session in the legislative council on Friday, the Minns government and the Coalition voted at 1.36am on Saturday morning to allow the Mount Pleasant mine to increase its annual extraction rate from 12.5MT to 16.5MT until 2032.

At the start of debate, the resources minister, Courtney Houssos, told the upper house “this is a unique bill applying to unique circumstances”.

“Taking the decision to intervene in the way we have is not an easy one, but it is an important one,” she said. “And it does reflect the gravity of the situation facing hundreds of workers and their families in the Hunter.”

The legislation has drawn outcry from legal and anti-corruption experts, climate groups, crossbenchers and civil liberties organisations, after the high court ruled last month that an approval for an expansion that would increase the mine’s annual output to 21MT by 2048 was invalid.

In a 3-2 split ruling, the high court found the state’s Independent Planning Commission (IPC) failed to properly consider imposing conditions to minimise greenhouse gas emissions that would be generated by the mine when the coal is sold and burned overseas.

Despite saying he would not bypass the court’s decision, the NSW premier, Chris Minns, subsequently announced the legislation, claiming it would save 400 jobs ahead of Christmas.

On Friday, the government and Coalition used their numbers to block almost 200 amendments to the bill sought by the Greens, who called the legislation “exceptional and unprecedented and dangerous”. Animal Justice party MLC Emma Hurst also opposed the bill.

“Labor describes the circumstances as exceptional, says this case is unique, and assures the public that other companies shouldn’t expect the same treatment,” the Greens climate spokesperson, Sue Higginson, told the parliament.

“But a political assurance offers no protection against the precedent being set.”

She accused the premier of being motivated by a desire to withstand a threat from One Nation to Labor seats in the Hunter.

“It’s very clear to us why Chris Minns has gone here, and that is because we’re really on the eve of an election, and One Nation is right now his threat,” she said at a press conference on Thursday.

The Hunter region, the centre of NSW’s coalmining industry, is emerging as a key battleground at the March 2027 state election. The One Nation NSW upper house candidate and former coalminer Stuart Bonds contested the Labor-held federal seat of Hunter in 2025, winning 41% of the two-candidate preferred vote. The party’s NSW leader, Mike Newman, is running in the Labor-held lower house seat of Cessnock.

Higginson told the upper house that in bringing the bill, the government had turned its back on the advice of the state’s Net Zero Commission, which has said further coalmine expansions are incompatible with meeting legislated emissions reduction targets.

“But who seems to care any more? What does the rule of law matter when [the premier] visits the Hunter and decides he needs to do something for someone so that he can remain somewhat popular in his own mind?” she said.

The Coalition, which supported the bill, said it was “about people”.

“It’s about the hundreds of workers at MACH Energy’s Mount Pleasant mine who, since the high court handed down its decision in October, have been left wondering whether they will still have a job in the new year,” Nationals MLC Sarah Mitchell said.

She said “losing hundreds of jobs in one go would completely devastate the Hunter region”.

The legislation ties consent for the mine expansion to a jobs guarantee protecting workers at the mine. Data provided to the government by the mine for the jobs guarantee, which covers more than 720 workers, is subject to an independent audit.

At a press conference on Wednesday, Minns said the legal proceedings would return to the land and environment court for consideration, but it would be “a tragedy if, in the meantime, that community effectively shut down as a result of this decision”.

“Most of the workers from Mount Pleasant live in Muswellbrook, and if the mine closed, the unemployment rate would jump by 5% just in that small town,” he said, speaking alongside workers at the mine and the Mining and Energy Union, which has welcomed the legislation.

In a report published on Tuesday, the parliament’s legislation review committee found the Mount Pleasant bill would “effectively undermine” proceedings that were still before the land and environment court and “would undermine procedural fairness” by removing avenues for legal challenge.

That report also highlighted concerns with a so-called Henry VIII clause that the barrister and anti-corruption campaigner Geoffrey Watson warned this week posed a “serious corruption risk” to the state.

The clause, allowing the resources minister of the day to override state laws as they apply to the Mount Pleasant development, was amended by the government in the lower house. Following the amendments, it cannot be used to prevent changes from being subject to Icac investigations or to change the permitted coal production limit.

The Nature Conservation Council of NSW said the government had been “embarrassed into amending its own coalmining bill”, while the Greens said the amendments did not go far enough to constrain the power any resources minister might hold under the clause.

The Law Society of NSW said on Thursday it was concerned that without further amendments, the Henry VIII clause risked “undermining the rule of law”.

Houssos told the upper house on Friday the government had carefully considered concerns and moved amendments to the clause because it “recognises the importance of appropriate safeguards and accountability”.

One in four women around the world have no power of consent if their husband or partner demands sex, according to a UN report.

In Latin America and the Caribbean, one in 10 women are not able to refuse sex, said the report by the UN Population Fund (UNFPA) published on Thursday.

The findings align with research showing adolescent girls endure very high rates of intimate partner violence.

“These are really sad figures,” said Jean-Paul Murunga, a lawyer with the global human rights organisation Equality Now. “Especially given that the continent of Africa as a whole has adopted the Maputo protocol, which sets [binding legal standards for] equality between women and men. We still have a lot of work to do to ensure women and girls benefit from these commitments.”

The Maputo protocol, a landmark treaty adopted by the African Union in 2003, guarantees women’s rights and obliges states to protect women from violence. It affirms that marriage must be based on full consent and guarantees women’s right to health.

“The underlying principle is unambiguous: sexual intercourse without free and voluntary consent constitutes sexual violence, including within marriage,” said Murunga. “Marriage does not create perpetual or implied consent. A woman retains the right to decide whether, when and how she engages in sexual activity”.

#8. Race to replant five South African baobabs after export blocked

Environmentalists in South Africa are racing to replant five centuries-old baobabs after the 20-tonne trees were dug up by an exporter, who tried to ship them thousands of miles to Georgia.

The baobabs arrived at a reserve in Timbavati, in north-eastern South Africa, on Wednesday, where they will be replanted and supported by cables to help them recover. The trees had been stranded at a warehouse in Durban since July, when they were intercepted by authorities who said they had been moved without the necessary permissions.

The department of forestry, fisheries and the environment confirmed the trees had been destined for Georgia but did not name the buyer.

The case echoes an episode in Kenya four years ago, when eight baobabs were uprooted to be exported to Shekvetili Dendrological Park, owned by Bidzina Ivanishvili, an influential Georgian billionaire and the country’s former prime minister. Ivanishvili has filled the park with ancient, giant trees from Georgia, transporting them by boat along the Black Sea.

The export of the Kenyan baobabs was initially blocked after an outcry by environmentalists, who condemned their export as biopiracy, but later allowed to go ahead.

Otherwise, such exports are very rare, say experts. David Newton, programme office director for east and southern Africa for Traffic, an NGO that monitors the trade in wild animals and plants, said: “It is very unusual to see baobabs being used as ornamental plants … mainly because they are exceptionally difficult to dig out of the ground and transport, and then to successfully re-establish.”

Sarah Venter, founder of the Baobab Foundation, who has led efforts to replant the South African trees, said she believes they have a good chance of surviving if they are replanted quickly. The winter months of May to October, when the trees lose their leaves and are dormant, is a good time to move them.

“As soon as spring starts [in November], the trees use their water to flush their new leaves, and that’s when we need to get them into the ground as soon as possible,” she said.

The rescue mission has faced delays because of the challenges associated with moving such large trees, which are transported on special trucks capable of carrying abnormal loads. The five baobabs weigh between 19 and 29 tonnes, have a diameter of up to 4.5 metres (15ft) and a height of up to 12.5 metres.

Baobabs, known as the tree of life, are protected under South African law, which means a permit is required to cut or disturb them.

“A baobab isn’t just part of an ecosystem, it is an ecosystem in itself,” said Wambui Ippolito, a landscape designer, lecturer and horticulturist from Kenya. “These trees are biodiversity anchors.”

Animals ranging from bats and bush babies, to grey-headed parrots and the southern ground hornbill shelter in its cavities, while elephants, monkeys and warthogs feast on its fruit.

The trees have huge cultural importance, added Ippolito. “It has always been a sacred tree in Africa. It doesn’t matter where you go: Senegal, Mali, Ghana – wherever you go, if there is a community around a baobab tree, that tree is sacred,” she said. “Somebody’s great-great-great-great-great-grandparents sat under that tree, and it is part of human history in that area.”

Baobabs have shallow roots, which means they do not need to be planted very deeply, and these regrow fairly quickly, said Venter. Once replanted, the trees will be stabilised with cables and protected by electric fences to prevent damage from elephants.

The trees are thought to be between 200 and 300 years old. “Baobabs easily reach 1,000 years old, so they’ve got a few hundred years ahead of them if they survive,” said Venter.

A criminal case has been opened against the owner of a landscaping company allegedly responsible for the illegal harvesting and transportation of the trees.

The speed at which AI investment has been growing - with more than $1 trillion spent in the last year alone by a small handful of major tech companies - has led to ongoing concerns of a financial bubble. Questions are being raised over whether those investments will pay off, and when.

Investors were also spooked after the Democrats' resounding win in the US midterms in early November, which sparked fears of a reduction in AI development.

Despite a positive first day of trading, Anthropic's shares fell on Friday, dropping at one point by more than 12% to about $85, close to its initial public offering (IPO) price of $83. It closed down 8.5%, reversing half of the gains made on Thursday.

The artificial intelligence (AI) company's introduction to the Nasdaq stock exchange on Thursday was a crucial test for the industry and was largely seen as a success, with trading starting at $111.50 before easing to end the day up 17% at $97.17.

Some have also noted the similarities with Elon Musk's SpaceX, which at the time of its debut in June was the largest IPO on record. Shares in the rocket and AI company experienced large swings in its first few weeks of trading.

Musk's firms Tesla and SpaceX both experienced falls of more than 7.6%. SpaceX shares were hit after it announced a bond sale to finance what the company said was the largest purchase of Nvidia chips on record.

Meta closed down more than 5% and Amazon was down more than 4%, while Apple and Alphabet experienced smaller falls.

Europe's main stock markets - which contain fewer tech stocks - also took a hit.

Germany's Dax index closed down 2.6%, France's CAC 40 was down 1.9% and the UK's FTSE 100 ended trading 1% lower.

Earlier on Friday, tech-heavy Asian markets saw steep falls.

South Korea's Kospi and Japan's Nikkei 225 indexes closed down about 5%, with shares in memory chip makers Sk Hynix, Kioxia and Tokyo Electron, and other tech firms such as Samsung Electronics, seeing losses.

Taiwan's stock market dropped about 6% on Friday, taking the weekly fall to more than 17% and what was reported to be its biggest weekly fall since 1990.
