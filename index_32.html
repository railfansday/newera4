⚖️ CFTC Clears Stock Perps, Gets Sued the Same Day

A perpetual future is a leveraged bet on a price that never expires. You hold it as long as you can meet margin and keep paying a funding rate. It’s analog product would be options in TradFi, with a few differences (primarily no expiration date). Crypto traders have used them for years, mostly on offshore venues like Binance and onchain platforms like Hyperliquid, because U.S. law had no clear home for the product.

On Tuesday the CFTC said Americans can now trade them on individual stocks.

Hours later, Wall Street sued to stop it.

The approval came as a statement from CFTC Chairman Michael Selig. Equity perpetual futures are now tradable on CFTC-registered exchanges, classified as security futures products under the Commodity Futures Modernization Act of 2000, which puts them under joint CFTC and SEC oversight.

The list of venues that can actually do this is short, and it was assembled quietly. A firm needs to be a CFTC-designated contract market and separately notice-registered with the SEC as a national securities exchange for security futures products. Seven entities hold both today: Cboe, CME, Coinbase, Kalshi, Bitnomial, Nadex, and ICE Futures U.S. Five of those seven filed inside a single 30-day window in September.

Kalshi is furthest along, with a filing covering 56 to 58 names and a launch expected within days. Coinbase has filed to list perps on Apple, Tesla and Nvidia. So those two are immediate winners. But Hyperliquid and Lighter, the top crypto perps protocols, are next in line for potential CFTC approvals and are likely future winners as well (LIT +10% on the day).

Then came the lawsuit. A new group called the Capital Markets Alliance filed in the Southern District of New York, naming Cboe, Citadel Securities and the Healthy Markets Association among its members. Its argument runs on two tracks.

The group wants the policy eliminated. The American Securities Association piled on separately, pointing out that Congress banned selling security-based swaps to retail investors at all.

A CFTC spokesman called the suit “troubling,” adding that “our markets should be governed by law, not litigation campaigns intended to block innovation.” The CME already tried this once, suing over the CFTC’s crypto perps approval on the theory that perpetuals are swaps rather than futures. That one went nowhere.

Well, remember that old phrase: “First they ignore you, then they laugh at you, then they fight you, then you win."

We’re at the fight stage. Next stop, winning.

CREATINE IS THE prom king of social media—everyone’s talking about it. And not always nice things, either. Between this polarization, there’s the seemingly reasonable personal stance of Andrew Huberman, PhD: Take creatine, but also take a creatine break.

Just in case you’re unfamiliar, a Huberman is a neuroscientist and associate professor in the Departments of Neurobiology and Ophthalmology at Stanford University School of Medicine. And he only hosts one of the most popular health podcasts of all time, Huberman Lab. When Huberman says anything about anything, people listen.

So when Huberman posted that he does a “creatine washout” every four months “just to see what effect it continues to have over time,” you have to wonder.

IT’S THE TIME period after you stop supplementing with creatine. The term is used frequently in scientific research that assesses the effects of creatine supplementation, often in athletic performance studies.

But, admittedly, there aren’t that many studies that look at the creatine washout phase itself. The most recent goes all the way back to 2004: “Effects of repeated creatine supplementation on muscle, plasma, and urine creative levels.”

In that study researchers found that levels of muscle phosphocreatine—that’s the amount of creatine stored within muscle cells—did not return to pre-supplementation baselines even after a 30-day washout period.

Since that 2004 study, there hasn’t been any supportive—or contradictory—science, but the lead author says the findings hold up more than 20 years later.

“Four to six weeks is a typical response and depends on dietary creatine, response to supplement, etc.,” says Eric Rawson, PhD, FACSM, Chair of the Department of Health, Nutrition, and Exercise Science at Messiah University, and a co-author of the 2004 washout study.

MAYBE, BUT NOT for reasons you might think.

“Questions about creatine washout and/or cycling supplementation are best handled from both physiological and behavioral perspectives,” says Rawson. “When I am asked about washout and cycling, I always ask people if they have similar concerns about their multivitamin. Or their calcium supplements. Or whey protein.”

Meaning, do you have the same washout concerns about creatine as you do your other supplements? Probably not.

“There appears to be a lingering concern about long-term creatine supplementation that is not supported by data.”

That said, Rawson says that some people, including many athletes, can feel overwhelmed by demands of maintaining their fitness edge—to the point where it’s good to take a break for mental health.

“Taking an off-season break from some of the usual routines, which includes, training, meal frequency, macro-nutrient intake goals, and the daily dietary supplement routine, can provide a break and perhaps some relief from the usual routine,” he says.

How long a break is sort of up to you and your long-term goals. But let your body and brain be the judge of that—not whatever you hear on social.

The service lets eligible firms convert securities held at DTC into tokenized “digital twins” and back again, delivering them to digital wallets of their choice. The service currently runs on the Canton Network and DTCC’s private Besu network, with more blockchains planned for 2027.

Morgan Stanley is one of 20 DTC participants that have signed up so far, alongside Bank of America, Citi, J.P. Morgan, Fidelity Investments, Citadel Securities, and UBS, among others. DTCC said it developed the service with input from more than 150 firms.

#5039. What Is x402? How AI Agents Pay With Crypto

What Is x402? How AI Agents Pay With Crypto

Nobody really built the web with payments in mind. Early HTTP specifications included a status code, 402 "Payment Required", that was reserved for a future system of online payments. Then it sat unused for about 30 years. Payments were slow and expensive, and they needed accounts, card details and middlemen who could reverse charges. None of that works for a small payment made by software.

That gap matters more now because AI agents are starting to browse, call APIs and buy services for their users. An agent can't fill in a checkout form or sign up for a monthly plan for every tool it uses. Cloudflare says more than half of internet traffic is now non-human, and it reports seeing a billion HTTP 402 responses on its network every day.

x402 is an attempt to finally give the internet a native way to pay. This guide covers what it is, how it works, who backs it, and whether there's a token involved.

The 402 "Payment Required" code has been in the HTTP standard since the 1990s. It was set aside "for future use", and x402 is the first big effort to actually use it.

x402 is an open payment standard built on top of HTTP, the protocol that moves data around the web. Coinbase launched it in May 2025 with partners including AWS, Anthropic, Circle and NEAR. It lets APIs, apps and AI agents pay for services in stablecoins as part of an ordinary web request.

x402 is not a blockchain and it is not a coin. It's a set of rules: message formats, headers and a request-and-response flow that any server or client can follow. Payments are mostly made in USDC, Circle's dollar-backed stablecoin, with Coinbase's Ethereum layer-2 network Base as the main settlement chain. The protocol works with any chain, though, and it now supports Solana, Polygon, Arbitrum and others, as well as traditional card payments.

On April 2, 2026, the Linux Foundation announced it was launching the x402 Foundation, with Coinbase contributing the protocol and companies including Microsoft, Google, Visa and Mastercard expressing initial support. The new x402 Foundation became fully operational on July 14, 2026, and now stewards the protocol under vendor-neutral governance.

This means the buyer doesn't need to hold ETH for gas.

x402's main pitch is very small payments made by machines. Settlement on Base takes seconds and costs well under a cent, so it makes sense to pay fractions of a cent for a single API call. There's no sign-up, no API key and no subscription. The agent just pays and gets what it asked for.

Adoption has grown quickly. No. x402 is a protocol, not a cryptocurrency, and it has no official native token. Payments are made in existing stablecoins, mostly USDC. Speculative "x402" tokens have appeared on various chains, but none of them is issued or backed by Coinbase or the x402 Foundation. Be very wary of any token that claims to be the official x402 coin.

To use x402, an agent or app needs a wallet holding a supported stablecoin on a supported network, usually USDC on Base. USDC is widely available on major exchanges including Coinbase, Kraken and Binance. Coinbase lets users send USDC straight to Base. In a section covering operational resilience, the agency warns that external agents operated by customers, fintechs, or other businesses could create risks for banks even when the banks don't control those systems.

It suggests examiners consider measures including identity verification, delegated permissions, transaction limits, monitoring, and the ability to suspend or revoke access.

The guidelines aim to tackle the dilemma of how to let customers use AI assistants to manage their finances without giving autonomous software unrestricted access to accounts that banks are already facing.

The new OCC guidance arrives less than two weeks after reports that major banks were discovering unauthorized third-party AI agents operating inside customer accounts, sometimes taking actions that appeared to go beyond users' instructions.

Morgan Stanley was blocking outside agents, while JPMorgan, Bank of America, and Citi were restricting access, according to an Axios report on October 22. Bank executives described the situation as "horrifying," raising questions about who controls an autonomous agent once it has access to someone's money.

The OCC's framework makes a distinction between an AI system's technical sophistication and the damage it could cause.

A simple agent authorized to initiate financial transactions may present greater risk than a more advanced model limited to writing internal documents, the regulator said.

Under the 22-page framework, examiners can consider the consequences of failure, whether an action can be reversed, and how much control humans retain over the system.

The agency outlines four illustrative risk tiers. Fully autonomous payment agents and real-time credit decision systems fall into the highest category, while basic internal productivity tools occupy the lowest.

Higher-risk systems may warrant tighter restrictions on autonomy, independent testing, and human approval before consequential actions can proceed.

The framework also addresses AI systems that continue operating while making harmful decisions. Suggested safeguards include circuit breakers, rate limits, restricted permissions, and emergency shutdown controls.

Those considerations are especially relevant to the outside agents described in Axios's earlier reporting, where banks struggled to distinguish legitimate customer-authorized software from systems operating beyond their intended permissions.

The OCC stressed that the document does not create new regulatory requirements or enforceable standards for banks.

Instead, it gives examiners a way to evaluate AI within existing operational, compliance, and cybersecurity risk categories. Banks are not required to establish a separate AI risk category.

The document uses the mandatory term "Super Intelligence," which the Donald Trump administration insists on using in place of “artificial intelligence,” but its scope includes conventional AI and “autonomous agents” rather than only hypothetical systems with intelligence beyond human capabilities.

The framework covers AI used in payments, fraud detection, customer service, and other banking operations. It also extends its risk considerations to external AI agents connecting through bank channels.

#4981. How Aussie Bitcoiners can avoid 2027’s shock CGT hike

From setting up an SMSF or a company, to just leaving the country entirely, here are some strategies Australian Bitcoiners can employ to deal with 2027’s massive CGT hike.

The Australian government’s decision to scrap the 50% capital gains discount for all investments in the May budget came as a big shock for crypto owners. The new scheme effectively doubles the taxable component of a gain, minus a small discount for inflation.

But there are legal ways to minimize the amount of CGT you’ll have to pay — if you start prepping now.

Some Aussie crypto holders are taking drastic measures — like Finder founder and crypto king Fred Schebesta who is relocating to Singapore which offers 0% CGT, stating the tax changes were a “killer blow.”But there are less dramatic methods to reduce your CGT bills including setting up a self managed super fund (SMSF), a company to trade crypto at the company tax rate, or to just cross your fingers and hope the opposition will reverse the changes after the next election.

Since 1999, Aussie asset owners have only paid tax on half of their capital gain. From July 1, 2027 they’ll pay their full income tax rate — which means 30% CGT if your income and gains are between A$45,000 and $135,000, 37% for the portion between $135,000 and $190,000 and 47% above that. (All figures in this story are in Aussie dollars)

Adding insult to injury, low-income earners who aren’t on welfare like students and retirees will pay a minimum 30% CGT rate no matter how little the gain.

The only discount now available accounts for the effect of inflation on your cost basis. So if you buy a house for $1 million in 2027 and sell it for $1.5 million after 10 years, with inflation at 3%, you won’t have to pay tax on the $344,000 or so attributed to inflation.

“The challenge for cryptocurrency is that the CGT changes, indexation really helps slower growing assets not fast ones,” explains Andrea Yuen, Swyftx CEO.

So if you bought Bitcoin for $10,000 and sell it ten years later for $100,000, the inflation discount is only around $3,439. Under the old system you would pay tax on $45,000 of gains. Under the new system you’ll pay tax on $86,561 of gains.

Stockspot analyst Chris Bryki compared how ten different asset classes would fare under the new rules. “Crypto sits at the bottom because almost its entire return comes from capital growth,” he wrote in The Australian newspaper.

Here are a few different strategies to help crypto holders deal with the changes, ranging from little to no effort, to restructuring your entire life.

Any gain you’ve made before July 1 2027 will be taxed under the existing 50% discount scheme. So your best bet is to hope a raging bull market pushes the value of your portfolio as high as possible before that date.You should faithfully record the value of every crypto asset you have that day, as any gain from that value will be taxed at the higher rate.

But Simpson (who points out he’s not a financial advisor) adds, “I probably wouldn’t be betting the farm on the fact that we’re gonna have a huge bull run”

Knowing you had to cut ties with your parent to protect yourself doesn't make the holidays any easier. Here's how to navigate the season in a way that protects your well-being and helps you regain some joy.

For many adult children of dysfunctional parents, holidays can be painful reminders of both your current reality and what you've lost. You may find yourself grieving the parent you're estranged from, but also the parent you always wished you'd had. It's completely natural to feel confused or upset; part of you may still long for a nurturing, loving connection, while another part knows that staying in a toxic relationship would only cause more pain.

Holidays can amplify these feelings because they're built around an idealized version of family that's never been your reality. You may feel pressure to attend gatherings that feel unsafe, or you may be flooded with painful memories when everyone around you seems to be celebrating. The goal is to learn to tolerate such feelings, knowing that they will lessen, and to treat yourself with compassion.

The first decision is whether to attend family events at all. This is always a choice, even when it doesn't feel like one. Choosing not to attend a gathering when your estranged parent will be there isn’t necessarily a sign of avoidance but of maintaining a boundary. If you do choose to attend, it should be because it genuinely serves you—and not because of guilt, obligation, or pressure from others.

Take time to weigh your options. Ask yourself: What's the emotional cost of attending? What do you hope to gain—and is it realistic? And will being there require you to compromise your boundaries or safety? (For people with abusive or toxic parents, skipping a holiday gathering may be the healthiest decision.)

Once you've made your decision, preparation can help you avoid reactive choices in an already emotional situation. Anxiety can build for days or weeks before a difficult holiday, so don't wait until the day arrives to think through your approach.

If you're not attending: Resist the pull to leave the day unstructured. A completely open day can intensify rumination and sadness. Plan something intentional that gives you something grounded to move toward.

If you're attending: Go in with a clear plan so you feel more grounded and in control.

Estrangement invites you to rethink what holidays can look like. You don't have to organize your life around traditions that cause pain; giving yourself permission to step away is progress in itself.

Think creatively about how you want to spend the holiday. Start new traditions, volunteer, or do something you genuinely enjoy but rarely make time for—a hike, a movie, or a good book. It's also completely fine to use the time to rest, reflect, practice self-care, do chores, or pursue hobbies. There are no rules: How you spend the day is completely up to you, whether that means making new meaning out of the holiday or simply getting through it in whatever way feels manageable.

#4906. Many Women Over 40 Avoid Jumping Workouts—But This Simple Exercise Is the Key to Stronger Bones

And you only need a small dose of plyo to reap the benefits.

THREE YEARS AGO, at 52, Tina Tang could deadlift 310 pounds without blinking and squat 210 like it was part of her warm-up. She had discovered powerlifting in her early 40s, and by all appearances, she was the picture of strength.

So when her annual physical revealed osteopenia—low bone density—she was stunned.

As a coach who trains women over 40, Tang believed she was doing everything right for her bones: lifting heavy, paying close attention to her form, eating enough protein to support muscle maintenance and growth. But one critical piece of the bone-health puzzle was missing from her routine, she would come to figure out: plyometrics.

Many women, particularly those over 40, have been told (or assume) that jumping is off-limits as they age. “The misconception is fueled by claims of increased joint pain, pelvic floor leakage, and the idea that bones become too fragile to tolerate impact,” says board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Pamela Mehta, MD, founder of Resilience Orthopedics in Los Gatos, California.

The truth: For most otherwise healthy women, plyometrics—and jumping and weight-bearing impact exercises more broadly—are among the most effective ways to protect bone health long term, says Dr. Mehta. And beyond just helping prevent bone loss, plyometric training can actually help reverse declines, emerging data suggests.

A recent review published in Current Osteoporosis Reports found plyometrics are among the most effective tools for improving bone density and reducing fracture risk in post-menopausal women. Meanwhile, a 2025 review in BMC Musculoskeletal Disorders found that when already-active people added plyometrics to their routine, they saw promising improvements in bone mineral density at the spine and hip.

Tang is living proof of these findings. At 54 years old, and just two years after adding plyometrics to her workout routine, Tang got a follow-up DEXA scan (the gold-standard test for assessing bone density) that revealed measurable improvements, including in her right hip, the area that had taken the biggest hit, per her initial scan.

“The trend is for women to continuously lose more and more bone as they age,” says Tang. Once you develop osteoporosis or osteopenia, only a small percentage of people can regain density. “So the fact that I improved mine—and plyometrics was the main thing I changed—is major,” she says.

So even though plyometrics may have gotten a bad rap among the 40-plus crowd, that reputation is rooted in nothing more than outdated pseudoscience and fear-mongering. These explosive, higher-impact exercises deliver the exact type of loading that spurs bone growth, development, and increased density.

In sum, stronger bones, better balance, faster reaction time, and a body that’s more resilient as you age are just a skip, hop, and (broad) jump away. Here's what to know—and what to do.

“Plyometrics are a category of exercises that require you to produce a tremendous amount of force in a short amount of time,” says strength and conditioning specialist Alena Luciani, MS, CSCS, founder of Training2xl. Often described as explosive or powerful, plyometrics are more impactful than traditional weight-bearing or high-impact exercises by design.

Consider traditional squats versus plyometric variations. Sure, goblet squats—which involve holding a weight in the front rack position as you sit to depth—can support bone density by challenging the musculoskeletal system, explains Luciani. But their higher-impact cousin, the jump squat, takes the stimulus to the next level. “You have to move faster and produce force much more quickly than you would during a goblet squat,” she says.

That rapid, high-impact loading is the exact type of stimulus that bones need to remodel and grow stronger, says Parini A. Patel, DO, an interventional pain and musculoskeletal medicine physician at Atlantic Health in New Jersey. In fact, recent research suggests that high(er) impact, more explosive training regimes (like plyometrics) provide even more bone benefits than traditional strength training.

One 2023 review published in the Journal of Sports Sciences observed that high-impact jumping exercise improved bone mineral density more than non-jumping alternatives.

Bones respond best when forces hit them quickly and sharply—like the impact of landing from a jump, explains Dr. Patel. “Plyometrics naturally provide these conditions in ways that low-impact activities cannot,” says Dr. Patel. Each time you land, sensor cells in the bones (called osteocytes) detect that burst of impact and signal the bone’s builder cells (osteoblasts) to lay down new bone, she explains. Over time, even short doses of this kind of impact can significantly improve bone strength.

Just as important as their impact on bone density, plyometrics also strengthen the systems that help prevent fractures in the first place. “Plyometrics also train and prime your central nervous system, helping to increase reaction time,” says Luciani. “Fast reaction time is significant for adults as they get older because it can be the difference between tripping, falling, and breaking a hip while walking on the sidewalk, and catching yourself so that the break never happens,” she says. This is key as hip fractures have been shown to increase the risk of future fractures drastically and are associated with higher mortality rates—especially in older women.

While plyo training has benefits for people across the gender spectrum, women are especially poised to reap the benefits. Women face significantly higher lifetime risks of osteopenia and osteoporosis by a large margin. (Eighty percent of all people with osteoporosis are women, studies suggest.)

One cause of this discrepancy? Hormones, says Dr. Mehta. Estrogen in particular has a protective impact on the bones; while the hormone is best known for its role in reproduction, estrogen supports new bone formation.

Unfortunately, that means that when estrogen levels drop during perimenopause and the menopause transition, the protective impact wanes and women start to lose bone mass—and quickly, says Dr. Mehta. (Men experience hormonal changes with age, too, but the effect of reduced testosterone levels on bone is far less dramatic.)

“Women also reach a lower peak bone mass in early adulthood compared to men, providing less skeletal reserve as age-related bone loss begins,” says Dr. Patel. In addition, women have smaller frames than men, and therefore are predisposed to have smaller bones, thinner cortices, and lower bone volume than men, she adds. With that, the natural declines associated with age and hormonal changes have more detrimental impacts on women over time.

Not to mention, because women live five to six years longer than men on average, “they spend more years in a lowest estrogen state," she says, which means the internal structure of the bones gradually weakens over time, raising the risk of fractures.

Women’s comparatively heightened risk for osteopenia and osteoporosis, broadly speaking, makes them ideal candidates for nearly any kind of bone-protective interventions. But plyometrics may be an especially powerful addition.

Consider this: The spine is the most common osteoporotic fracture site amongst women, while the hip is a close second. Breaks in either area can cause pain, as well as lead to immobility and loss of independence, which leads to other complications long-term. The good news is that both regions are highly responsive to plyometric training. “The hips and spine respond especially well because they absorb the most force when you land,” Dr. Patel says.

Importantly, health care providers emphasize that plyometrics aren’t meant to replace traditional strength training, but to complement it. Strength training supports muscle and tissue health more broadly, while plyometrics deliver the impact bones respond to particularly well, explains Dr. Patel. Together, the two types of loading work together to support the health of your entire frame—and research backs it up.

A study published in Applied Sciences last year found that participants who tacked on twice-weekly jump training to their usual exercise routine had improved lower-limb joint health in just 12 weeks. Meanwhile, a 2025 review in Current Osteoporosis Reports found that high-impact plyometric exercise increased lumbar spine bone mineral density in post-menopausal women. But, the authors note that the greatest benefits often appear in programs that combine resistance training with impact loading, reinforcing the idea that the two approaches work best together.

And those widespread myths that say plyometrics are off-limits after middle age? Hogwash. “Jumping does not cause wear-and-tear, worsen osteoarthritis, or increase fracture risk,” says Dr. Patel. “When done appropriately, plyometric drills are one of the most effective ways to stimulate bone formation and preserve bone density during midlife,” she says. The real risk arises from improper progression, inadequate landing mechanics, or unrecognized musculoskeletal conditions—not the jumping itself, she says.

No matter your age, goals, or training background, it’s wise to consult a physician before giving your movement practice a major overhaul, says Dr. Patel. A clinician can review your medical history, medications, bone density status, and any underlying conditions to determine whether impact training is appropriate for you at this time. Or, if you’ll need modifications.

In this challenging environment, most of us want what we’ve always wanted: to be genuinely happy. Yet we are not willing to do what it takes to become happy.

A study from almost a century ago of the residents of the Northern English town of Bolton shows us the severe effects of not carving out time for our loved ones, a decision that most of us make every day. In the year my father was born, just over 2,000 kilometers (1,200 miles) to the east in Hungary, 1938—and then again in 2014—the residents of Bolton were asked what made them happy.

The most common ingredients of happiness among responses spanning 78 years were economic security and personal relationships, which were often tied together. Little mention was made of external events.

Joseph Roberts, a miner, despite answering the survey in 1938 when the threat of Hitler loomed large—the Nazi leader had already occupied Austria and parts of what was then Czechoslovakia—shared that what made him happy was returning from a day of hard work and spending time with his family.

I am guessing you would agree that happiness is a seed you must grow in your life. Yet what most people do not understand is that you can’t grow your happiness alone; you can only do so with the help of others.

Since you cannot grow your happiness alone, it must also be true that happiness is a seed that grows in the gardens of others. How does this work?

We all seek happiness yet find it elusive. The reason is that most of us do not realize that one of the best ways to experience happiness is to help others experience it.

Plato understood this well. “Caring for the happiness of others,” observed the Greek philosopher over 2,500 years ago, “we find our own.”

Research also bears this out. Not only do people feel even more appreciated, happy, and connected to us than we expect them to when we give to them, but we also counterintuitively often benefit, as psychologists Sonja Lyubomirsky and Harry Reis note in their book How to Feel Loved, even more than they do.

I was reminded of this truth last month, when I spent nine days in a small town in rural Kenya where I had lived and worked as a Peace Corps volunteer 35 years earlier. Facilitating a pro bono leadership conference for rural primary and secondary school leaders and supporting five Kenyan women educators in teaching a women’s empowerment program for single mothers in one of Kenya’s most impoverished regions, I was struck by how happy I felt connecting with the people around me.

Returning to my comparatively more hermetic life of writing, researching, and teaching in Rome has been a jolt to my system; yet the memories remain and bring peace. In the end, I am like everyone else I’ve ever met in the world: traveling on an eternal search for connection and meaning.

Following the Italian way of expressing love, when you tell the other person “Ti voglio bene” (which means “I wish for your happiness”), you offer them the seed of happiness to plant in their own garden. You give them your support to grow in the way they desire, toward their own goals and values, or “to become,” as the former president of the American Psychological Association and the father of client-centered therapy, Carl Rogers, once wrote.

If the seed flourishes and becomes the flower of happiness, the other person may or may not bring you this flower one day. Sound risky? It may be; yet it’s the only way you will ever have the experience of seeing this flower in full bloom.

In other words, interconnectedness is the only path that will enable you to experience and sustain happiness throughout your life.

“Sounds good, Mr. Idealistic Author,” you may be thinking. “But how do I make daily decisions that will bring this interconnectedness into my life?”

If you want a more interconnected life, you will first have to ask yourself some tough questions about your daily decisions, right down to whether you should spend your time today ordering another gadget on Amazon or going hiking with a friend. Let’s consider this choice.

What do you think will bring you more happiness—an Amazon delivery or going on a hike with a friend? A study by Amit Kumar of the University of Texas has found that once you have a base level of financial resources, experiences rather than objects are the drivers of happiness.

Why? Experiences (“doing” something such as going to a meditation retreat or on a bicycle ride or to a restaurant or a concert) are more often shared, while objects (“owning” something such as a new shirt or book) are most often enjoyed alone.

In the end, the love we’ve shared with others is all we take with us. “All that we love deeply,” Helen Keller sagely observed, “becomes a part of us.” This is why experiences, which facilitate the sharing of love, produce much more long-term happiness than objects.

How, then, can you usher more happiness back into your life? Prioritize doing activities you enjoy in person with people you care about rather than buying objects to enjoy by your lonesome.

Just as you pursue objects to purchase and yet Prime shipping does not drop happiness at your doorstep, you also pursue social information about the people in your social network. As I write in my book Screened In, this search—like buying objects—does not bring the social connection that is the purveyor of genuine happiness into your life.

In sum, then, stop texting and call, go out with, see, visit, meet—in short, spend time with other, values-congruent people as often as you can.

In other words: Just. Connect. In person. In real time.

Spend time with the people you love, or could love if you were to get to know them better. Then happiness will cease to be a distant stranger and will become a part of your life again.

#4882. What Is Rhabdo? What Every Woman Should Know About the Rare Condition

A woman’s death during the New York City Marathon has put the condition back in the spotlight.

At the New York City Marathon on Sunday, 24-year-old Rachel Apa of Lewisberry, Pennsylvania, collapsed two miles from the finish line. She later died at the hospital.

The medical examiner’s office is still investigating her cause of death. Her father, Steve Apa, told the New York Times that the cause was rhabdomyolysis, commonly known as rhabdo. The rare condition occurs when skeletal muscle breaks down, releasing its contents into the bloodstream, says Robert Glatter, MD, an emergency room doctor and an assistant professor of emergency medicine at Lenox Hill Hospital.

Apa’s death comes amid growing conversations about rhabdo on social media. People have shared their experiences with the condition after intense exercise, particularly at cycling classes.

But while these stories are alarming, rhabdo is still relatively rare. Yet they raise questions about the risks of intense exercise, how to recognize when something is wrong, and just how concerned the everyday exerciser should be. Here’s what experts want you to know.

Rhabdomyolysis happens when skeletal muscle breaks down and releases its contents into the bloodstream. The resulting spill can produce dangerous electrolyte imbalances and kidney injury, particularly when someone is dehydrated, Dr. Glatter says.

Although rhabdo is often associated with intense exercise, physical exertion is just one possible cause. “Rhabdomyolysis can also follow crush injuries, seizures, prolonged immobilization, heat stroke, severe infections, certain drugs or toxins, metabolic abnormalities and, more rarely, inherited muscle disorders,” Dr. Glatter says.

Exercise-related rhabdo typically happens when an individual suddenly performs an unusually difficult, repetitive workout involving large muscle groups—particularly when doing eccentric exercise, or when the muscle contracts while lengthening. The risk may be higher when the activity is unfamiliar or far more intense than the person is accustomed to. Some examples include high-repetition squats, lunges, downhill running, and intense cycling, Dr. Glatter says.

“The key issue is often not simply that the workout was ‘hard,’ but that its intensity or volume was far beyond what that person’s muscles were conditioned to tolerate,” he says.

Symptoms include muscle pain "dramatically out of proportion to ordinary post-workout ache,” Dr. Glatter describes, as well as dark-colored urine, swelling, weakness, and difficulty moving the affected muscles. Other possible symptoms include dizziness, fainting, confusion, persistent vomiting or feeling generally ill, he says. “Someone who develops severe or rapidly worsening muscle pain or swelling after a strenuous workout—especially together with marked weakness, dark urine or substantially decreased urination—should be evaluated urgently.”

It’s important to note that symptoms can start while someone is still exercising. An athlete may notice muscle pain that feels disproportionate to effort, unusual heaviness or weakness, severe fatigue, or a drop in pace or intensity that would normally be manageable, Dr. Glatter says. In severe cases, athletes can collapse, appear confused, or develop nausea, dizziness, and swelling.

Other risk factors also include dehydration, exercising in extreme heat or humidity, a recent viral illness, certain medications or drugs, and underlying genetic or metabolic disorders, such as sickle cell.

Although many recent social media accounts of exercise-related rhabdo have come from women, it’s important to know that an unaccustomed high-intensity workout can overwhelm muscles regardless of sex. Those stories don’t necessarily reflect who is most likely to develop the condition, and studies have shown generally higher rates in men.

It’s complicated. “It’s probably a combination of greater awareness and a genuine increase in some types of exertional rhabdomyolysis, but the evidence does not support describing rhabdo as a new epidemic,” Dr. Glatter says.

Rhabdo has been around for decades, and it’s difficult to determine overall incidence rates because mild cases may never be diagnosed. Still, some research suggests that exercise-related cases have increased in certain settings. A 2022 analysis published in the Physician and Sportsmedicine looked at emergency department data from 2000 through 2019. It estimated more than 40,000 cases of exertional rhabdomyolysis and found an increase over that period.

Dr. Glatter suggests this might be, in part, due to shifts in exercise culture. An increase in the availability of fitness classes such as high-intensity interval training, cycling, and CrossFit-style workouts can expose people to unfamiliar movement patterns at high intensities.

“These workouts are not inherently dangerous, and millions of people perform them safely. The risk arises when workload increases too quickly relative to conditioning, particularly when heat, dehydration, illness, medications or inadequate recovery are added to the equation,” Dr. Glatter says. “A highly motivated newcomer trying to keep pace with a class may continue well past the point at which her muscles are accustomed to working.”

It’s also important to note: Being well-trained does lower the risk of rhabdo, but it does not eliminate it. “In a trained athlete, rhabdomyolysis is often best understood as a ‘multiple-hit’ phenomenon, where several physiologic stresses occur at the same time,” Dr. Glatter says. Someone may be proportionally prepared for the activity, but factors such as unusually high intensity or duration, heat and humidity, dehydration, recent illness, inadequate recovery, electrolyte abnormalities, and certain medications or supplements can lower the threshold for injury. Rare underlying conditions, such as sickle cell trait or certain metabolic conditions, can also predispose a healthy athlete to rhabdo.

That doesn’t mean you should fear strenuous exercise. Starting at a manageable level, building up gradually, and staying hydrated can help reduce the risk.

“The body adapts extraordinarily well to exercise when intensity and volume are increased gradually,” Dr. Glatter says. “Going from relatively little training to an all-out, high-repetition workout involving large muscle groups is a very different physiological challenge.”

Exercise-related rhabdo is uncommon, but can happen when a workout places demands on muscles that are far beyond what they’re accustomed to. High-intensity exercise can be part of a healthy routine; the key is to build toward it gradually and pay attention to how your body responds. If you develop severe muscle pain or swelling, weakness, dark urine, or substantially decreased urination, seek urgent medical care.
