#7207. Pet Brands Find New Ways to Invite the Family Dog to Thanksgiving Dinner

In 2023, pet information site Veterinarians.org polled 1,000 dog owners and discovered that 68% planned on making a Thanksgiving meal just for them.

Think that’s a lot? This year, that number is closer to nearly all of them.

Premium pet-food brand Ollie recently surveyed 1,000 dog owners and found that, as the holidays approach, 96% of dog households plan to prepare Rover “something special to eat.”

Few are more aware of this shift than pet brands, which are rolling out Thanksgiving-specific products and promotions to help consumers include dogs at the dinner table—at times literally.

Ollie—which sits in the fresh, human-grade dog food category—is offering a Holiday Feast meal made with traditional Thanksgiving ingredients like turkey, cranberries, and sweet potatoes. On Nov. 21 and 22, select Petco stores hosted Petsgiving, a cooking lesson on how to cook a dog-appropriate Thanksgiving meal.

“These are the latest in our series of retail-tainment events designed to create opportunities for connection and bonding between pets and pet parents,” Petco’s SVP of brand creative Alison Hiatt told ADWEEK.

In a similar bid to “let dogs join in Thanksgiving,” legacy pet food brand Purina recently announced a limited edition run of its Beggin’ line of treats in flavors like Bacon ’n Turkey and Honey ’n Ham. Just Food for Dogs is selling its limited-run Thanksgiving Feast Recipe online this year for the first time.

And to promote its limited-time Harvest Turkey dinners, premium brand Maev has launched its “Seat at the Table” campaign that presents dogs “as respected guests of honor at the Thanksgiving table.”

“We’re in the midst of a fundamental shift in the role dogs play in our lives, from existing on the periphery of our identity to becoming an extension of it,” said Maev’s senior director of brand Maddy Tank. “We’re increasingly bringing them into the rituals, routines and experiences that make us human.”

Holiday promos are nothing new, of course, but why the concerted effort now to give dogs a place at the Thanksgiving table? It’s a combination of several forces.

First, more people own dogs. In 2025, dog ownership in America rose from 51% to 53%, according to the American Pet Products Association. Most of that growth is coming from zoomers and millennials, and these are the generations most likely to regard a pet as a full-fledged family member—by rates of 68% and 69%, respectively, according to Euromonitor data.

“There are also fewer people having children,” Ollie CMO Allison Stadd told ADWEEK. “So dogs are increasingly filling that role that people might have filled with human children in the past.”

Even among families with children, Purina senior brand manager John Paul Vella added, “dogs have been elevated to child status among consumers.”

Finally, there’s a growing awareness that feeding fido scraps from the Thanksgiving table can actually be harmful, a concern that opens the door to these specialty meals and offerings.

Some of which can be extremely special. Ollie automatically enrolled everyone who bought its Holiday Feast online before Nov. 3 in a drawing to win a $1,700 Baccarat crystal bowl—for the dog, of course.

How did you hear about the news of Jensen Huang’s return?

I was walking my daughter to her bus stop this morning, and my phone blew up with texts from our bureau. It’s maybe the most important business story of the year, and I was a little frustrated that I was out of position when it broke. So I began texting sources for comment.

TVNewser: How did the call with President Trump occur?

Javers: He called me as I was in my car on the way to our CNBC bureau. I wasn’t expecting it at all. And your audience will understand the technical aspect of this, which is that on a phoner, you can’t communicate with the control room during the interview. Javers: We’ve really never seen anything this extraordinary in corporate America — at least in my lifetime. I think it’s too early to say how this will play out, and certainly too early to say whether it will be successful or not. One thing is for sure: This story isn’t over yet.

The higher education groups suing to strike down new limits on the Curricular Practical Training program are likely to succeed, a federal judge ruled.

Appropriations for programs such as Title I, Part A for low-income school communities ($18.4 billion), Title III for English learners ($890 million) and safe schools and citizenship education ($431 million) would stay the same as FY26 appropriations. Funding for the Office for Civil Rights would remain at $140 million, according to the spending plan.

The proposal recommends increases for several other programs, such as an additional $50 million for special education education programs ($15.5 billion), $60 million more for charter school funding ($500 million), and $5 million more for 21st Century Community Learning Centers, which help fund after-school and summer activities ($1.3 billion).

Overall, the Senate Appropriations committee’s budget proposal recommends $93.9 billion for the Education Department in FY27, a 19% increase over FY26 appropriations.

The FY27 proposal "makes meaningful investments" in programs such as childcare and education, said Sen. Shelley Moore Capito, R-W.Va., in a Monday statement. Capito is chair of the Senate Appropriations Labor, Health and Human Services, Education and Related Agencies Subcommittee.

But the full committee's vice chair, Sen. Patty Murray, D-Wash., said in a Monday statement that the larger Labor, Health and Human Services, Education and Related Agencies proposed funding bill "fails to rein in this administration — to stop the dismantlement of the Department of Education, prevent [President Donald] Trump from politicizing every federal grant, and so much more. A separate budget proposal for the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services recommends raising funding for Head Start early childhood education programs by $50 million for a total of $12.4 billion.

FY27 began Oct. 1, but before the deadline, Congress enacted a continuing resolution to delay decisions for an overall final federal budget until Dec. 11. By that date, Congress will either need to finalize the FY27 appropriations or enact another continuing resolution. If lawmakers do neither, there could be a government shutdown.

In June, the House Appropriations Committee advanced its FY27 spending plan that recommended an overall allocation of $71 billion, or a 10% cut, to the Education Department. That proposal included a $2 billion cut for Title I funding, a $46 million boost for special education programs and the elimination of the Office of English Language Acquisition.

Both the House and Senate committees' bills differ from Trump's proposed budget, which sought to cut Education Department spending by 3% to $76.5 billion by consolidating or eliminating several programs, such as English learner supports and Teacher Quality Partnership grants.

The Trump administration's spending proposal also recommended the elimination and consolidation of several Individuals with Disabilities Education Act national programs like teacher training and technical assistance, but that recommendation is not reflected in the House or Senate committees' budget plans.

The paths for the House and Senate committees' spending bills are unclear given the advancing fiscal deadline and partisan politics at play, said Tara Thomas, senior government affairs manager at AASA, The School Superintendents Association, in a Nov. 17 post on AASA’s website.
