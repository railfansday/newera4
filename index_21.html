Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This Future of Marketing Briefing covers the latest in marketing for Digiday+ members and is distributed over email every Friday at 10 a.m. ET. More from the series →

SharkNinja got a taste of F1 fandom when its products turned up in Brad Pitt’s racing film last year. Fans warmed to the brand and plenty finally twigged that Shark and Ninja were the same company. Then the buzz faded.

“The movie was great but it spiked and then it left,” said Laura Dyer, svp of global brand and partnerships at SharkNinja. “What’s great about this is it’s going to continue.”

“This” is a five-year sponsorship of McLaren and it got off to an early start. F1 sponsors tend to arrive over the winter, ready for their logos to be unveiled on the new car in February. SharkNinja turned up in mid-September, with most of the season already run, because it asked McLaren whether anything could go live before the year was out. McLaren said yes, which hands the brand a few months of practice ahead of 2027.

The practice matters since SharkNinja has never backed a sports property this big. Its previous sports deals were built around a single moment. At the 2025/1015 Women’s Rugby World CUp it was an official appliance partner, running standards at match venues, a fan zone at Battersea Power Station and a social series on matchday prep. A year earlier it put Thierry Henry, Peter Crouch and Bastian Schweinsteiger on a faux video call to squabble over who could host the best Euro 2024 watch party. Both were finished within weeks. McLaren, on the other hand, gave SharkNinja four racing series for five years. McLaren engineers in its labs and a direct line to drivers Lando Norris and Oscar Piastri.

That last part is what Dyer is most focused on. Where Norris and Piastri do appear, she wants them helping shape the idea, which is a change from the old sponsor routine of a driver turning up, reading the lines and leaving. “Getting them to do the things that they’re comfortable with is way better than handing someone a script and saying ‘there you go’,” Dyer continued. Fans follow the drivers as closely as the teams these days so keeping both onside matters. That goes double for a brand hoping to win those fans over on social media.

Most of that content will run on Mclaren’s channels and SharkNinja’s, and nothing goes live until both sides have signed it off. Even then, it won’t automatically land on Norris or Piastri’s accounts. “Their channels are theirs to own and theirs to editorialize,” said Dyer. If Norris or Piastri’s post about a blender, it’ll be because they fancied it.

That puts the onus on the content itself, though Dyer was frank that SharkNinja’s yardstick for it is still rough. Her answer on engagement, she warned, would be “really wooly.” Comments, shares and dwell time all go into the mix, weighed together, with reach a step behind. F1 and McLaren fans are fiercely loyal and Dyer said the brand is “really respectful of that”. Success as she put it, is a fan seeing a post and thinking. “‘oh yeah, like wicked. I totally get it’. Rather than this feels like advertising.”

Further out, SharNinja will track brand recognition and whether putting McLaren behind a specific product line shifts sales. As Dyer explained: “When we go hard at a product line with McLaren, we want to see whether or not that changes the trajectory of sales.”

Down the line, McLaren could even have a hand in the products as well the marketing. A set number of hours a year, the team’s engineers sit with SharkNina’s in Needham, Massachusetts, to “literally pen to paper solve problems together,” as Dyer put it. Battery life is one area they’ll dig into. Whether anything ends up with McLaren’s name on the box is an open question, and one Dyer didn’t answer. The engineering link was a big part of why SharkNinja picked McLaren, though. The brand is “really obsessive about the concept of marginal gains,” she said. “If we can get a battery to last like 50 seconds longer or get a fan to be 10% more efficient, we’ll do it.”

Sports sponsorship has been heading this way for a while. Live sport is one of the few remaining moments when a huge audience watches the same thing at once, and brands have been paying more to be part of it. What they’re buying has changed with the price. A logo on the hoardings and ad slots in the broadcast used to be the deal. These days marketers want to know what the fans themselves are worth, whether those fans follow the team or the people in its cars and kits.

“We operate more like an agency than a lot of traditional rights holders in that we insight on entering every conversation talking about the brand first,” said Ryan Fischer, president at Fenway Sports Management on an Advertising Week New York panel earlier this month. [We’re] understanding their objectives and strategies, then trying to identify the insight, whether that’s a consumer insight or a story we can help amplify, and letting that lead where we go within the portfolio rather than starting withoutselves.”

SharkNinja’s appearance in the F1 movie predated Dyer’s arrival but she was there to help execute it. Before it came out, she said “people saw us as Shark and Ninja”, said Dyer. Afterward, it was ‘Oh, they’re SharkNina,” she continued. The fan response caught the team off guard. “We were all pleasantly surprised by the sentiment and engagement with our brand in that movie by a fan base that I think really got us,” Dyer said. “That was definitely the inflection point to say okay, we should go further into the sport.”

The aim of it, like most sponsorships of this scale, is younger fans. Yes, F1’s global audience tops 900 million worldwide but the brand isn’t trying to carve that up — at least not yet. As Dyer put it: “We’re here for everyone, truthfully, and we want to stay for everyone.”

What’s scarier for a marketer: a campaign that fails to scare anyone, or a campaign that scares its audience so much they never want to buy the product again?

For Cheetos, apparently, the answer is a demon covered in orange snack dust.

The brand’s latest Halloween ad, “Man at The Bottom of The Bag” was ranked the ninth-scariest ad of all time by creative and media intelligence platform DAIVID. The spot, which is part of Cheetos’ Flavor of Fear collaboration with horror studio Blumhouse Productions, is predicted to leave 15.3% of viewers experiencing intense fear. Even HBO Max’s Halloween trailer, which promotes its season of spooky films, ranked 10th.

It turns out Halloween ads are (shockingly) losing some of their bite. This year’s campaigns were 32% less likely to provoke intense fear than those released in 2025 according to DAIVID — which means they’re less scary, sure, but not necessarily less effective.

DAIVID compiled the ranking using its AI-powered creative intelligence technology, which predicts the emotional responses audiences are likely to have towards ads, as well as its potential impact on brand and business metrics. And ads were ranked according to the percentage of viewers predicted to experience intense fear, defined as an emotional response score between eight and 10 on a 10-point scale.

Which begs the question, what came top of the all-time scary ads chart?

In first place was 2025’s I Choose Freedom campaign: “End The Horror”, which predicted 24.5% of viewers would feel intense fear after watching it.

Second place went to 2025’s Universal: “Dare To Answer The Black Phone”, which predicted 21.6% of viewers would experience intense fear.

Third place went to the Six Flags: “Tick, Tick, Tick” from 2024, where 19.5% of viewers were predicted to experience intense fear.

And let’s not forget Cheetos in ninth place, followed by HBO Max in 10th.

Still, there is some good news. The average Creative Effectiveness Score, according to DAIVID, sat at 5.84 out of 10 last year, and rose slightly to 5.85 for 2026.

Simply put, scaring the living daylights out of consumers can remain optional. But making the ad work is the real trick — or treat.

While Amazon has not provided a specific dollar Q4/FY 2026 revenue forecast for its advertising services division — total revenue is expected to be in the range of $240 to $247 billion — it is expected to be a key pillar in its overall growth strategy.

“We expect year-over-year advertising revenue to grow in the high teens in fourth quarter 2026,” reads its official filing, which also trumpeted its recent launch of Amazon Ads Agent, a key growth engine for the division.

Intuitively, Amazon is putting AI at the center of its advertising business, previewing the consolidation of previously separate buying tools into a single platform that gives its technology greater responsibility for planning, executing, and optimizing campaigns — a concern for some.

Unveiled at the e-commerce giant’s flagship advertising conference, unBoxed, in San Francisco, California, last month, Amazon Ads Agent brings sponsored advertising, display, video, and audio into a unified buying experience.

The platform builds on Amazon’s earlier integration of its demand-side platform and advertising console, extending its conversational AI assistant across campaign creation, targeting, optimization, measurement, and customer support.

Advertisers can now use natural-language instructions to plan campaigns, generate creative, forecast audience reach, adjust budgets, and analyze performance. The system can also recommend and execute optimizations, reducing the manual work traditionally associated with programmatic advertising.

Delving deeper into Amazon’s publicity material, two offerings underpin the overhaul: Full-Funnel Campaigns and Digital Video Audio, or DVA+, as it’s more commonly termed.

One key aspect is Full-Funnel Campaigns, which combine sponsored ads, display, video, and streaming TV within a single campaign. Advertisers specify their products, creative and budget, while Amazon’s AI determines how campaigns run and continuously optimize toward objectives including new-to-brand customers and long-term sales.

The system also shares signals across upper- and lower-funnel advertising, including campaigns running on third-party open-internet inventory, with Amazon claiming beta testing shows up to 67% higher return on long-term ad spend and 29% lower cost per new-to-brand customer than manually configured full-funnel campaigns.

Meanwhile, DVA+ consolidates sponsored display, sponsored TV, and programmatic display, video, and audio buying. Advertisers can select an objective, budget and creative, leaving targeting and optimization to Amazon, or use advanced settings to retain conventional DSP controls.

For Amazon, the commercial opportunity extends beyond simplifying campaign management, as making programmatic buying accessible to smaller advertisers without specialist teams could expand adoption of its advertising infrastructure.

Meanwhile, integrating sponsored advertising with upper-funnel formats gives Amazon another opportunity to capture budgets traditionally managed through separate agency teams and buying platforms.

However, Amazon’s expansion comes amid renewed scrutiny of how large advertising platforms operate, a familiar debate in countless antitrust filings, and one underlined by recent charges against the e-commerce giant’s ad offering. In August, the Federal Trade Commission and 22 state attorneys general ⁠sued Amazon over alleged deceptive and unfair advertising auction practices.

The complaint alleges Amazon secretly introduced surcharges into auctions, resulting in Sponsored Products advertisers paying their own winning bids approximately 80% of the time by 2024, despite representations concerning second-price auctions. Amazon disputes the allegations, which remain unproven in court.

Advanced settings also preserve controls over bidding, audience targeting, supply selection, and frequency. MacLean characterized that configuration as “human-led, AI-supported,” maintaining that buyers can retain the conventional DSP experience while using AI-assisted recommendations.

Amazon also says it provides reporting on where ads are delivered and is developing explanations for its agent’s recommendations. Mandel argued that advertisers should be able to audit automated changes, inspect fees and inventory decisions, and challenge platform performance claims using independent measurement. He further opined, “If spending less with Amazon is better for the advertiser, will its agent recommend that?”

Amazon’s new platform offers advertisers a simpler route into programmatic buying while extending the company’s influence over how advertisers allocate budgets, and that’s a story that it’s selling to Wall Street.

However, its longer-term success will depend not only on whether automation improves performance, but also on whether advertisers can independently determine how those results were achieved, a story Madison Avenue will be more eager to hear.

Affle has traditionally used AI to help advertisers improve targeting, engagement, and sales. The companies say the deal will help deliver a more unified, full-funnel approach, bringing together Affle’s lower-funnel mobile performance marketing business and Perion’s focus on attracting upper-funnel brand dollars across CTV, retail media, and DOOH.

The mashup, per a statement from Affle CEO, chair, and managing director Anuj Khanna Sohum, could “unlock greater ROI for advertisers and publishers alike, while driving global scale for premium consumer conversions across industry verticals such as e-commerce, retail, and healthcare.” It will also bolster Affle’s cost-per-converted-user mobile business in developed markets and support its goal of 10x-ing its growth in a decade, Sohum said.

With Perion’s capabilities folded in, Affle will now have “a way to both expand geographically to become global, and to garner upper-funnel capabilities in out-of-home, CTV, and commerce media,” said Terence Kawaja, founder and CEO of investment bank Luma Partners, which served as the sole financial advisor to Affle on the deal. “If you have an excellent conversion technology capability, then apply that to upper-funnel agency-led advertising solutions, you can basically turn those into outcomes,” he said.

The pairing will “create new opportunities for brands, agencies, and media owners worldwide,” Tal Jacobson, Perion CEO, added in a statement.

But the price tag was also relatively cheap considering it’s about a fraction of what Perion used to be worth, and Perion’s on-hand cash offsets about half the cost. Excluding the roughly $228 million in cash and investments on Perion’s balance sheet as of Sept. 30, Affle is effectively paying about $192 million for the operating business. That’s roughly 3.7x the $51 million to $53 million in adjusted EBITDA that Perion has projected for this year.

Neither Affle nor Perion responded to requests for comment on the deal value.

The agreement adds to a wider pattern of open-web consolidation following a succession of mergers and acquisitions this year: In May Publicis spent $2.5 billion on LiveRamp; Nielsen agreed to pay $2.15 billion to take DoubleVerify private in August; Stagwell’s acquired publisher adtech platform Freestar last week; Private equity firms Vista Equity Partners and Quinti Capital are also reportedly making a bid for Criteo.

Perion historically bought into those channels, but faces intense competition in those arenas from bigger players like Amazon, which gobbled up over 75% of U.S. retail media spend last year, per Emarketer.

Perion’s own CEO said in August that the company was built around “diversifying away from the open web,” and its web channel was the primary drag on revenue in the second quarter. Historically dependent on the open web and search syndication (thanks to a long-standing partnership with Microsoft Bing), and punished by public markets in recent years—even after a nearly $193 million cumulative stock repurchasing push through Sept. 30—Perion matches the profile for the kind of culling playing out across the market. Before the Affle deal was announced, Perion’s stock was down about 79% from its five-year high of nearly $42 in April 2023.

That’s not to say Perion didn’t try to spread its eggs across different baskets first. The company made a handful of acquisitions in recent years, including video adtech platform Vidazoo in 2021 ($93.5 million), DOOH player Hivestack in 2023 ($100 million), AI-powered media buying firm Greenbids in 2025 (up to $65 million), and Premier Retail Networks in August of this year (up to $12 million).

Luma Partners’ Kawaja acknowledged that the low price was “icing on the cake” for selling Perion on the deal. Affle, he said, “looked at a number of different potential targets, and they’re all trading fairly cheaply.” So the pricing, he said, “was just opportunistic.”

But he insisted the Perion takeover is more strategic than financial in nature, and said the pricing “didn’t drive” the decision. “These are not financial guys that said, ‘Hey, look, it’s trading low. Under the terms of the arrangement, expected to close next year, Perion, which has traded on the Nasdaq since 2006, will become a subsidiary of Affle’s Israeli operation Affle 3iL Ltd, and will come off the exchange and the Tel Aviv exchange, where it also traded. The combined company will still trade on the public National Stock Exchange of India.

The acquisition is backed by debt financing from Citibank, and will require shareholder and U.S. antitrust approval. Terms also include a 40-day ‘go-shop’ period during which Perion can solicit rival offers.
