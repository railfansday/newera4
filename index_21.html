#7249. Mail Metro Media’s next creator play is representing them

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Mail Metro Media now thinks “more like a talent agency” and commissions “like a broadcaster”, according to Hannah Blake, its managing director of creator and new media. Its creators have, in other words, become the business.

Audiences tune in for them, brands pay to reach those audiences, and the deals have grown from around £5,000 apiece to worth seven figures in October. Holding on to those creators is now as important as finding them since any one who leaves for an outside agent can take the audience and advertisers along too.

Blake spelled out that risk on stage at the Digiday Media Strategies London event in October.

“The huge risk is that we make these people super famous and then they go and get signed by a different agent,” she said.

So in September the ad sales arm for the Daily Mail hired David White from Whalar as head of creators, with a brief to build an in-house talent agency that represents its creators and signs outside talent too. White ran Whalar’s Stars business in New York, the talent management arm that handles creators’ careers beyond brand deals and was also a senior talent manager there.

What the business ultimately ends up with is still open. Blake described something that “potentially could look more like a talent agency and an employer,” and she hopes to have it in market by the end of the year. The final shape matters because running a talent agency would be a big change of job for a newspaper group’s ad sales team. It exists to sell audiences to brands while an agent’s job is to get the best deal for the talent. Mail Metro Media would be trying to do both at once.

That’s the potted history of how Mail Metro Media reached this point. The full story is a lot more nuanced.

It started four years ago when Daily Mail launched Eliza, a social-only brand for young women fronted by six fashion and beauty creators. Its hit format, “Guess Which Outfit’s Most Expensive”, puts two women on the street, one in a £10,000 outfit and the other in a high-street copy, and leaves viewers to guess which is which. It kept going viral, people kept coming back and advertisers soon followed. That’s despite it being relatively small next to the rest of the Mail’s portfolio, and yet “we were doing lots of direct advertisers relationships quite quickly”, Blake said. Fast forward to now,  and brands often book content on Eliza and then want its creators posting on their own accounts too, which Mail Metro Media packages into the deal.

The Mail’s own TikTok was a different story. The Mail and Metro launched on the platform around the same time as Eliza and the former became the biggest news publisher there, with 20 million followers, on the back of rapid-fire news clips. But the money didn’t follow.  “The huge issue was there was no monetization there,” Blake said, even though publishers had widely expected TikTok to start paying them for views. Advertisers weren’t keen either, Every rival was posting the same kind of clips and the Daily Mail couldn’t build a brand into footage it had brought in from elsewhere.

Together, they showed the publisher that the money was in shows it owned and creators audiences came back for. It shifted course accordingly, and over the past year it relaunched the Mail and Metro on social around repeatable shows it owns, such as the sports debate show “Dugout Debate” with creators at the front and helping shape them. Blake said the operation now pulls 5 billion monthly video views across 10 brands and signed its first seven-figure deal last month. With that success comes a new kind of risk. The creators fronting those shows have built followings of their own and “now some of the superstars are beginning to emerge,” Blake said. Left unchecked, the publisher ends up making stars for outside agencies to sign.

In some ways, the publisher has already been acting like an agent of sorts. Alongside its core bundle where brands pay one flat rate for a run of content with “no differentiation between the talent,” Blake said it has spent the past year selling individual creators on their own channels. It calls this add-on “Creator Amp.” One example is Mimi Yates, a young journalist who fronts much of the news output and has built a following of her own. Brands can book her as part of a wider campaign or on her own, and on their personal accounts the creators “all set their own rates,” Blake said.

That arrangement was easy to manage while the talent was still unknown.

“When we started, our creators were all emerging,” Blake said. With bigger names now coming through, the publisher wants something sturdier. “We are now at the point where we’re beginning to formalize that relationship a bit more,” she said, and hiring White was the first step.

He will recruit from two places. One is from its own staff. The other is outside talent Blake described as “the next generation of emerging news and entertainment talent”.

Moves like this bring some awkward conversations with them. Daily Mail’s creators are salaried and Nathan Aspell, director of the New Media division, said the company owns the IP they develop. Blake described those same creators setting their own rates on their own channels. How the money is split between the employer and agent will go a long way to deciding whether a breakout star sticks around.

Axel Springer’s version hands journalists a share of the financial results in return for production, marketing and distribution. Daily Mail hasn’t said what its agency will charge.

Then there’s the small matter of journalism. The same person can read the headlines and sell the mascara, and the publisher would be both their employer and their agent. That’s a lot of hats for one newsroom.

White’s first signings will show how far the publisher plans to take this venture. Blake thinks publishers are headed this way regardless. “I actually think it’s just a byproduct of where the opportunity is,” she said.

For a lot of publishers, signing talent still feels like someone else’s business and talk of newsrooms turning into agencies tends to raise eyebrows. Blake sees it as following the money, which increasingly sits with “people-centric IP that exists across multiple different touchpoints”.

Plenty of rivals are following it too.  Axel Springer launched its own spin on the same idea last month, while Hearst did so earlier this week. Increasingly, publishers see a talent operation as the next step in their latest pivot to video, which runs on personalities whose audiences, and the ad money chasing them, follow them wherever they go.

“Publishers are responding to the audience’s desire for personality-led formats while limiting strategic dependence on individual journalists and managing talent,” said Laura Darcey, research analyst at Enders Analysis.

OpenAI, Google, Amazon and Walmart are betting on agent-led holiday shopping. Digiday charts how far it still has to go.

#7248. Paid media now plays largest role in brand-funded creator growth: report

While pumping paid dollars into creator content is the biggest piece of the category pie, using creators for production is the fastest growing, Madison & Wall found.

Madison & Wall’s research illustrates how creator marketing is evolving further away from the narrow #sponsored post model — what the advisory and consulting firm calls audience-led spend — into a multifaceted media engine that is core to consumer outreach. The advertiser-funded creator economy is forecast to grow 15% this year to $116 billion and reach $178 billion by 2030. It is on pace to grow at twice the rate of the total ad market as brands try to meet demands around creative content volume, authenticity and audience access.

“[Creators] have become an essential layer of the commercial advertising and media supply chain, serving simultaneously as media channels, creative production houses, commerce drivers, and performance engines,” the report reads.

Putting paid media dollars behind existing creator content that performs well has become the largest aspect of the advertiser-funded creator economy, providing reach and scalability without the TV commercial gloss that many consumers have begun to reject. Reliably achieving organic reach has also become less certain as platform algorithms change. Due to these factors, media-led creator spending is expected to reach $83 billion by 2030.

Marketers are not broadly expanding their budgets to support creator initiatives, but rather siphoning off resources previously allocated to channels like traditional media, digital search, paid social and programmatic. Madison & Wall emphasized that creator partnerships often employ multiple approaches, including combining audience-led and media-led spend, rather than focusing on one bucket. The study did not analyze consumer-funded creator income, meaning subscriptions, tips or other forms of revenue creators receive from their audiences.

While media-led spending is the biggest piece of the creator pie at the moment, asset-led spending, where brands pay creators to produce content, effectively using them as a content studio, is the fastest growing, currently representing 12% of the market. That helps explain the budgets being drawn from traditional creative production and could be a detriment to third-party creative agencies and production shops, which are also contending with the rise of artificial intelligence-generated content.

Those marketing services intermediaries, as well as talent managers and technology platforms, are retaining about 8% of advertiser-funded creator investments on a net basis as part of what Madison & Wall terms infrastructure spend. The rest of the market is made up of a miscellaneous segment that accounts for 7% of spend and encompasses creator engagements such as live events, barter arrangements and name, image and likeness deals.

The second part of Madison & Wall’s study will break down some of the growing pains the creator economy faces as it looks to sustain momentum, including fragmentation, pricing inconsistencies, contractual logjams, measurement shortcomings and questions of ownership over areas like data and intellectual property. That installment will also dive into creator fatigue, a mounting problem for marketers who have valued creators as a more approachable and trustworthy way to catch consumer attention.

Consumer expectations will also shape how far brands can take personalization. Tier expects AI agents to give people greater control over their purchasing journeys, potentially challenging brands’ access to customer data.

Maintaining consumer trust requires “being transparent with the user about what the value exchange is and making sure that they’re bought in,” he added.

#7053. In modern higher ed, rigor is a function of design, not a delivery format

Academic rigor in online courses is now a non-negotiable leadership priority because the entire higher-education ecosystem has shifted: online learning is no longer supplemental. It is the learning environment for millions of students. The stakes for quality, credibility and transferability have never been higher.

That rigor should be judged by one thing above all: how effective the learning experience is and whether it produces measurable outcomes. At Sophia Learning, we're constantly assessing whether those outcomes prepare students for future academic or professional success. This matters especially as institutions explore flexible learning pathways, including self-paced environments. When those environments are well-designed, grounded in mastery-based progression, aligned assessments and continual course improvement, they strengthen academic quality. And when they're validated externally through a faculty-led process, they can align with institutional standards for both rigor and transferability.

Academic rigor is not a function of delivery modality. Whether learning occurs in a traditional classroom, online or through a self-paced environment, academic quality is determined by clearly articulated learning outcomes and alignment between instruction, practice and assessment.

That is equally true with mastery-based progression. Learners advance by demonstrating competency. Multiple assessment methods (formative and summative) provide a more complete picture of learning. It’s an approach that allows every student who has the grit to succeed.

This approach also relies on continuous course iteration. Courses evolve to address students’ needs based on learner performance data, to reflect changes in the field as understood by faculty and to strengthen academic integrity.

Because of their relative novelty, self-paced models are often subject to a higher level of scrutiny. Accordingly, there should be higher investments in design and technology and quality assurance review processes. These investments result in courses that are both flexible and academically sound but are based on a new and emerging set of definitions.

Structured course design creates consistency even when learners progress at different rates. Clear learning objectives, sequenced content, robust and embedded practice opportunities and aligned assessments provide a support system to help students succeed and meet high academic expectations.

In many cases, flexibility improves learning outcomes. Self-paced environments allow learners to spend additional time on challenging concepts, revisit instructional materials and practice until mastery is achieved. This deeper engagement can improve both comprehension and transfer to future academic situations and employment.

Flexible learning pathways are particularly valuable for nontraditional students, including working adults, military-affiliated learners, caregivers and individuals balancing multiple responsibilities. For these students, set schedules can create barriers to persistence and completion. Self-paced learning removes many of those barriers while maintaining high standards and expectations.

Similar to traditional learning models, self-paced learning relies on faculty-led evaluation. At Sophia, experience is essential: Sophia Learning content and assessment subject matter experts (SMEs) have an average of 17 years of teaching experience. Seventy-one percent of Sophia Learning content SMEs have a PhD or equivalent degree. This ensures that rigorous academic standards are met and that course content matches students’ needs.

As some institutions increasingly incorporate alternative credit pathways into their degree programs, external validation can play a critical role in maintaining confidence and trust while ensuring transferability.

Independent review processes provide an additional layer of quality assurance beyond internal course development and evaluation. For example, Sophia Learning collaborates with our 115 institutional partners to review courses and articulate them into degree programs that align with institutional academic requirements. Nearly 3,000 colleges and universities have reviewed Sophia Learning transcripts as part of transfer credit evaluation processes.

Sophia courses also undergo review through the American Council on Education® (ACE) credit recommendation process, in which external faculty evaluators assess course quality, learning outcomes and assessment practices. These reviews provide institutions with an independent perspective on academic alignment and transferability.

Importantly, external validation complements rather than replaces institutional oversight and the institution’s internal quality assurance systems. Together, these processes provide a multilayered framework that supports academic integrity while enabling innovation in course delivery and credential pathways.

Ultimately, discussions about rigor should be grounded not only in course design principles but also in measurable student outcomes.

Evidence from institutional partnerships increasingly demonstrates that outcome-aligned, flexible learning pathways can boost student success. Students who use Sophia to augment their degree programs at other institutions often exhibit stronger persistence and continued engagement in their programs.

Academic quality must be ensured by continual assessment and validation. Courses need to address the academic and professional needs of the greater learner population.
