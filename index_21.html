#7400. How political creators are juggling platforms to find the three Rs: reach, revenue and rules

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Political creators need platforms that can deliver reach, revenue and clear rules. But no single platform reliably provides all three: YouTube offers ad revenue, Instagram can deliver huge audiences without paying creators and TikTok’s moderation and engagement can be unpredictable.

“A good platform for a political creator does four things,” said Ryan Schram, executive director of the Center for the Creator Economy at Syracuse University. “It helps new audiences find your work, it pays you fairly for that work, it tells you clearly what the rules are, and it lets you keep your relationship with your audience if you leave. Most platforms do one or two of those well. Very few do all four.”

Schram said that political creators gravitate to where political conversations are already happening, noting a 2024 PEW Research study that found that 85 percent of the most-followed news influencers are on X, half are on Instagram, and 44 percent are on YouTube.

“Journalists, campaigns, and elected officials are on X, and its users come for news. The catch is that creators and audiences aren’t in the same places,” Scharm said. “Audiences are larger on YouTube and Facebook, so the most successful political creators publish on several platforms rather than relying on one.”

Ahead of particularly contentious U.S. midterm elections, influencers are shaping narratives and highlighting key issues. Digiday spoke with six political creators and Schram about which platforms they are finding most fruitful, and which they’re struggling with.

YouTube remains a mainstay for many news and politics creators. “YouTube has the largest news audience and pays the most,” said Scharm. “It’s paid out more than $100 billion over four years and shares 55% of ad revenue.”

As far as the algorithm that drives that revenue, a YouTube spokesperson told Digiday it does “not favor or penalize content based on political orientation.” There are instances where YouTube’s systems “elevate high-quality voices,” like when major breaking news happens and the platform gets a special “breaking news shelf” on the homepage.

YouTube has leaned into news creators over the last few years, with its news, civics and podcast partnerships program, led by Brendan Feldman, offering advice on how to grow and monetize their channels. “For these last few years, a lot of focus has been on the real renaissance of journalists coming to YouTube, many in an independent capacity…and how we can best support them in building really vibrant businesses on our platform, connecting with audiences,” said Feldman.

Creator Aaron Parnas said he focuses on YouTube and Facebook in terms of monetization, though it’s only about 1 percent of his revenue pie.

“YouTube is the platform most worth investing in and also the hardest to grow on,” said V Spehar of Under the Desk News. “But if someone was just starting, I’d say focus on YouTube. It’s the most stable and fairly monetizes – but it’s hard to grow. So you may have to create for YouTube first, and then use clips to build discoverability by posting the clips on all socials until you figure out which social platform you perform best on.”

Several of the creators Digiday spoke with said Instagram has good engagement, but at least one mentioned the platform isn’t a revenue source. “I don’t make a penny because Instagram doesn’t pay out for anything, in terms of Reels. So I make zero money on Instagram, but I had 450 million views last month,” said Parnas, who added he gets much higher viewership on the platform than elsewhere, but frustratingly isn’t earning anything for that elevated engagement.

Newly independent news creator Mark Istook, said he found Instagram to be easier to predict, and the comments are far less “poisonous and toxic” than on sibling Meta-owned platform, Facebook.

The jury is split on Instagram’s utility. While creator Grant Hermes found Instagram might as well be a “black box” in terms of understanding its underlying structure, creator Adam Mockler, however, has figured it out, boasting 90 million views a month on the platform.

Mockler pulls in even more views on Facebook, boasting 120 million on the platform which he called an “underdog” that has been “really swinging lately.”

“I genuinely think Zuckerberg changed something when Trump got into office,” he said. “They opened politics back up on Facebook, and it allowed advertisers to buy up spots on political stuff again.” It’s performing so well, Mockler has doubled down, putting more resources and more team members, he did not offer specifics, on the platform, even hiring an intern that helps post on the platform.

Parnas said he also focuses on Facebook because it, along with YouTue, is where the most revenue comes from.

Mockler also told Digiday he’s leaning into Reddit, a platform notoriously difficult to break through.

“We had a 17-year-old high schooler posting on Reddit for a while…I view Reddit as more valuable than TIkTok right now,” Mockler said, adding about 70 percent of the intern’s role was him posting his appearances on CNN or other traditional news platforms in several subreddits, while the rest was on the official Mockler Media Reddit account, all to get people to engage with his content and find him on other platforms. “I view it as internal PR,” Mockler said.

Spehar and others said TikTok has been a tough platform lately, with inconsistent flagging and engagement hampering their revenue and reach.

“[TikTok] blocks political content or mislabels it as ads and live streams focusing on politics can be falsely reported. Overall it’s hard to get your perspective out if you don’t already have a big channel at this point,” Spehar said, adding the platform is still a good place for creators to build, but is more saturated than it once was.

Parnas placed TikTok on the third rung of his monetization scheme, underneath YouTube and Facebook and alongside Instagram and Istook said the platform should be more reliable when it comes to engagement, but is “all over the map” after it closed a deal with the U.S. earlier this year.

“My views on TikTok have remained very, very consistent over the past year,” said Parnas. Yet just a few days ago, one of his political videos got stuck in an “under review” stage for nearly three hours. “I just took it down,” he said.

Istook said he has the biggest audience on TIkTok but found the platform the least predictable in terms of engagement. “Something will do millions of views, and something else will do 1,500, which just is hard to make sense of with a follower count like I have,” he said.

#7395. ‘They hold us hostage’: Publishers furious over terms change still can’t quit Facebook

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Publisher trade groups around the world are pressing Meta to change its planned terms of service, but heads of audience and social media told Digiday that their continued reliance on its platforms raises questions about how much leverage they have to force changes.

The tension is familiar: publishers want to stop tech platforms from using their journalism in AI tools without compensation, but can’t easily give up the reach those platforms provide.

Meta recently announced it would consult with industry groups and leaders before updating its terms, but that has done little to pacify publishers. Several publisher trade groups Digiday spoke with, including the News Media Alliance, Digital Content Next, and The Professional Publishers Association, said they hadn’t yet been in contact with them.

In a video posted to LinkedIn, Tami Hoffman, director of public policy at The Guardian, warned that some publishers may ultimately decide to abandon Meta’s platforms over the company’s updated terms of use.

“Policy moves slowly, and some publishers may take the tough decision to come off these platforms altogether. This issue is filling up my inbox,” Hoffman said in the video.

That possibility is already weighing on publishers as they watch to see if Meta makes changes to its terms or provides detail on how publishers can opt out, according to Melissa Chowning, founder and CEO of Twenty-First Digital, an audience development and marketing consultancy for publishers and brands.

“The ones paying close attention are still asking questions about whether Meta traffic is worth the trade-off. Now they just have more time to make that call. I suspect if the terms don’t change, we’ll see several publishers move off of Meta. Hard to say how many that would be,” she said.

But internally, the situation is more complicated.

And that’s because Meta’s value to publishers is not really its referral traffic anymore. Heads of audience at three publishers told Digiday Meta’s social platforms are still crucial for other audience development and business strategies: branded content, paid media, reach, engagement, awareness, and connecting with community. They aren’t ready to cut ties, putting into question how much leverage publishers really have in their fight against Meta’s AI scraping terms.

One head of audience said the publisher had deprioritized Facebook after traffic from Meta’s platforms fell from accounting for more than a fifth of its global traffic to a negligible share. But Instagram remains an important platform for brand awareness, community engagement and marketing rather than referral traffic, they said.

“Meta Zero is already here,” the head of audience said.

When analyzing social traffic to the top 10 publisher websites (including The New York Post, The Wall Street Journal, The New York Times, People, BBC, AP News, CNN, USA Today, Fox News and CNBC), Facebook traffic was down 5% year-over-year, according to Similarweb data shared with Digiday.

Meta’s platforms accounted for an average of 3.47% of global publisher traffic in July 2026, according to Chatbeat’s most recent data, which includes over 2,500 domains.

The head of audience said they didn’t think it was likely they would remove themselves from Meta’s platforms.

“They hold us hostage,” they added. “This change in terms is another brazen move by Meta.”

It echoes the debate over blocking Google’s crawlers. Publishers have threatened to do so over AI scraping, but have yet to follow through, partly because they still rely on Google for search discovery and traffic, even as referrals decline.

Publishers’ relationships with Google and Meta have some important parallels, but they also differ in significant ways, stressed Eilidh Wilson, head of policy and public affairs at the U.K. publisher trade body the PPA. “Both platforms wield enormous power over audience access, making withdrawal a ‘nuclear option’ for publishers,” she said.

The key difference is how publishers use these platforms, she explained. “Referral traffic from Meta has declined sharply, particularly since 2023, when it deprioritized branded content,” said Wilson. The result: publishers that had invested millions in social media teams and content saw traffic collapse almost overnight.

However, declining traffic has not diminished publishers’ reliance on Meta or its market power, stressed Wilson. “Publishers have adapted, using Meta for audience engagement, conversion and subscription acquisition,” she said. “A publisher may receive relatively little referral traffic from Meta but still depend on it for a significant proportion of new subscriptions. Referral traffic alone therefore understates Meta’s power over publishers. This is why we need an SMS [Strategic Market Status] investigation into Meta, as we’ve seen with Google Search.”

Another head of audience at a news publisher said social platforms remain critical to reaching new audiences, building awareness around its journalism and connecting with readers on platforms where they spend their time.

“I don’t see us abandoning our social accounts,” they said. “I think publishers can recognize the downsides of these relationships while still seeing tremendous value in being on these platforms. It’s not an either-or proposition.”

A third head of audience wasn’t yet certain how their outlet would evaluate its relationship with Meta going forward, as it was a decision that would be made at a more senior executive level. But they had strong words about the upcoming terms change.

“This is another example of Meta using brute force to get what it wants from the publishing industry. Publishers are being asked to accept sweeping access to their sites as the price of reaching audiences on Meta’s platforms. It’s a shameless pursuit of Meta’s own benefit, with little regard for the sustainability of the journalism its products depend on,” they said.

Meta has also recently started charging brands for link posts, which can help drive audiences to their sites. While publishers are excluded from this, the first head of audience wondered for how long.

All of this points to why a number of publishing trade bodies are turning to regulators and lawmakers to pressure Meta into changing its terms, leading to developments like the CMA investigation, the Norwegian court injunction and U.S. Senators urging Meta to abandon the proposed terms.

Wilson said Meta needs to “unbundle” its terms and separate access to its platform from permission to crawl publishers’ content. Publishers should be able to allow link previews or advertising while blocking uses such as AI training and product development, explained Wilson. Those choices need to be enforceable: protections such as robots.txt should be written into binding terms, not left to public assurances, she added.

Publishers should also be told which crawlers and third parties collect their content, what they collect and how that material is used. Meta’s terms, Wilson argues, should not retroactively authorize past copying or make publishers responsible for content they do not have the rights to license.

“This goes beyond communication and transparency. The terms themselves must change,” said Wilson. “As drafted, they leave publishers with an impossible choice: withdraw from services their businesses depend on, or accept crawling and use of their content without firm legal safeguards. Regardless of Meta’s intentions, that legal exposure is unacceptable.”

#7394. Where agency strategists see opportunity as AI challenges their worth

Let Marketing Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

While strategists fear the tech will replace their roles, the majority agree that there are also reasons to be optimistic, according to a WARC report.

The advertising industry has faced significant challenges this year, marked by agency consolidations at major holding companies and sweeping headcount reductions. Between August 2025 and August 2026, the industry recorded a loss of 6,900 jobs, per Emarketer.

While there are many factors behind the cuts, one of the most prominent has been the rise of artificial intelligence across all aspects of the industry. From data analysis to campaign generation, AI’s adoption has been swift across nearly all marketing disciplines, leaving many executives apprehensive about the future.

Still, 66% of agency-side strategists agree there are clear reasons to be optimistic, according to WARC’s Future of Strategy 2026 report.

“We’re not saying the problems are solved, but we are saying the tides may be turning,” said Lena Roland, content director at WARC Strategy. “After a period of layoffs and consolidation, there’s a sense that things might be stabilizing and strategists can [begin] to look forward again.”

The reasons for the guarded positivity are simple: Clients are seeing a need for thinking that is rooted in human insights, Roland explained. Strategy influence with clients jumped to 50%, up from 41% in 2025, per WARC.

“This indicates that clients are increasingly recognizing the value of strategy, particularly at a time when growth is harder to achieve,” Roland said. “AI can help us understand the world as it is; strategists can help us understand what it can become.”

Ironically, AI is helping fuel strategists’ confidence. Nearly two-thirds, 65%, of the 489 agency-side strategists surveyed said AI gives them time for more nuanced, strategic work. Simultaneously, 55% believe the tech will refocus strategy on human perspectives. WARC’s report was fielded in August.

“Strategists are positioned as the best people to be guardians of reality by grounding ideas in real human insights,” Roland said. “In an age of AI, humanity is a competitive advantage. AI can’t replace human judgment or intuition, and it can’t ask the questions that are really worth asking.”

Not yet, anyway. Despite their optimism for the near-term, strategists are more wary about the long-term. More than half of the strategists polled in WARC’s study — 56%, up from 41% last year — are concerned that AI will eventually advance enough to replace them.

“They’re more optimistic about the future, but they’re also realistic about the scale of change,” Roland said. “The strategists that will thrive are those who learn to use AI well, while still bringing the human perspective, critical thinking and commercial understanding that makes their work valuable.”

However, the technology could become a double-edged sword: The same tools that strategists are using to move upstream with clients could also be leveraged by account executives, explained Michael Farmer, chairman and CEO at marketing consultancy Farmer & Company.

“When agencies talk about AI [right now], they’re not talking about how they can do a more strategic job for their clients,” Farmer said. “If they have the will, the senior account executives can [use AI] to offer more strategy.”

Doing so would elevate account executives to more of a business partner role, rather than one more akin to a traffic manager whose job is to get stuff out the door, Farmer said. He explained that over the past 30-some years, the account executive role has been diminished through the rise of programmatic negotiating, the unbundling of media and creative and the introduction of strategists in the late ‘90s and early 2000s.

“They took away a responsibility that the account people already had,” Farmer said. “They used to tell me when I first got started that the strategist was ‘the conscience of the consumer’ … They were there to kind of provide discipline between caring about the brand and caring about the brief that they got from the client.”

Often, that meant rewriting the briefs accordingly, Farmer said. Now, AI can often write those briefs as well — if not better — than a strategist can, which is reflected in the sentiment from strategists that they have more time to think beyond the brief. Agencies and their strategists will need to find how to prove their value beyond their traditional remit.

“Strategy as a discipline has too often been treated as a supporting function, or even thrown in for free, rather than recognized as a valuable service in its own right,” Roland said. “There’s an opportunity now for strategists to become genuine growth partners, helping shape not just how a brand communicates, but where the business goes next.”

#7298. Hollywood’s King of Culture Is Building a Billion-Dollar Entertainment Empire

Charles D. King may not be a household name like Steven Spielberg, Alfred Hitchcock, and Walt Disney. But in Hollywood, he operates in the same echelon.

King joined these luminaries at the top of his industry as the recipient of the Producers Guild of America Milestone Award in 2024, recognizing his “immeasurable contribution to the film industry” as the founder of multi-platform media company Macro, dedicated to bringing BIPOC stories to life.

But it wasn’t until 2025 that King’s Hollywood clout came to bear on a deal unprecedented in Tinseltown history.

The Warner Bros. horror thriller Sinners, written and directed by Ryan Coogler, took in $279.7 million domestically, according to Box Office Mojo, making it the seventh highest-grossing movie of the year—and the only one in the top 10 not based on a popular IP.

Arguably, however, the most groundbreaking part of the film happened behind the scenes. King was part of the team—along with Coogler’s agent at William Morris Endeavor, Craig Kestel; his attorney Jonathan Gardner; and WME COO Dan Limerick—that negotiated the deal giving Coogler final cut, first-dollar gross participation, and ownership of the film’s rights after 25 years.

“We knew Sinners was going to be special,” King told ADWEEK. “The ownership deal was well earned given Ryan’s extraordinary track record and is in alignment with deal precedent for a select number of very accomplished filmmakers.”

He added: “I hope this deal serves as a reminder to Hollywood and beyond of the enormous potential upside of partnering with extraordinary storytellers and original voices.”

Trailblazing seems to be in King’s DNA. Years earlier, he became the first Black partner at Hollywood powerhouse agency WME in its over 100-year history—and the first at any major Hollywood talent firm.

Now, King is focused on scaling his own entertainment empire with Macro. Founded in 2015, the film, TV, and digital content studio has grown to include a talent representation arm, M88, and a creative agency, Brand Macro. This past September, Macro acquired the streaming platform Allblk from AMC Global Media to further its distribution and storytelling ambitions.

His goal is to build Macro into a billion-dollar company through culture-shaping work that uplifts underrepresented voices. That mission has produced lauded films such as Judas and the Black Messiah, Fences, and Sorry to Bother You, but “impact can be measured in a lot of ways,” he noted.

“There’s the careers that we’ve launched by financing films by first-time filmmakers,” King said. “We’ve had productions where we’re making sure there’s diversity and a range of folks hired as part of our crews. There’s the make-up of the creatives of our company and everyone that we partner with, which is extraordinarily diverse.”

King has been living by that ethos long before it took shape as Macro. Coogler, who became the first Black winner of the Best Original Screenplay Oscar for Sinners (not to mention directing Marvel’s Black Panther franchise), met King in 2012 and remembers his strong influence at Hollywood’s ground level as an agent.

“He made sure our voices were heard in those agency hallways, advocating for the value of Black filmmakers and the value of Black stories,” Coogler told ADWEEK.

As CEO of Macro, King’s ability to bring financing “makes all the difference in the world” when it comes to getting projects greenlit, Coogler added, as does his business savvy acquired from years of experience. “Just him having so much earned knowledge from his position as an agent and his position as a producer and financier and being able to share his business strategy with like-minded folks has been such a great tool to have,” Coogler said.

“And it’s been a joy to watch them build community through the work that Stacey [Walker King, chief brand officer of Macro and King’s wife] does,” he added.

Walker King also runs Brand Macro, the company’s marketing agency. In addition to working with brands, Brand Macro also focuses on building community through events that bring together Black and POC talent, such as Macro Lodge at the Sundance Film Festival and the Macro HBCU Summit, which connected students interested in entertainment with industry insiders.

For his work in elevating, financing, and championing diverse creative talent, Charles D. King is the recipient of the Adcolor | ADWEEK 2027 Beacon Award.

“I feel incredibly blessed that a vision I had more than three decades ago has grown into something that resonates so deeply with people—not just Black filmmakers, but Latino, Asian, LGBTQ+, and white storytellers who feel that the traditional system hasn’t made room for the stories they want to tell,” King said. “I think one of the most powerful things we’ve built is belief. Belief in what’s possible.”

After King graduated from Howard University’s School of Law in 1996, he moved to Hollywood to set out on the path that would lead to Macro. But King remembers the vision for the company starting much earlier.

Growing up in Decatur, Ga., King got his first taste of entrepreneurship watching his father, a physician, start and build a medical practice.

He also experienced firsthand the power of representation in entertainment. On the television drama L.A. Law, he idolized Blair Underwood’s portrayal of the “charismatic” and “intelligent” Black law firm associate Jonathan Rollins.

“There was the power of content and [seeing] an aspirational person,” King said. “I saw myself in this person and thought, ‘I could be like that guy one day.’” King has since worked with Underwood on two Macro projects, the 2020 film Really Love and the upcoming Netflix romantic drama Don’t Ever Wonder. “It’s such a beautiful, full-circle moment,” he said.

But his path shifted between his sophomore and junior years at Howard. While working a summer job at MTV in New York, a lawyer told King he’d make “a good lawyer—but a great agent.”

When King graduated, he inked his vision in the Howard Law commemoration book. Asked what he’ll be doing in 10 years, King wrote: “Be at the helm of a diversified entertainment and media company.”

Despite his mother being a writer and a poet, King never wanted to be a creative force himself. Instead, he realized early on that he had a talent for seeing potential in others, and leveraged this to immense success as an agent. He signed multi-hyphenates like Coogler and Tyler Perry, the filmmaker and actor behind the Madea franchise, before they became household names.

When King keys into what he looks for in people, he names qualities like vision, ambition, and brilliance. “I would also say authenticity,” he added. “People who authentically know who they are as artists and as people, and you see that throughline in everything that they touch.”

Investing in people with a strong point of view has brought industry accolades as well. The 93rd Academy Awards nominated Macro’s historical drama Judas and the Black Messiah for six Oscars, making King, Coogler, and Shaka King (no relation) the first all-Black producing team to be up for Best Picture. (The film ultimately won two Oscars: Best Supporting Actor for Daniel Kaluuya and Best Original Song.)

“That experience—I’ll take it with me forever,” King said. “That movie meant so much to all of us. Everyone on that movie is bonded for life. To really be in it as a producer, and to then get the blessing of being the first film from an all-Black producer unit to get an Oscar nomination, was just truly icing on the cake.”

King says his ability to connect with people from different backgrounds is “just part of who I am.” He remembers being “really small and skinny” before having a growth spurt in high school, enabling him to relate to people who were “not necessarily as accepted socially.”

“I’m more inclined in that direction, as opposed to being someone who only wants to talk to who’s in the ‘in crowd,’” he said. “I’m going to interface with people who I think are intelligent and talented, and who I enjoy spending time with.”

When he started in the mailroom at what was then William Morris Agency, he was the only Black trainee and worked hard to “build the bottom of my pyramid” through relationships.

“I was dropped into a pot of gold to understand this industry from the ground floor up,” King said. “I recognized the uniqueness of the opportunity, and I cultivated and built relationships with everyone,” from his fellow mailroom trainees to assistants at other agencies and young executives just getting their start.

Now, at Macro, he’s reached the top of his proverbial pyramid, able to build community the way he wants. He estimates between 350 and 400 interns have come through Macro since its founding, several of whom have gone on to become “executives at every one of the major talent agencies.” Macro alumni have also won Emmys, and sit in the C-suites of companies around Hollywood. Among them is Mark Wright, vice president of film and TV at Higher Ground, the production studio of Barack and Michelle Obama.

For King, a major part of turning out future executives is fostering a culture of collaboration at the beginning of their careers: “Everyone at our company has a voice, and great ideas can come from anywhere.”

King launched Macro during the production boom of the Peak TV era in the mid-2010s. According to the New York Times, the number of TV shows in the U.S. jumped 52% in about six years, from 349 in 2013 to 532 in 2019. During that time, studios also implemented DEI policies in response to campaigns such as 2015’s #OscarsSoWhite, creating a robust pipeline for diverse talent and projects.

Seven years later, Hollywood is undergoing massive shifts, from how shows are produced to consolidation among the major studios. The number of TV shows and feature films being made has dropped significantly. In October, Paramount Skydance closed its acquisition of Warner Bros. Studios. And new forms of content, like made-for-social microdramas, are cutting into audience share.

But King continues to rely on what’s gotten him this far: faith, relationships, and his deep knowledge of people and the industry.

And in some ways, these shifts have provided unprecedented leverage for the people who can break through the noise. In March, Macro announced a multi-year first-look, co-financing, and distribution deal with Sony Pictures.

The acquisition of streaming platform Allblk, founded by BET’s Bob Johnson in 2014, gives Macro a direct distribution channel and a closer relationship with its audience.

“I have been looking at Allblk for 12 years,” King said. “We think that having ownership of this platform will serve our business well as potentially a provider of content. But, more broadly, it’s a larger market opportunity that we saw needed to be unlocked.”

And there could be more deals to come, with King considering acquiring assets being “spun out” from consolidating media giants and pushing further into producing live events.

He also recognizes AI as a tool for efficiency and the “democratization of opportunity,” with creators being able to build their own audiences. But he made clear that AI should “never replace real storytelling, writing, and acting,” and that protecting intellectual property is a priority.

“Our vision forward will continue to be to assess the marketplace and adapt,” King said. “That’s one of the benefits of being a smaller, more nimble institution, as opposed to a much larger institution where it’s harder to pivot and maneuver. We’ve had to make adjustments like every thoughtful company, making sure we’re right-sizing our organization for future growth, and for where the market is headed.”

According to Coogler, “change seems to be the law” of Hollywood, but King is well positioned to succeed.

“From what I’ve seen, people who can be both patient but can also act fearlessly, and people who have good taste and great relationships with talent, seem to always be able to make the moments of transition work best for themselves,” Coogler said. “And Charles is both of those things.”

Whatever the future holds, King lives by a piece of advice from his grandmother: “Go out there and be yourself.” He credits staying true to his principles and vision with guiding him from the agency mailroom to starting Macro and becoming an Oscar-nominated producer.

“I’m just Charles King,” he said. “I’m not changing who I am. And I’ve tried my best to operate with that same compass and treating people the way I would want to be treated.”

“He has a great heart,” Coogler added. “It’s very rare to find somebody who is this talented at business but who’s a good person at the core.”

ADWEEK: What makes covering this year’s midterm elections different from other elections, especially presidential elections?

Douglas Rohrbeck: Midterm elections present a different challenge because there isn’t one presidential race driving the entire national conversation. Harper: The biggest unknown is the composition of the electorate— who shows up to the polls and who skips out.  We saw significant shifts among key voting blocs in 2024, including Latino voters and younger voters.

We’re watching whether the enthusiasm we’ve seen among Democratic voters translates into turnout in battlegrounds and whether Republicans can mobilize their coalition without President Trump himself on the ballot.

Rohrbeck: The biggest unknown is always how the results will unfold and where the story will take us. That’s why flexibility is so important. We have correspondents positioned across the country, a seasoned anchor team, and the production capabilities to pivot quickly as developments warrant. Stirewalt: We know the polls will be wrong, but we don’t know by how much or in what direction. And it behooves all of us to come into the final day of the election with enough humility to say that our assumptions may or may not be true. It’s being open to the possibility that Democrats may be doing better than expected, that Republicans may again be doing better than polling suggested, or that polls are right on the money.

AW: How are you going to differentiate yourselves from your competitors?

Our goal is to make a complicated election night accessible, informative, and meaningful to viewers, regardless of where they’re watching.

Rohrbeck: On election night, Bret Baier and Martha MacCallum bring the experience, credibility, and perspective viewers have come to expect from us. So, I would say we’re going to have a transparent process, and we’re going to have independent race calling so that there’s no funny business.

Our national sample will also be roughly three times the size of the 2024 National Exit Poll. That gives us an extraordinary opportunity to understand what motivated voters and how different communities cast their ballots.

I think it’s going to be a lot of fun, and it looks fantastic.

We want viewers to understand not just the results, but why some races can be projected quickly while others will require patience.

Rohrbeck: Our Fox News Decision Desk will be analyzing results throughout the evening, and we’ll make projections when the data supports them. Ultimately, accuracy will always take precedence over speed. Our responsibility is to give viewers the most reliable information possible.
