A former Ohio State University graduate student could not convince a federal appeals court to overturn a jury’s verdict in favor of the school in her sexual harassment lawsuit because she failed to show that her trial was unfairly prejudiced, according to a Tuesday decision.

The plaintiff in Huang v. The Ohio State University alleged that a faculty advisor subjected her to unwanted touching and that, when she resisted his advances, he retaliated by manipulating her candidacy exam for an engineering doctorate. She brought claims under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act as well as the 14th Amendment’s due process right to bodily integrity.

A district granted summary judgment for Ohio State on the plaintiff’s Title VII claims, holding that she was not an employee of the university within the law’s definition and therefore could not bring those claims. At trial, a jury held for the plaintiff’s advisor. She appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which reversed and ordered a new trial.

At the second trial, the jury determined that the plaintiff was not an employee because Ohio State and the advisor did not have the “right to control the manner and means by which [the plaintiff] accomplished her work.” The jury also ruled in favor of the advisor on the plaintiff’s 14th Amendment claim.

The plaintiff again appealed to the 6th Circuit, alleging that the district court incorrectly instructed the jury on what facts were relevant to its determination of whether she was an employee. She also claimed that the court abused its discretion in excluding certain evidence and that it improperly prohibited her from requesting economic damages in her closing argument.

Each of these claims failed, the 6th Circuit held.

The plaintiff forfeited her challenge to the court’s jury instructions because she failed to raise it, despite the district court specifically asking whether the parties had objections or edits to its instructions. To grant a third trial on this point “would be unfair to the court and the defendants,” the 6th Circuit said.

In her evidentiary argument, the plaintiff claimed that the district court improperly prevented her from submitting portions of a slideshow containing the results of an Ohio State campus climate survey on sexual assault. She argued that the presentation would have made her allegations more plausible.

However, the plaintiff failed to include the relevant slides in her appeal, leaving the 6th Circuit unable to conclude whether they had sufficient weight to affect the jury’s verdict. The appeals court also could not determine whether the slides offered anything “above and beyond” other testimony in the case.

In all, the plaintiff failed to show that any of her allegations amounted to unfair prejudice warranting a new trial.

The order approving the sale came over the objection of a former Bacone professor who raised numerous concerns, including that the Muscogee Nation might have a claim to the land under a 19th century treaty. Bacone — originally named Indian University — began teaching classes in a Baptist mission house in 1880.

In the early 20th century, the institution deepened its focus on Native American education, recruiting Native staff and faculty and developing classes in Native American culture. In the 1930s, Bacone established an arts center that developed a distinct aesthetic and expressive movement.

By the 21st century, some faculty members questioned the college’s commitment to Native American students and faculty. In its last years, only a handful of faculty were Native American, and its Native student makeup fluctuated widely, dipping to as low as about one-third in 2018.

In fall 2023, Bacone had just 106 students, less than a tenth of the 1,184 students it enrolled in 2010. By February 2024, The Associated Press reported that the college was teetering on the edge of closure amid lawsuits, debts and the risk of accreditation loss. When visiting the campus, a reporter for the news service found buildings in disrepair, molding Native works of art and accusations that administrators had embezzled funds.

When Bacone initially filed for bankruptcy, leaders intended to keep the college open. But after a federal bankruptcy trustee found evidence of financial mismanagement, a judge ordered the college into Chapter 7 bankruptcy. That kicked off a court-managed liquidation of what was, until its closure, the oldest continuously operating college in Oklahoma.

Career and technical education and dual enrollment are “powerful levers” together, said a researcher at the Community College Research Center.

At DeKalb High School and New Boston High School in northeastern Texas, students in healthcare courses use mannequins to practice caring for patients, while students in welding classes fabricate real parts.

They are participating in dual-enrollment programs developed with Texarkana College that are designed to help high school students make progress toward a postsecondary credential before they graduate.

The community college works with school districts to train high school instructors to teach the college coursework. Texarkana also sends academic coaches to both high schools to help students chart a post-high school plan in their chosen field.

The partnership between the high schools and the community college serves as an example of what an October report from the Community College Research Center calls an "unprecedented opportunity” to do more to pair dual enrollment with career-focused education.

The report estimates that more than 1 million of the nation’s 3.8 million annual high school graduates are taking three or more high school credits of career and technical education, or CTE, but not taking any dual-credit courses.

Sarah Griffin, research associate at CCRC and lead author of the new paper, said that matters because 2025 research from the center found that Texas students with a CTE focus were more likely to enroll in college and complete a degree when they took dual enrollment courses in high school than those who did not.

There was also an economic payoff. By age 24, the prior research found, the CTE students who took dual enrollment classes were earning 16% more than CTE students who did not.

“CTE and dual enrollment together are really powerful levers for students because they are facilitating exploration of careers and college programs for students,” Griffin told Higher Ed Dive.

“Because they're so widespread, they're already serving large numbers of students across the country,” Griffin said. “Therefore, they're really practical for practitioners to be able to leverage.”

Shaun Dougherty, professor of education and policy at Boston College and a former senior adviser at the U.S. Department of Education, said the report's call for a greater focus on career-connected dual enrollment is “fairly aligned with current policy” at the state level.

Dougherty pointed to Colorado's Concurrent Enrollment Programs Act and Kentucky's Dual Credit Scholarship as examples of states providing a favorable policy environment for combining dual enrollment with CTE. The Colorado law covers tuition for dual-enrollment students, while the Kentucky program helps high school students in the state pay for dual credit classes at participating colleges.

“While there has been pushback on four-year college-going as a policy priority, career-aligned programs that span secondary and postsecondary levels and that focus on preparation for relevant skills have gotten far more focus,” said Dougherty.

Griffin said it’s paramount for those looking to establish career-connected CTE programs to develop a vision of the optimal dual enrollment experience for CTE students and how it will help them find their way after high school — whether that means going to a community college, a four-year college or a technical program that’s aligned with a specific job.

Griffin recommended a CCRC-developed framework called Dual Enrollment Equity Pathways for those looking to boost dual enrollment for CTE students.

Pursuing these practices to scale up dual enrollment for CTE students will pay off in the long run, Griffin said. The strategy is a practical approach for helping students earn a postsecondary credential and increasing the likelihood “they'll be able to secure a good-paying job by the age of 30,” she said.

Voters approved a 1% sales tax to help with funding. In an assessment, each school’s needs are weighed against what replacement would cost.

As it became clear in early November that Richmond voters would pass a 1% sales tax measure for school construction and renovation, Richmond Public Schools Superintendent Jason Kamras was grateful that residents would approve a tax increase even while households around the country are experiencing an affordability crisis.

“We are dealing with decades of deferred maintenance [and] disinvestment,” Kamras told Facilities Dive. “Our challenges are legion, which is why I think the voters so overwhelmingly supported the 1%.”

The district faces a roughly $680 million, 20-year backlog in maintenance, repair and upgrades to a portfolio of more than 40 schools, according to an independent assessment the agency conducted in 2024. Almost a quarter of the schools are more than 100 years old. About 70% are more than 50 years old. Only a handful are less than 15 years old.

Earlier this month, voters in Richmond were asked to decide whether to increase the sales tax by 1% to create a fund exclusively to build new public schools and renovate existing facilities. When the final votes were counted, 68% of the electorate voted in favor of the assessment, according to an Axios Richmond report.

Kamras says the actual need is even more than what the assessment shows — “well north of $1 billion,” he said.

With passage of the 1% assessment, the district could raise an estimated $850 million over the next 20 years. But that’s still not enough to meet all of the needs, Kamras said.

“I wish the sales tax would solve everything,” he said. “It's going to take us a lot farther, a lot faster, but it by itself does not solve all of our facilities' needs.”

The district currently gets about $2.5 million a year for facilities work, according to the school.

Adding to its challenges, the district fired its director of facilities earlier this year for improper use of funds and as of mid-November was operating with an interim director, according to the district’s website. The former facilities chief is alleged to have made personal purchases using his school district credit card, according to news reports.

“The investigation found direct evidence of asset misappropriation and unprofessional conduct” by the official, a school district investigation found, ABC 8 News reported in October.

Among the needs the district faces is a $938,000 roof repair at Woodville Elementary School, $2.5 million for brick wall repairs at John Marshall High School and $1.7 million for updated windows at Thomas Jefferson High School.

Mold, flood damage and HVAC issues are widespread, according to the assessment, which used a facility condition index to determine what each of the school’s needs are weighed against what it would cost to replace the school.

“The Facility Condition Index … is defined as the ratio of the cost of current needs divided by the current replacement value (CRV) of the facility,” the assessment says.

Schools that score between 0 and 5 are considered new or well-maintained, with little or no visual evidence of wear or deficiencies. Those with a score of between 5 and 10 show wear but are still in a serviceable and functioning condition. Those with a score of between 10 and 30 show signs of hard or long-term wear and are nearing the end of their useful or serviceable life. Those with a score of 30 and above have reached the end of their useful or serviceable life, making “renewal” necessary.

The Richmond City Council will need to pass an ordinance to enshrine the sales tax increase into law. The district has been using funds from a local meals tax to patch together funds for school construction dating back to 2018.

Kamras said the city will determine which projects to prioritize based on feedback from nine stakeholder meetings, as well as building conditions, enrollment trends, and concentration of student need.

Richmond is not alone in its need for school renovations. School districts on average only receive about 17% of their construction and equipment funding from states, according to 21st Century School Fund’s State of our Schools report. The federal government doesn’t help much, either, adding just 3.2% of the funds schools need, the report says. Local communities tend to be left to foot the bill.

The result is that U.S. public schools face an $85 billion shortfall on facilities, the report found.

Mary Filardo, executive director of the 21st Century School Fund, would like to see the states and federal government do more to support school facilities across the country, she told Facilities Dive. She said the country should view its ailing school infrastructure similar to debt.

“You can not replace that leaky roof, but it's not going away. You still owe that building a roof,” she said.

Filardo said that schools’ HVAC systems are complicated. They’re not just for temperature control, but also air filtration and dehumidification. Retrofitting older schools also presents unique challenges.

“In the 1950s, there would be one outlet in a classroom, maybe,” she said. “If you were lucky, one on each wall.”

From the states’ perspectives, Filardo sees a wide range of approaches to start addressing needs. Wyoming, for example, has used coal lease bonuses — mining company payments for extraction permits — and Massachusetts has implemented a statewide sales tax.

Kristen Nye, executive director of Virginians for Modern Schools, an organization supporting the measure, said voter education proved to be very important in Richmond’s case. She added that the measure opened up opportunities that may have previously seemed out of reach.

“Let's say those plans haven't been touched in well over a decade,” Nye said. “They're sort of blowing the dust off of them and taking them back out in the community again.”

Richmond City Councilor Katherine Jordan led the city’s legislation that put the sales tax measure on the ballot.

“Very often education is a popular talking point on the campaign trail, but when it comes to budget time, somehow it just doesn't make it through,” she said.

Richmond School Board Member Katie Ricard said that if the measure had not passed, it might have been put back on the ballot for a future year. RPS might also have looked to the state for more funding.

Although the measure passed, the school district will need additional funding, said Ricard, noting that she expects the district to continue applying for grants.

“We have 42 school buildings across the district,” Ricard said. “It just gets really costly to maintain all of them. They are old, and their systems are pretty much hanging on by a paperclip, sometimes until they fail.”
