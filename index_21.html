Spokespeople for Publicis Groupe, WPP, Havas and Dentsu declined to comment. Stagwell did not respond to a comment request by time of publication.

“Our automated moderation technology reviews content uploaded to TikTok to identify violations of our Community Guidelines. In a statement provided to Digiday, a TikTok spokesperson said: “We condemn child sexual abuse and exploitation. “Alongside these ongoing actions, as soon as these claims were published, we took further action to identify and remove content, and submit reports to The National Center for Missing & Exploited Children where appropriate. As bad actors continuously adapt their tactics, we work tirelessly to improve our safeguards, and we will continue to actively cooperate with authorities, as we have always done.”

#7490. How SharkNinja is doubling down on its creator strategy

The home appliance brand introduced two new creator programs, SharkNinja First Fans and SharkNinja Storytellers, to tap into the quickly growing influencer economy.

SharkNinja CEO Mark Barrocas in 2008 told a retailer that his plan was to build the company “one five-star review at a time.” In Barrocas’ retelling, the retailer’s executives dismissed the idea as foolish because they couldn’t imagine who would write a review of the brand’s products. That was before the rise of influencers.

Over the ensuing 28 years, SharkNinja has become a multibillion-dollar global company, which built its brand through a social-forward, ultra-connected strategy that includes partnerships with celebrities including David Beckham, Courteney Cox and Alix Earle, alongside a host of influencers.

SharkNinja is doubling down on that strategy with two new programs designed to give storytellers, both inside and outside of the company, access and opportunities to develop and create even more content.

The programs, SharkNinja First Fans, for company employees, and SharkNinja Storytellers, for general consumers and creators, are designed to help the company continue to grow and manage its expanding list of creators and partners.

“We felt we were underserving this community by not creating a space where we could have more scaled one-to-ones with them, bring them further into the fold in terms of our product board maps, and incentivize participation,” said Michelle Crossan-Matos, chief brand and experience officer at SharkNinja.

Over 200,000 posts about SharkNinja’s varied household products, which include blenders, vacuums and ice-cream makers, have been made this year, garnering more than 19 billion impressions, according to the company. Last year, it recorded more than $6.4 billion in sales.

The secret sauce is the company’s practice of fostering, nurturing and actively supporting those who want to create content around SharkNinja’s products, according to Crossan-Matos.

“We bring creators all the way upstream with our products, and we’re having them react to and test and make content with some of our products before we go to market. It’s always been core for our strategy,” said Crossan-Matos.

The new programs use leaderboards and rewards to ensure the content keeps coming. For outside creators, the rewards, which can include commissions and perks, are tied to growth and activity. The most active creators will get exclusive first looks at upcoming products and even get a voice in shaping them. The company has created tutorials and other materials, including an artificial intelligence-powered app, to help the internal creators develop compelling narratives.

Though not exactly commonplace, employer-supported creator programs are not unheard of either. Starbucks has its Green Apron Creators, IBM has a Buzz Squad, Gap opened up its creator ecosystem to employees, and Dell has compensated its employees for sharing content on their own channels. The scope of SharkNinja’s creator efforts are notable.

“I don't know if anybody is doing it the exact way that we are,” Crossan-Matos said. “I think to have both [consumer and employee programs] launch as quickly as we did and at this scale … Our appetite is to drive this globally.”

The company can support these programs on a global scale because of its broad product base, Crossan-Matos said. SharkNinja creates products across 41 consumer categories and launches an average of 25 new products every year.

“We're very lucky to have so many products for so many different types of communities globally,” Crossan-Matos said. “Creators just fit so seamlessly into our co-creation and our storytelling. We think [these initiatives] are going to propel more people to tell their own stories in a way that is even more relatable because they're everyday people.”

Keeping an eye on metrics will be key to measuring program success.

“We’ll be looking at content performance, just like we do with everything. We will be looking for what posts are going viral, what things are gaining traction,” Crossan-Matos said. “We expect really high numbers as we expand.”

#7487. GTA 6 could be a windfall for creators, but standing out will be tough

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Rockstar Games’ Grand Theft Auto (GTA) 6 spawned its own cottage industry in the creator economy.

Creators have spent months building toward the official launch today with hype videos teasing 24-hour marathons from top streamers like Kai Cenat and Darren Jason “IShowSpeed” Watkins Jr., and others dipping back into GTA 5 to capitalize on the buzz. GTA 5 was the fourth most-watched video game on the platform in the last week, according to Twitch Tracker. Pre-launch GTA 6 views topped 40 billion views on YouTube, according to a spokesperson.

The creator economy looks very different from when GTA 5 launched in 2013. This time, some creators have found ways to access GTA 6 early by switching regions, building anticipation before the official release. The day before launch, on November 18, it broke the all-time launch week streaming record with 7.88 million concurrent views, according to Steam Charts. Over the weekend, video game reviewers, streamers, musicians and athletes will play in the immersive game world and the real city it’s inspired by, Miami, Florida.

“The scale of this is hard to even wrap our heads around,” said Ashray Urs, head of Streamlabs, who said he hasn’t seen this level of interest in getting involved in streaming since the COVID pandemic.

The immense popularity of GTA 6, which earned $1.5 billion in revenue before anyone could even play it, is both a boon and a hurdle for the creator economy. Streaming platforms will be inundated with live feeds. Standing out will be difficult, but crucial. Brand partnerships can pay dividends, but your content will need to meet expectations.

Creators have been preparing for this moment for over a year, and two separate delays have only further complicated things. “Everyone has been thinking about this moment. Are you going to do a marathon? Are you going to do casual streams? Are you going to do video on demand [VOD] on YouTube?” said Reed Duchscher, CEO and founder of management company Night Media. “A lot of our clients are just going to be constantly streaming.”

Cody “Clix” Conrad, a professional Fortnite player and streamer with 8.7 million followers on Twitch, is missing esports tournaments to make GTA 6 content, telling Digiday he and a group of his creator friends are renting a house to stream 24/7 (other streamer cohorts, including Vanoss Gaming, are doing something similar). The budget is “under 50k for the entire week” to create content, which he says is the “most efficient marketing spend of the week.”

Duchscher said Night Media’s top streamers, which include Kai Cenat and Darryl Dwayne “DDG” Granberry Jr., aren’t just video game creators, and GTA isn’t necessarily their biggest event of the year (Cenat’s Streamer University pulls in millions of viewers and big brand partnerships). Still, he’s expecting they’ll have double their typical concurrent viewership. “We’re quite confident Kai’s going to have over 100,000 concurrents on his live stream,” Duchscher said.

But Cenat has a built-in audience. For other creators, standing out may be tougher: Experts expect “tens of thousands of live streamers” to play GTA 6 in the coming days. Some think creators will have to “lean heavily” into short-form GTA 6 content to stand out.

“There’s going to be a million moments that occur while you play the game over the weekend, [and creators will need to be] the most entertaining reaction of playthrough of those moments,” Conrad said.

Others should take advantage of the game’s ties to the real world. “The landmarks that were in Vice City [the fictional city in GTA 6], are probably the areas where you stream from,” Duchscher suggested. “You have to get really creative because the clippable moments are going to matter so much more.”

Another strategy for the coming weeks: Skip GTA 6 altogether. Nevada Carney, head of strategy at Mutiny, the gaming arm of social-first creative agency Saylor, said some of their partners are thinking about “counter programming.”

“Smaller creators who know it’s going to be so flooded with GTA might be more inclined to stream some other games that are two, three, or four on the list,” Carney said.

It’s a crowded creator field, but one that has been preparing for well over a year, planting partnership seeds that will blossom in the next few weeks. There have been some serious setbacks: Grand Theft Auto 6 was originally slated to release last fall, then delayed to this spring, then again to today. Those delays messed with brand budgets and partnership plans.

“We thought this game was going to come out much earlier, and so we’ve had to move these back, which has then affected budget and has moved budget out of the fiscal year into another fiscal year, and we’ve had to then renegotiate,” Duchscher said.

Conrad said he doesn’t have any specific partnerships for his upcoming marathon, but hosted an IRL event in Miami themed around GTA 6 with his sponsor, JBL, the day before launch.

Noah Eichen, executive creative director at Mutiny, said the brands they work with have pushed planned partnerships and activations into late December or early next year to accommodate.

“There’s a lot of allocation of budgets that happens that way, just to kind build around this behemoth, knowing that it’s coming…it’s unavoidable. But I think that everyone’s been planning for it for so long that a lot of those budgets got shored up a long time ago,” Eichen said.

Interestingly, brand safety appears to be less of a concern than in the past, according to Mike Murphy O’Reilly, chief revenue officer at Dexerto. “GTA has always been one of those things for brands that, when you talk about advertising or doing something in gaming […] it’s at the top of that list as an absolute no,” he said. “GTA 6…the moment is big enough they’re willing to break their brand safety rules.”

There’s also the expectation that GTA 6 will have a very long tail. The game is launching without its infamous multiplayer mode, which kept the previous installment alive and thriving for 13 years. That mode, known as GTA Online, spawned role-playing communities of content creators (known as GTA RP) that drew millions of eyes and sponsors.

“Brands are trying to figure out the longevity of the partnerships…if they can’t get into GTA now, they know their brand can get into episodic type content that will be started with RP,” said Carney. “Brands are scrambling at the moment because they don’t know how to use their media spend…that seems like that’s the bigger question: What’s the long term play with GTA 6?”

The next few days will be flooded with GTA 6 content, clips, and brand activations. How that extends into the holidays and beyond into 2027, when Rockstar will release the game on PC and likely add its online component, remains to be seen.

“There couldn’t be more momentum headed into this game launch…I’m a little concerned it is going to have a hard time living up to the hype,” said Duchscher.

Eichen thinks creator creativity may keep the hype train going. “It’s almost impossible to guess what’s going to come out of this because people are so creative…[creators are] going to build new things that we never imagined,” he said.

The core problem is a supply-demand imbalance. Streaming ad revenue is growing through increased volume, not higher prices. New inventory floods in, making cheap inventory abundant. As major subscription services continue to promote free ad-supported streaming tiers, the buyers’ market will only continue.

The solution is to grow demand, not increase prices. Fortunately, the ad dollars needed to support the streaming market already exist, sitting in local and midmarket budgets that haven’t been able to buy national CTV efficiently. The opportunity is to bring these advertisers into the broader video and streaming market, then develop ways to keep them investing.

Correcting the imbalance requires creating new demand, then ensuring that content quality and product performance are strong enough to eventually shift pricing dynamics.

When it comes to growth, the glut of cheap national SVOD supply may be a problem for programmers, but it’s an opportunity for local and midmarket advertisers. If the current streaming market is largely aimed at Fortune 500 companies, the industry has to consider the thousands of smaller advertisers that make steady annual investments in local linear buys. Taken collectively, these advertisers represent billions of dollars in ad budgets.

Because these advertisers have smaller budgets, many haven’t tried streaming. It is more cost-efficient for them to execute local and spot buys on linear than to invest in targeted or national streaming campaigns. If that inventory doesn’t deliver results for advertisers outside the Fortune 500, those advertisers will invest their budgets elsewhere, creating another demand imbalance.

Household-level addressability turns cheap impressions into targeted impressions worth buying, even within highly premium inventory. The more information brands have about who will see their ads and whether those ads are likely to resonate with the viewer, the more they are likely to pay.

It is no secret that streaming is the future of TV, and shifting revenue numbers reflect that. To build a sustainable future for media companies and advertisers, the entire ecosystem must first provide ease of access and then addressability.

Making streaming easier to buy at scale would help solve the demand problem. Once more advertisers are in the market, addressability creates greater competition for impressions, solving the pricing imbalance.

Brands paying influencers to make campaign creative have become key drivers of investment in the booming creator economy. Creative agencies might want to take note.

“You get the authentic, scroll-stopping content that people want to engage each with. But you also get the reach,” said Katy Ball, head of social at M&C Saatchi Sport & Entertainment.

As spending on paid social channels — and video in particular — continues to rise, demand for creative assets to appear in those ad slots is increasing.

The number of assets attached to individual campaigns has risen 30% in the last five years, according to a CreativeX study of 115,990 ad campaigns, representing $11 billion in ad spend. Per the study, just five assets receive 88% of media investment in an average campaign.

The need for more campaign assets is fueling the growth of asset-led spending, the fastest growing segment of the creator economy, according to a report published this week by research consultancy Madison & Wall.

Currently, 12% of the $112 billion spent on creators globally goes towards creators producing ads on behalf of brands, per the study. That’s expected to rise 16% every year through 2030.

The days when brands principally paid creators to rent their following are rapidly disappearing. Influencers are now media partners, ambassadors and increasingly, cogs in the creative production supply chain. And brands see creator-made ads as a solution to their social asset shortage.

“For us, the appeal is simple. Buying space in someone’s feed doesn’t mean you’ve earned their attention. Good creators understand what their audiences care about and how to give them something worth stopping for,” said Jared Kozel, co-founder and chief creative officer at agency Super Nice. “That’s valuable creative judgment to bring into an ad no matter the brand or category.”

Influencer marketing experts note that several different but related practices are included under the broader umbrella of creator-led production.

While some brands simply pay creators a fee in order to receive video or still assets for use in a campaign, whitelisting (also called “dark advertising”) has become a favored method of managing such arrangements.

In this context, an influencer grants a brand limited access to their social media accounts, allowing them to book a paid media campaign through that account, using assets made by the creator while taking advantage of discrete audience targeting.

“What we’re seeing is a lot of recutting of media spend to be allocated towards creator development in order to fund whitelisting,” said Olivia Wedderburn, head of influence at Born Social.

Madison & Wall’s research suggested that funding for creative production may also be coming from PR, social or experimental budgets, as well as traditional media or production pots of money.

Another related practice, user generated content, sees brands paying regular social media users to license their content as creative assets in ad campaigns.

“They have to feed their their content engines, and they’re opening up as many different avenues as they can,” said Andy Ryan, co-founder at social and influencer agency Komodo.

The relative low cost of working with influencers, versus creative production agencies, is the primary driver behind brand spending on creator-made assets.

Although production spending is often a small fraction of an overall campaign budget, marketers are keen to squeeze every non-working (that is, non-media) dollar for all it’s worth. While that same desire is pushing some brands toward generative AI production techniques, others are turning to creators.

But as this type of spending grows, so to does the need to manage it effectively. “It’s never been more complicated to negotiate usage rights or exclusivity,” said Wedderburn.

Billion Dollar Boy, for example, recently added features to its Companion creator management tool designed to better facilitate creator-led production efforts and manage usage rights negotiations.

“The creator economy has never been more competitive, heightening the urgency for brands to understand the market context,” said Thomas Walters, the agency’s chief innocation officer and co-founder.

At the same time, increased brand spending in this area is already leading to an uptick in the fees commanded by some creators. Last year, agency practitioners reported creator rates ticking up 10-20% due to demand for expanded rights.

There’s also some evidence to show that creator-made assets perform as well as, or even better than, traditional advertising spots in a social context.

A report by System1, WPP Media and Goat published in June found that creator ads achieved 3.2X the brand-awareness lift for category leader ads as traditional advertising creative.

“We’re seeing a lot of brands say that is more valuable in terms of conversion and reach than some of the quick-and-dirty assets they previously would have relied on,” said Wedderburn.

That’s not unexpected. Audiences recognize the visual cues, language and production methods employed by creators as native to TikTok or Instagram, while clocking films shot on a closed set or using more traditional methods as foreign that same environment, a lesson that major advertisers like Nike are reluctantly absorbing.

“If programs are set up correctly, creators can deliver volume and variety faster and more cost effectively than a traditional production cycle,” said Nicholas Spiro, chief commercial officer, Viral Nation. “The shift we’re seeing is brands starting to plan creators across their media budgets, rather than as a separate influencer line item alone.”
