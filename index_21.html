#7371. LinkedIn study explores B2B buying decisions

Let Social Media Today's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The research highlights the importance of relational factors, such as testimonials and peer validation, when it comes to influencing client purchases.

LinkedIn has published new research which examines what drives B2B buying decisions, and provides guidance on how brands can ensure that their products are as appealing as possible to prospective clients.

The study, conducted in conjunction with WARC and Lions Advisory, analyzes the factors that go into creating "buyability." The authors examined 700 B2B case studies in order to discover the crucial factors that influence purchase decisions across these examples.

The findings show that many of the key factors that drive decision-making are relational, as opposed to being grounded in product functionality.

As explained by Mimi Turner, global head of thought leadership strategy at LinkedIn’s B2B Institute: “Confidence in product capability is still very important. But once baseline requirements are met, the top priority is no longer marginal product superiority or price. It becomes whether the decision can be successfully defended even if outcomes go wrong.”

In order to test this, the study measured each of the 700 campaigns against various criteria related to buying signals.

The researchers then segmented the campaigns into "high buyability" campaigns, which were those that included three or more of these signals, and "low buyability" initiatives, which included zero, one or two signals.

The results shoed that high buyability campaigns — meaning those with more focus on testimonials, peer validation and measures of industry leadership — significantly outperformed low buyability campaigns across commercial and brand measures.

“Evaluated against the WARC B2B Effectiveness Ladder, High Buyability campaigns were also 1.7x more likely to be classified as ‘Strategic Assets,’ the highest rung of effectiveness, reserved for initiatives that drive sustained commercial growth,” Turner said.

The data also found that customer-centric insights were present in 83% of high buyability campaigns, which were also four times more likely to feature customer testimonials.

In other words, including more elements that reinforce buying decisions through relatable examples and external validation can have a major impact on reassuring buyers and securing more deals.

LinkedIn further tested these concepts in a campaign for ServiceNow, which specifically focused on these buyability elements.

“The results included a 10x increase in consideration, a 13x increase in unaided brand awareness over three years, and subscription revenue up by 23% over the same period,” Turner said.

Turner said these findings indicate that recommendations, relationships and relatable buying situations can have a significant influence on campaign outcomes.

These notes could help B2B marketers improve the resonance of their campaigns.

#7370. Why Qualcomm teamed with F1’s new star in race for sports marketing ROI

The company’s consumer brand Snapdragon signed 20-year-old phenom Kimi Antonelli as a brand ambassador as it looks to connect with racing’s growing audience.

Snapdragon, the consumer brand of chipmaker Qualcomm, on Tuesday launched an anthem film around its partnership with Formula 1 driver Kimi Antonelli. The 20-year-old driver for the Mercedes-AMG Petronas team — of which the brand is also a partner — became the youngest Formula 1 World Champion ever earlier this month.

The video is part of a campaign timed to the 2026 Las Vegas Grand Prix on Nov. 21. It will run on F1 broadcasts outside the U.S. on Sky, on digital and in partnership with channels including Sports Illustrated and YouTube. Apple TV is the exclusive broadcast partner of F1 in the U.S., which prevents brands like Snapdragon from running traditional broadcast ads around the race.

“We've got other ways to bring it to life,” Qualcomm CMO Don McGuire said of the campaign. “We've got creators involved, we've got remixing involved, we have slipstreaming involved… hopefully turning it into an anthem for him that has really long legs.”

A 60-second hero video follows Antonelli during a day at a hotel. Wearing smart glasses and using a tablet, both powered by Snapdragon chips, the phenom listens to a version of the traditional Neapolitan song “Funiculì, Funiculà,” trains in the gym and kicks off a social media trend. The ad ends with a tagline, “Jamme, Jamme. To the top!,” which quotes the song and speaks to his meteoric rise in racing.

“We've been watching Kimi for quite some time, and we have always believed that he was meant for greatness and that he was going to ascend very quickly. I think we made the right bet,” McGuire said.

Snapdragon’s partnership with Antonelli builds on the brand’s deal with Mercedes-AMG Petronas, a strategic collaboration that kicked off in 2023. Qualcomm had previously partnered with the team in the 2010s, a deal that was not “ROI-driven, marketing-driven,” said McGuire, who became CMO in 2021. The new partnership looks to better connect the brand’s objectives with the growing audience of F1, both in the U.S. and globally.

“The sport more and more is not something that I can judge based on [advertising value equivalency]. We have some pretty good placements on the Mercedes cars, especially with cockpit cameras and things like that, but it's just so fleeting, from a visibility standpoint,” McGuire said.

Those issues are exacerbated by the Apple TV deal, which limits brands’ ability to measure impact during broadcasts. Qualcomm is committed to F1, Mercedes-AMG Petronas and Antonelli, especially as it seeks to bring the partnership from content and storytelling through activations and hospitality.

“We believe we're already seeing higher ROI coming out of some things that we've done in the short term,” McGuire said. “We believe in the sport, why we were there and the fundamentals.”

Outside of F1, Snapdragon is no stranger to sports. Since the start of the 2024-25 season, the brand has been the principal shirt sponsor of Manchester United. Despite the iconic soccer team’s on- and off-field issues in recent years, the deal has yielded “humongous ROI” for Snapdragon, McGuire said.

“When you're dealing with a sports brand that big, at scale, the brand can withstand the ebbs and flows of performance on the pitch because the fan base is so huge,” the executive explained. “That's the benchmark that we use [for sports partnerships] and maybe it's a little unfair because they are arguably the biggest sports brand in the world.”

Snapdragon’s latest F1 marketing comes on the heels of a holiday campaign that launched earlier this month. At the heart of “The Gift That Changes Everything” is a sweet 60-second hero spot that shows how a mom and dad make Christmas seem to come sooner. The parents use a Snapdragon-powered PC to learn how to build a "time machine" that gives their child a special Christmas morning experience.

The campaign is running across broadcast, connected TV and paid social in the U.S. with CTV and paid social in the U.K., Germany and France. Created in partnership with 72andSunny New York and 72andSunny Toronto, the effort iterates on a “Snapdragon. That’s How” creative platform that launched earlier this year. The campaign has seen strong preliminary results, McGuire reported.

The holiday creative arrives as the brand prepares for a season that could see hardware makers impacted by memory shortages, reducing Snapdragon’s total addressable market at a time when the Nvidia scandal is roiling the chip market. The brand continues to work to build awareness and affinity, regardless of short-term market conditions.

“This is about building muscle and emotion that can sustain the long-term,” McGuire said. “We feel really strongly that we had to lean into holiday as if it were any other holiday season, regardless of the macro conditions.”

#7364. Paid media now plays largest role in brand-funded creator growth: report

While pumping paid dollars into creator content is the biggest piece of the category pie, using creators for production is the fastest growing, Madison & Wall found.

Madison & Wall’s research illustrates how creator marketing is evolving further away from the narrow #sponsored post model — what the advisory and consulting firm calls audience-led spend — into a multifaceted media engine that is core to consumer outreach. The advertiser-funded creator economy is forecast to grow 15% this year to $116 billion and reach $178 billion by 2030. It is on pace to grow at twice the rate of the total ad market as brands try to meet demands around creative content volume, authenticity and audience access.

“[Creators] have become an essential layer of the commercial advertising and media supply chain, serving simultaneously as media channels, creative production houses, commerce drivers, and performance engines,” the report reads.

Putting paid media dollars behind existing creator content that performs well has become the largest aspect of the advertiser-funded creator economy, providing reach and scalability without the TV commercial gloss that many consumers have begun to reject. Reliably achieving organic reach has also become less certain as platform algorithms change. Due to these factors, media-led creator spending is expected to reach $83 billion by 2030.

Marketers are not broadly expanding their budgets to support creator initiatives, but rather siphoning off resources previously allocated to channels like traditional media, digital search, paid social and programmatic. Madison & Wall emphasized that creator partnerships often employ multiple approaches, including combining audience-led and media-led spend, rather than focusing on one bucket. The study did not analyze consumer-funded creator income, meaning subscriptions, tips or other forms of revenue creators receive from their audiences.

While media-led spending is the biggest piece of the creator pie at the moment, asset-led spending, where brands pay creators to produce content, effectively using them as a content studio, is the fastest growing, currently representing 12% of the market. That helps explain the budgets being drawn from traditional creative production and could be a detriment to third-party creative agencies and production shops, which are also contending with the rise of artificial intelligence-generated content.

Those marketing services intermediaries, as well as talent managers and technology platforms, are retaining about 8% of advertiser-funded creator investments on a net basis as part of what Madison & Wall terms infrastructure spend. The rest of the market is made up of a miscellaneous segment that accounts for 7% of spend and encompasses creator engagements such as live events, barter arrangements and name, image and likeness deals.

The second part of Madison & Wall’s study will break down some of the growing pains the creator economy faces as it looks to sustain momentum, including fragmentation, pricing inconsistencies, contractual logjams, measurement shortcomings and questions of ownership over areas like data and intellectual property. That installment will also dive into creator fatigue, a mounting problem for marketers who have valued creators as a more approachable and trustworthy way to catch consumer attention.

#7356. Brands Must Sharpen Their Storytelling For Agentic Commerce

When I went from being a Disney entertainment executive to Marriott CMO in 2013, I moved quickly to bring more storytelling function into a brand environment. We set up social media hubs around the world, which became a lab for using new tech to accelerate company stories.

Agentic commerce will soon impact marketing the way social media has done — both having started with a sense of unknown until hitting critical mass. Plus, storytelling in the age of agentic commerce has a potential topspin when agents, transacting on behalf of customers, gain insight beyond what the brand may have planned.

Let’s call this Creative Data 2.0, where connected loops help guide internal input and external output.

Leaders from different departments should meet in one shared group to decide how the company uses new technology. They’d weigh what the tech can do against the company’s goals, and the kind of presence the brand wants to have. Companies did something similar when social media was new, with several teams working together to figure out how to tell the brand’s story there.

In this era of AI, marketers influence consumers as well as the agents that act on their behalf. Agentic commerce will eventually convene multiple disciplines to develop a cross-functional playbook in service of coordinated goals. We’ll see transactions optimized because enterprise data presented the product mix to advance the brand story. We’ll see agentic commerce enabling seamless payments and sign on.

But brands must be intentional in their strategy and storytelling in order to be known by the agent. In the accelerated pace of AI, the timing and silos of brand storytelling are collapsing, as agents are tracking brand news and “always on” is the norm.

First, decide what role the brand plays in agentic commerce. From there, it is easier to decide how to assemble the work, influence the agents, and evaluate responses.

Depending on your brand’s sector or stage of development, these are some of the agentic roles organizations might need.

Advisor: Helps with high level information to navigate options and choices. Beauty brands for instance do this routinely if a favorite product has been reformulated. Brands getting into agentic commerce need to consider being more aggressive updating information whenever there’s a change.

Assistant: Anticipates various use cases and offers personalized options across categories. Think of how retailers organize party planning décor, food, and beverage by occasion. Brands need to think about how to structure information about their entire products in a similar fashion.

Operator: Creates a one stop shop with enough information to empower agents to do the heavy-lifting around shopping and buying. Think of how airlines compile an entire travel itinerary with locations, dates, prices and special activities all the way through to booking the trip.

It’s important to remember that the agentic advisor is not just content management; the agentic assistant is not just bookmarking the shopping cart; and the agentic operator isn’t just showing promos for related products and services.

These agents should act more like a customer’s BFF. They listen to preferences and eventually complete the purchase. And these roles shape the brand story.

Marketers today emphasize AI for efficiency in creative, performance, and process, but they must now deepen that scope to include agentic commerce.

Gartner forecasts 20% of commerce will be agentic by 2030, which coincides with predicted 20% adoption of programmable money, or digital funds guided by a framework of rules and safeguards.

This means the terms of purchase can be automated too. That combination of commercial change is seismic, as agents decide whether your product or service is right for a consumer.

Who better to navigate customer insight than CMOs, creative leaders with expertise in translating brand value? As storytellers, these skills can creatively power the team’s new relationship with agents designed to learn about your brand. As the flurry of AI headlines continues, a clear path exists for marketers and media leaders to bring brand storytelling and agentic commerce closer together.

#7346. WTF is a unified media platform?

Editor’s note: These technologies are ill-defined, please share feedback to “editorial@digiday.com” to talk more.

Ad tech has never been short on acronyms. Its latest crop includes: unified advertising platforms (UAPs), unified media platforms (UMPs), and unified buying platforms (UBPs).

This kind of language has added a new layer of confusion to the space, and ad executives across adland have been saying, “WTF…?” Digiday is here to help by trying to bring some clarity to the matter.

A simple way to approach the question is to think of such terms — UAP, UBP and UMP — as overlapping labels for technology that promises to make advertising easier to buy, manage and optimize across an increasingly fragmented media landscape.

Of course, AI is central to the pitch. Advocates of such technologies maintain that instead of separately operating campaigns across Google, Meta, Amazon and the open web, advertisers could potentially use one system to determine where money goes, then execute those decisions through the relevant platforms.

Some providers propose bringing buying and selling infrastructure together. Others want to sit above existing systems and coordinate them. This is an important distinction to keep in mind, as connective software is not the same as controlling how an advertiser spends its next dollar.

At its simplest, a UMP coordinates planning, buying, optimization, and measurement across multiple media channels. Its remit could include programmatic display, CTV, retail media, search, social, and direct publisher relationships.

According to most sources, the idea is not necessarily to replace every existing media trading infrastructure, i.e., demand-side and supply-side platforms, etc., but to make them work together more efficiently.

That is distinct from the traditional remit of a DSP, many of which have spent years expanding the inventory and formats available through their own buying interfaces. A unified platform, by contrast, promises to operate across buying environments that no single DSP controls.

Jay Friedman, co-founder of CartographAI, told Digiday that the emerging proposition is about budget fluidity, not simply consolidating DSPs and SSPs. “We define a UMP as a platform that executes buys across all primary digital channels (search, social, programmatic, and direct). This may or may not include agents,” Friedman told Digiday. “Traditionally, this has been the domain of agency order management systems,” he said, noting how several new entrants are hoping to challenge these players.

The terms overlap, but they differ in what they promise to unify. The chart below traces that range, from handling an individual transaction to coordinating campaigns across channels and deciding where budgets go.

Keith Weisberg of Unplugged Collective told Digiday UBPs would initially come to market as a repackaging of existing tools. He said the deeper shift would come when buyers could bring their data to the places ads run and delegate spending decisions to AI agents.

“I’d treat that as a net-new product, not a DSP feature,” he said. “However, we’re all plugged into DSPs, and even if it’s a new product, it’ll roll out via our current commercial relationships, because that trust (and legal contracting) took years.”

Ana Milicevic, co-founder of Sparrow Advisers, described the labels as directional distinctions rather than settled product categories. She added, “It’s not all just a collection of black boxes. Then that’s a net new layer in the stack.” The key distinction is whether a platform merely pushes buttons in existing interfaces or makes independent decisions about where budgets go.

Ciarán O’Kane, general partner at First Party Capital, was more skeptical about novelty. In wording expressly cleared for attribution, he characterized UMPs and UBPs as “meta DSPs” that act as workflow layers on top of existing systems by plugging into APIs. He also said many emerging players in the space that use this terminology are revisiting approaches the industry has tried before.

Media-buying teams have long struggled with the fragmentation of conducting ad campaigns using different buying interfaces, datasets, and measurement methods. AI can make the operational work of combining and interpreting those inputs less expensive, but it does not automatically create comparable performance data or independent buying authority.

“The frustration is very, very old. What’s new now is that AI is letting us operationally skip through some hurdles that were previously insurmountable,” Milicevic told Digiday. Tasks that once demanded a substantial analytics stack and specialist labor can increasingly be assisted by repeatable prompts and automated processes.

The commercial question is whether cheaper orchestration creates an entirely new buyer or simply a more convenient way to operate existing platforms. For advertisers, that distinction determines whether the new technology saves money, reallocates money, or merely adds another vendor to pay.

In an Oct. 8 blog post, titled “universal buying platform,” Apostrophe co-founders Brian O’Kelley and Anne Coghlan outlined a broader buying layer intended to connect intent, execution, and measurement across advertising environments, citing the recent launch of Amazon Ads Agent as an example of this trend.

Their argument is a public proposal, not evidence that a single independent platform already performs every part of the workflow at scale — O’Kelley did not respond to Digiday’s request for comment by press time.

Other companies are approaching the problem from different directions, with Olyzon — an entity that used the term “agentic orchestration platform” in its recent CTV launch material — serving as one example.

Jules Minvielle, co-founder and CEO of Olyzon, told Digiday the company does not own inventory and uses agents to coordinate buying through existing platforms, with advertiser-controlled approvals. “A DSP is one place where you can buy. Orchestration is all the work around the buy: building the plan, deciding where each dollar goes, activating across every platform, watching delivery, measuring, and feeding what worked into the next plan,” he wrote in an emailed statement, adding that uneven API access and inconsistent measurement remain substantial obstacles.

Ravi Patel, co-founder and CEO of SWYM.ai, drew a distinction between a common interface and control over the budget. “It can manage the ‘what’ and the ‘how much.’ It can’t manage the ‘which impression.’ That distinction is the whole story,” he explained, adding that campaign setup and pacing across platforms are already possible through APIs. However, impression-level optimization within the major walled gardens remains in their own systems.

Lucas Cridland, CEO of Basin Media, questioned whether agents are changing the underlying buying process. “At the moment, many environments are agentically operating in the same supply chain flow. They’re just doing it with agents buying; they’re not radically changing the way the process works. And that’s where the real edge exists.”

Not necessarily. Many of the new propositions depend on DSPs continuing to execute transactions, access inventory, and optimize within their own environments. The change may be who decides which platform gets the advertiser’s budget in the first place.

Milicevic maintained that “value always accrues to whoever is closest to the decision or outright owns the decision.”

Ergo, if DSPs cannot move upstream into genuine cross-channel decision-making, they risk becoming “dumb pipes,” i.e., a commoditized, essential infrastructure whose underlying value is less visible to buyers.

Incumbent DSPs, however, have established advertiser relationships, bidding technology, data integrations and scale. They could develop their own orchestration capabilities. A new intermediary would need to show that its independence produces better decisions, rather than simply adding another fee, according to several sources consulted for this piece.

The biggest obstacle may be the very platforms that independent systems are supposed to coordinate. Google, Meta, and Amazon control their own buying tools, data, and optimization systems. Access through an API does not necessarily provide the granular information or authority required to compare results and independently move budgets.

Trust is also an issue. A unified interface can display reports from multiple platforms without proving that the outcomes are comparable. Attribution windows, campaign objectives, and self-reported measurement differ across channels, meaning an independent buying layer needs a credible basis for deciding that one dollar is better spent in one environment than another.

Friedman was skeptical that interoperability would resolve the underlying problems, and highlighted how Google and Meta are unlikely to make their systems interoperable. He further questioned whether agencies could build the context and routing infrastructure needed to translate employees’ instructions into accurate buying decisions, even if every platform supported the Model Context Protocol. “Interoperability won’t solve anything,” he told Digiday.

Greg MacDonald, founder of Chelsea Strategies, said addressing media buying’s growing complexity could reduce spending on its mechanics and, over time, potentially improve advertiser outcomes. However, he remained cautious about how far unified platforms could go. “I’m skeptical that the walled gardens will give buyers more control than they have today,” he told Digiday, pointing to persistent difficulties with frequency capping across programmatic formats, let alone disconnected marketplaces.

“But you don’t have to solve every problem, and certainly not on day one to be useful,” MacDonald added. He compared the opportunity with programmatic guaranteed buying, which made the insertion-order process more efficient without fundamentally changing direct media buying.

Meanwhile, Milicevic said the more plausible near-term customer may be a multichannel advertiser between the largest global brands and small businesses that largely rely on a single platform. She also stressed the need for independent validation of results, rather than taking each platform’s own reporting at face value.

AI agents could help automate campaign setup, inventory discovery, negotiation, execution, and reporting. That makes agentic advertising relevant to the unified-platform debate, but not synonymous with it: automating tasks is different from making accountable, cross-channel budget decisions.

Unplugged Collective maps seven ways AI could change advertising (see graphic below). Per Weisberg, two are central to the unified buying debate: how agents decide where to spend and how they carry out those decisions across platforms.

Industry initiatives such as AdCP and IAB Tech Lab’s agentic work aim to establish ways for systems to communicate and execute workflows. Technical standards could reduce integration costs, but they cannot by themselves force walled gardens to disclose comparable data or surrender commercial control.

Seasoned industry observers have seen enough buzz phrases come and go to feel confident enough to make tongue-in-cheek observations when a new acronym becomes de rigueur among the industry’s chattering classes.

“I saw ‘UMP’ and I thought: ad network,” quipped Terence Kawaja, CEO, LUMA Partners, a prominent investment bank in the space, speaking on stage at this year’s Prebid Summit – channeling the industry adage, “everything is an ad network.”

Meanwhile, one prominent industry source, who requested anonymity to preserve industry relationships, dismissed the latest acronyms as another serving of “word salad.” However, the barbs, playful or otherwise, raise a palpable industry debate: As AI makes it cheaper to run campaigns across platforms, who gets to decide where the money goes?

The clearest test is whether advertisers entrust meaningful cross-channel allocation to an independent system — and can demonstrate that the decisions improve outcomes. Smaller-scale automation and campaign management do not, on their own, prove that a new market category has emerged.

Milicevic identified three indicators: real advertiser budgets moving under independent allocation, credible measurement outside any single walled garden, and changes in how marketing organizations structure teams. “If it’s truly structural, it’ll show up in the PNL, and if it’s slideware and fluff, then that’ll make its way into award entries and kind of the PR circuit,” she said.

For now, the most useful distinction is between controlling the infrastructure through which ads are purchased and controlling the decision about where the money goes. The latter is the more ambitious promise. Whether anyone can deliver it independently, at scale, and at a cost advertisers will accept remains unresolved.

Last week, OpenAI launched a ChatGPT Business Hub, a dashboard for businesses that claim and verify their accounts. It offers data on how their sites appear in ChatGPT, including mentions, profile views, website clicks and the topics driving discovery. For publishers, that offers an early view into activity they’ve largely had to estimate with third-party tools or infer from referral traffic.

“Until now, everything publishers knew about their presence in ChatGPT came from third-party tools running prompts at scale and estimating, or from referral traffic after the fact. This is a very early, very limited Search Console moment for ChatGPT,” said John Shehata, CEO and founder of publisher SEO analytics firm NewzDash.

Publishing execs are mixed on how useful they thought the data would be for their AI visibility and audience development strategies. Of the half dozen large publishers Digiday spoke with for this story, half were in the process of setting up their business accounts.

It’s early days, but one industry exec, who requested anonymity, said legal teams may need to be extra cautious around some of the potential implications of OpenAI’s terms of service.

OpenAI’s terms require businesses to agree not to pursue certain legal claims against the company, including disputes involving content published on their websites before they signed up. That may cause headaches for legal teams at publishers already grappling with how to protect and monetize their content in the AI era, if accessing valuable first-party visibility data comes at the cost of relinquishing certain legal rights — a trade-off that may give some pause before signing up.

When reached for comment, an OpenAI spokesperson said the terms “were not intended to cover this scenario.”

Despite those concerns, some publishers see a potential commercial benefit. Two publishing execs said they were interested in using the data to support their GEO offerings. AI attribution and measurement is a major hurdle for those businesses, but they said insights from brands’ first-party data could help publishers assess the performance of branded content campaigns alongside data from third-party tools.

“These insights potentially provide a really valuable piece of the puzzle that should validate our own success criteria around visibility and citations,” said Carly Steven, Daily Mail director of SEO and editorial ecommerce.

Shehata said the Business Hub data could help publishers demonstrate the value of their GEO offerings to brands, but cautioned that a publisher’s own visibility metrics don’t prove a brand’s campaign delivered results.

“With authorized access to a client’s data, [publishers can] finally prove that recommendations move real platform metrics. That closes the measurement loop missing from every GEO pitch. But a publisher’s own citation count is not proof of a client’s GEO return. A brand campaign still needs evidence at the brand level. The commercial opportunity is selling better evidence and better decisions, not citation counts,” he said.

Another publishing exec was less convinced the data would help their GEO business.

“Since [the hub] is only [tracking] mentions of the business owner, it isn’t so useful to our needs around influencing AI answers on behalf of partner brands. But we are signing up just so we can see if there’s anything there,” the exec said.

For example, if a brand pays for a publisher’s GEO offering, that publisher produces sponsored content articles about that brand’s product, and ChatGPT recommends the product with a citation link to the publisher’s sponsored content, the Business Hub wouldn’t report the mention of the product to the publisher, the exec explained. This means publishers will need to continue to use third-party AI visibility tools to measure those citations.

“At this stage, I see brand mentions in answers akin to billboards. It makes sense for Nike to have a billboard but not [a publisher],” the exec said.

Lily Ray, vp of SEO strategy and research at performance marketing agency Amsive, pointed out that the Business Hub tracks mentions of a business website, rather than its brand name surfacing in ChatGPT.

That offers a “limited view” of what is considered a mention in ChatGPT, though Ray expects the measurement to evolve. It also makes click-through rates appear disproportionately high. On the Business Hub dashboard for her SEO consultancy, Ray recorded four clicks from just 25 mentions. While “they’re very small numbers in absolute terms,” Ray said the first-party data gives her another point of comparison against metrics from third-party tools.

While the dashboard offers an incomplete picture, Shehata said publishers can still use its data to establish a baseline and track changes over time. He recommends three steps  publishers take once they get access to their Business Hub data. The first is to use the data to set a baseline for mentions, clicks and discovery topics so that the publisher can evaluate trends over time.

The second is to use discovery topics, which are “the most valuable data for audience teams,” to steer editorial experiments, Shehata said. These topics show which themes ChatGPT associates with a publisher’s site, where the AI treats the publisher as an authority, and white spaces around topics the publisher wants to own.

“A travel publisher seeing activity around family travel can strengthen those guides with original reporting and watch whether the numbers move,” Shehata said.

Publishers should also separate the metrics in the Business Hub dashboard and their outcomes, he added.

“A citation is a visibility outcome. A visit is an audience outcome. A subscription or sale is a business outcome. Publishers need to measure those separately,” Shehata said. He suggested publishers compare Business Hub trends against their own analytics, such as ChatGPT referrals, newsletter signups and conversions.

“If reported visibility grows while referrals stay flat, that raises a hard question about value the dashboard alone won’t answer,” Shehata said.

Mail Metro Media now thinks “more like a talent agency” and commissions “like a broadcaster”, according to Hannah Blake, its managing director of creator and new media. Its creators have, in other words, become the business.

Blake spelled out that risk on stage at the Digiday Media Strategies London event in October.

“The huge risk is that we make these people super famous and then they go and get signed by a different agent,” she said.

So in September the ad sales arm for the Daily Mail hired David White from Whalar as head of creators, with a brief to build an in-house talent agency that represents its creators and signs outside talent too. White was the first hire for the company’s Stars business in New York, the talent management arm that handles creators’ careers beyond brand deals and was also a senior talent manager there.

What the business ultimately ends up with is still open. Blake described something that “potentially could look more like a talent agency and an employer,” and she hopes to have it in market by the end of the year. The final shape matters because running a talent agency would be a big change of job for a newspaper group’s ad sales team. It exists to sell audiences to brands while an agent’s job is to get the best deal for the talent. Mail Metro Media would be trying to do both at once.

That’s the potted history of how Mail Metro Media reached this point. The full story is a lot more nuanced.

It started four years ago when Daily Mail launched Eliza, a social-only brand for young women fronted by six fashion and beauty creators. Its hit format, “Guess Which Outfit’s Most Expensive”, puts two women on the street, one in a £10,000 outfit and the other in a high-street copy, and leaves viewers to guess which is which. It kept going viral, people kept coming back and advertisers soon followed. That’s despite it being relatively small next to the rest of the Mail’s portfolio, and yet “we were doing lots of direct advertisers relationships quite quickly”, Blake said. Fast forward to now,  and brands often book content on Eliza and then want its creators posting on their own accounts too, which Mail Metro Media packages into the deal.

The Mail’s own TikTok was a different story. The Mail and Metro launched on the platform around the same time as Eliza and the former became the biggest news publisher there, with 20 million followers, on the back of rapid-fire news clips. But the money didn’t follow.  “The huge issue was there was no monetization there,” Blake said, even though publishers had widely expected TikTok to start paying them for views. Advertisers weren’t keen either, Every rival was posting the same kind of clips and the Daily Mail couldn’t build a brand into footage it had brought in from elsewhere.

Together, they showed the publisher that the money was in shows it owned and creators audiences came back for. It shifted course accordingly, and over the past year it relaunched the Mail and Metro on social around repeatable shows it owns, such as the sports debate show “Dugout Debate” with creators at the front and helping shape them. Blake said the operation now pulls 5 billion monthly video views across 10 brands and signed its first seven-figure deal last month. With that success comes a new kind of risk. The creators fronting those shows have built followings of their own and “now some of the superstars are beginning to emerge,” Blake said. Left unchecked, the publisher ends up making stars for outside agencies to sign.

In some ways, the publisher has already been acting like an agent of sorts. Alongside its core bundle where brands pay one flat rate for a run of content with “no differentiation between the talent,” Blake said it has spent the past year selling individual creators on their own channels. It calls this add-on “Creator Amp.” One example is Mimi Yates, a young journalist who fronts much of the news output and has built a following of her own. Brands can book her as part of a wider campaign or on her own, and on their personal accounts the creators “all set their own rates,” Blake said.

That arrangement was easy to manage while the talent was still unknown.

“When we started, our creators were all emerging,” Blake said. With bigger names now coming through, the publisher wants something sturdier. “We are now at the point where we’re beginning to formalize that relationship a bit more,” she said, and hiring White was the first step.

He will recruit from two places. One is from its own staff. The other is outside talent Blake described as “the next generation of emerging news and entertainment talent”.

Then there’s the small matter of journalism. The same person can read the headlines and sell the mascara, and the publisher would be both their employer and their agent. That’s a lot of hats for one newsroom.

White’s first signings will show how far the publisher plans to take this venture. Blake thinks publishers are headed this way regardless. “I actually think it’s just a byproduct of where the opportunity is,” she said.

Increasingly, publishers see a talent operation as the next step in their latest pivot to video, which runs on personalities whose audiences, and the ad money chasing them, follow them wherever they go.

“Publishers are responding to the audience’s desire for personality-led formats while limiting strategic dependence on individual journalists and managing talent,” said Laura Darcey, research analyst at Enders Analysis.

The opportunity is to bring these advertisers into the broader video and streaming market, then develop ways to keep them investing.

When it comes to growth, the glut of cheap national SVOD supply may be a problem for programmers, but it’s an opportunity for local and midmarket advertisers. If the current streaming market is largely aimed at Fortune 500 companies, the industry has to consider the thousands of smaller advertisers that make steady annual investments in local linear buys. Taken collectively, these advertisers represent billions of dollars in ad budgets.

Making streaming easier to buy at scale would help solve the demand problem. Once more advertisers are in the market, addressability creates greater competition for impressions, solving the pricing imbalance.

The complaint traces the funding disparity back to 1890, when Maryland wanted to take advantage of the federal Second Morrill Act.

That law sought to bolster the country’s land-grant universities with additional annual funding. But the money wasn’t available for states whose public land-grant institutions had racially discriminatory admissions unless they had a comparable college “of like character” for Black students, per the statute.

According to the lawsuit, Henry Alvord — who was then the president of University of Maryland, College Park, the state’s first land-grant institution — was “in a dilemma.”

The university “was in desperate need of additional funding but the idea of permitting Black students to attend [UMD] was unfathomable because his and many others’ racist beliefs would never allow [UMD] to be integrated,” the lawsuit states.

Alvord eventually found a solution to his dilemma in the Princess Anne Academy, a “small, remote and run-down preparatory school on the southern point of the eastern shore” that could — on paper — become a land-grant college for Black students, operated by Morgan College, which later became the HBCU Morgan State University.

However, the “lion’s share” of the new funding under the Second Morrill Act went to UMD, which did not admit Black students until a 1950 lawsuit.

“Black students would be sent to far-off Princess Anne to receive junior and senior high school-level classes on Princess Anne Academy’s dilapidated campus instead of a school of ‘like character’ to [UMD]’s state-of-the-art college,” plaintiffs said in the complaint.

According to the lawsuit, the underfunding and neglect of the academy — which later became UMES — continued throughout the 20th century and amid regular federal scrutiny of segregation in Maryland’s higher educational system and disparately low funding for UMES.

In 2023, then-President Joe Biden’s secretaries of education and agriculture wrote to Maryland Gov. Wes Moore — who is named as a defendant in the new lawsuit along with other top officials — to address the historical funding disparities at UMES as part of a broader effort to encourage more funding at historically Black land-grant colleges across the country.

“The longstanding and ongoing underinvestment in University of Maryland Eastern Shore disadvantages the students, faculty, and community that the institution serves,” the Biden officials wrote. “Furthermore, it may contribute to a lack of economic activity that would ultimately benefit Maryland.”

They calculated that UMES had been underfunded to the tune of $321.2 million just over the prior 30 years before the letter.

“Education agencies and institutions that consider race, national origin, or sex as a means to withhold opportunities in selecting, supporting, and preparing teaching candidates violate federal law and compromise education excellence."

EdTrust, a nonprofit that advocates for racial and economic equity in American education, said in a statement to K-12 Dive that the Trump administration is using civil rights protections meant to "dismantle discrimination and expand opportunity" to instead "undermine pathways into teaching for people historically excluded from the profession betrays that purpose."

Scholarships that address those barriers help ensure that family wealth does not determine who can become a teacher."

The Education Department's investigations, Duncan said, "risk turning civil rights enforcement into a tool for preserving the very inequities it should confront.”

CUNY, which houses the NYC Men Teach Program being investigated by the department, said in a statement to K-12 Dive that it was "proud of its role educating the teachers of tomorrow and our programs are open to all students regardless of race, gender or national origin."

According to the city's website, the program was created around 2015 in collaboration with the federal Education Department and then-Mayor Bill de Blasio with the goal of putting an additional 1,000 men of color into the city’s teacher pipeline.

A March 2026 evaluation of the program conducted for the NYC mayor's office by Westat, which provides research services for government agencies, shows that the program contributed over 3,100 new male teachers of color to New York City Public Schools between school years 2015–16 and 2023–24.

AACTE added that it does not comment on active federal inquiries involving specific institutions or state agencies.

That decision undid race-conscious policies in college admissions, and the Education Department under Trump has used it to justify scaling back other diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives in education.

Scholarship availability, teacher pipelines and student affinity groups were among the top areas beyond college access that advocates were concerned could be impacted in the wake of the ruling.

DHS published the rule in July, arguing it was needed to protect national security and prevent visa fraud. International students would have been able to seek extensions on their visas, but the decision would have been at federal officials’ sole discretion.

The rule would have changed long-standing policy permitting foreign students to stay in the U.S. for as long as it takes to complete their academic studies.

Several higher ed groups and unions, including the Presidents’ Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration and the American Federation of Teachers, sued the agency in August over the regulations.

Saylor handed the plaintiffs an early win when he temporarily blocked the visa cap from taking effect. Writing that they were likely to succeed in their case, the judge flagged that the rule could be abused by the Trump administration.

“It is not difficult to imagine how that system might be abused to punish those whose viewpoints or research are disfavored by the officials making the decision — or, indeed, punish disfavored institutions,” Saylor said in his Sept. 14 order.

He repeated those concerns Monday, describing the student visa process DHS sought to set up as an “unreviewable, completely discretionary renewal system.”

Saylor further said DHS failed to weigh “less burdensome” options.

The DHS rule would have done more than limit student visa stays. Some international students would have been barred from changing their field of study or transferring colleges. And they could not come to the U.S. to pursue an academic degree at the same or a lower level than a degree they already possess.

For example, an international student who holds a law degree would effectively be banned from pursuing a master’s degree in the U.S., Saylor said.

“Put bluntly, DHS is seeking to prevent or discourage the very activity — providing educational opportunities for foreign students in the United States — that Congress sought to permit and encourage,” he said. “The statute simply does not grant DHS such broad and open-ended authority” to undermine the student visa program.

The judge’s decision may not put the case to bed, as the Trump administration could still appeal it. The continued uncertainty comes as new data shows a decline in international enrollment in the U.S.

In 2025-26, the number of foreign students attending U.S. colleges dropped 2.7% year over year, according to the latest Open Doors report. And preliminary data from fall 2026 found that international enrollment declined another 9% annually at surveyed institutions. Both decreases were driven by losses of new students and those pursuing graduate degrees.
