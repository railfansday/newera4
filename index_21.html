#7279. Media Briefing: Publishers’ revenue growth defies traffic declines, but tougher comparisons loom

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This Media Briefing covers the latest in media trends for Digiday+ members and is distributed over email every Thursday at 10 a.m. ET. More from the series →

This week’s Media Briefing looks at how The New York Times, People Inc., News Corp and Reuters are navigating tougher year-over-year revenue comparisons after reporting strong growth despite declining referral traffic and AI disruption.

Declining referral traffic and AI disruption aren’t stopping some of the largest publishers from growing revenue, according to recent earnings. The New York Times, News Corp, People Inc., Reuters all reported gains in the third quarter, fueled by a mix of subscriptions, advertising and content licensing.

But their latest earnings also point to a common challenge heading into the final part of the year: sustaining that momentum as last year’s revenue gains make for tougher comparisons.

Notably, AI licensing has delivered real revenue growth for People Inc. and Reuters, especially as they look to diversify revenue beyond advertising. But the initial boost from those deals is becoming harder to replicate as those agreements mature.

“The short-term revenue win that publishers got by striking LLM deals helps for a quarter or four to eight, but it’s often at the expense of lost audience and inventory,” said Matt Prohaska, CEO and principal of Prohaska Consulting.

The New York Times is also bracing for tougher year-over-year comparisons in the fourth quarter, although in its case, the challenge stems from an advertising supply increase in 2025.

Meanwhile, News Corp’s latest earnings painted a more mixed picture, revealing publishers face different levels of exposure to declining audiences and traditional advertising pressures, even within the same company. The company posted strong revenue growth in its Dow Jones business, amid declines in its News Media division.

Digiday broke down what media executives said in their earnings call this week so far (still a few more to go, with USA Today Co., BuzzFeed and Ziff Davis later this week), and what it reveals about where publishers see their businesses heading next:

What was said: “The comps get tougher as the year goes on. Q4 is our biggest quarter in ads this year and last year. I’ll remind you we grew 25%. And last year we benefited from these sort of structural supply increases in certain parts of the portfolio.” – Meredith Kopit Levien, CEO, The New York Times Company.

What it really means: The Times benefited from a significant expansion of ad inventory last year, helping drive 25% YOY growth in digital advertising in Q4 2025. That sets a high baseline for the same quarter this year, making it more difficult to sustain that pace of growth, Kopit Levien said in the earnings call on Nov. 4. Digital advertising revenue growth has already slowed, from 20.7% YOY in Q2 2026 to 12% in Q3.

Because of this, the Times expects more modest advertising growth this quarter – though Kopit Levien said in the earnings call that the company will continue to add supply going forward, though it will be “more incremental and more across the portfolio.”

What was said: “The licensing revenue, as I said and as we saw, was really impressive. We continue to have real success across Apple News, AI, and other content partnerships. In Q4, those growth rates will slow some as we lap the Meta AI deal that we announced last Q4. We still expect full-year growth to be about what we delivered in licensing in the first half which translates to something roughly about 20% Q4 growth. Again, our focus is to get back to the growth we want and expect.” – Tim Quinn, CFO, People Inc.

What it really means: People Inc. has three publicly disclosed AI licensing deals, with OpenAI, Meta and Microsoft. The Meta deal Quinn referenced during the Nov. 3 earnings call was signed last December. While People Inc.’s licensing revenue grew 49% year over year in Q3 2026, Quinn cautioned investors against expecting a similar growth rate in the fourth quarter, as the company begins to lap the initial revenue boost from its Meta deal.

But People Inc. execs remained optimistic about the AI licensing market. “They will continue to evolve and there’s a big place for our content. There’s increasingly a place for recency — meaning new content — in that marketplace,” Quinn said in the earnings call.

However, the majority of People Inc.’s licensing revenue growth came from non-AI content licensing deals. Apple News has been a “great success,” Quinn said, alongside distribution deals with platforms such as Yahoo and MSN. In total, about 15 of People Inc.’s brands are successfully generating content licensing revenue, Quinn added. Overall, People Inc.’s digital revenue grew 2% YOY in Q3 2026.

What was said: “We expect Reuters growth to be flattish year-over-year in the fourth quarter due to lapping $5 million of transactional AI licensing revenue from the prior year period, along with tougher comps at agency and in portions of the professional business.”  – Gary Bischoping, CFO, Thomson Reuters.

What it really means: Reuters also has three publicly disclosed AI content licensing deals: with Meta and Microsoft in October 2024, and a deal with Amazon in May 2026. However, a Reuters executive previously told Digiday that the company has other AI content licensing deals that it has not spoken about publicly, and cited the company’s earnings as a sign of this. The full list of Reuters’ AI licensing partners and the number of deals is not clear. But what is clear is that revenue from these deals has helped boost Reuters’ overall business.

Reuters organic revenue grew 12% in Q3 2026, thanks to about $19 million of transactional generative AI content licensing in the quarter, per Bischoping. When excluding transactional AI licensing revenue in the third quarter of both 2026 and 2025, Reuters’ revenue grew 7% YOY. While AI licensing was a meaningful contributor to Reuters’ growth this past quarter, it can create volatility in year-over-year comparisons, as its not recurring revenue for the company.

Also, stronger prior-year performance in Reuters’ agency and professional businesses will mean tougher comps in Q4 2026, Bischoping said.

What was said: “News Media continues to navigate complex market pressures, including a challenging advertising environment and the ongoing evolution of the search and social algorithm landscape. As mentioned earlier, that search landscape will change dramatically with the advent of agentic AI. Revenues for the segment declined slightly versus prior year to $540 million.” – Robert Thomson, CEO, News Corp

What it really means: Revenue at News Corp’s Dow Jones business — which includes titles like The Wall Street Journal, Barron’s and MarketWatch — grew 8% year over year, fueled by subscriptions, licensing and its professional information businesses, according to its earnings released on Nov. 4. Digital advertising revenue increased 12%, matching the growth rate reported by The New York Times in Q3. These results are an example of how large publishers are finding ways to grow revenue beyond traditional traffic-dependent business models, even as some face tougher year-over-year comparisons ahead.

However, revenue at the company’s News Media division — which includes The Sun, The Times of London and the New York Post — declined 1% YOY. The division’s ad revenue fell 2% YOY, despite gains in subscription pricing and content licensing revenue. Audience declines were also evident: The Sun’s global monthly unique users fell 26% YOY in September, while the New York Post’s digital audience declined 11%.

#7270. Agencies find AI overuse is leading to brief bloat

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This article is part of Digiday’s coverage of its Digiday Media Buying Summit. More from the series →

The advertising world rushed to embrace generative AI. But now that agencies and advertisers have come to rely on the tech to keep up, they’re finding themselves vulnerable to its toxic byproducts.

In particular, agency leaders are finding the over-use of AI tools to write advertising briefs risks undermining the productivity and the day-to-day sanity of marketing staffers.

According to M&C Saatchi’s North American CEO, Nadja Bellan-White, over-reliance on AI for brief writing is approaching “a tipping point… It’s a little bit ridiculous.” Speaking during last week’s Digiday Media Buying Summit, she estimated that 85% of the briefs sent to the agency by clients were written using AI tools.

The impact? Corporate documents that run into the hundreds of pages, replete with marketing jargon but little actual vision. “How many many times can you say ‘framework’ in one sentence? How many times can you try ‘economies of scale’? How many times can you tell me I need this to ‘tie back to commercial objectives’?”

Brief writing has become one of the most common applications for AI tech in marketing. More than half (56.4%) of marketers and agencies now use it to build out strategy, media or campaign creative briefs, according to a survey conducted by Basis Technologies between July and August.

Media agencies use agents to suggest audience segments, run analyses of competitors’ media plans or suggest search keywords to bid on. Creative shops and influencer agencies use AI to skip through reams of consumer research, brand guidelines and economic trends, or to find ways of trumping received marketing wisdom. In-house teams at high-spending brands use generative tools to spin up strategy approaches. Even publishers now use agentic tools that respond to client briefs with AI-generated insights.

Staffers will point to the hours clawed back or to the insights discovered from a broader or deeper reading of research. Behind closed doors, however, they kvetch about the cost.

“I’ve seen some of the worst briefs I’ve ever seen in my career recently, and it’s because they’re so disjointed and there’s no through-line on what… the marketing goal is,” said one agency executive, speaking under Chatham House rules at a townhall session at DMBS.

“AI is making it worse. It’s pontificating on a sentence structure that makes [a brief] 10 pages long,” said another.

Complaints about the proliferation of memos or emails, or indeed badly written campaign briefs, aren’t new. At the start of the decade, industry veterans will recall there was even a cross-industry initiative to encourage better brief writing. But just as it’s sped up the production of digital creative assets, Claude and ChatGPT tools have enabled bad writing to spread like knotweed. As another agency exec put it: “There’s a lack of critical thinking.”

Industry leaders are beginning to acknowledge those problems in public. “[AI] can be a good sparring partner,” said Bellan-White. “But at the end of the day, you need to be the one to make the decision. You understand the client’s briefs. You understand the market context. You’ve got to really think about what it is that’s recommended.”

It’s not hard to see how AI has become a crutch. Good briefs take time to craft, time that clients are loath to grant their agencies, or to use themselves, for something so far removed from the end product. Little surprise, then, that staffers turn to a solution that does the work for them like a white-collar version of the shoemaker’s elves.

In the Brothers Grimm tale, however, the shoemaker eventually retakes control of production. But advertising staffers are long-term practitioners of what academics call “cognitive offloading,” the delegation of knowledge work to a tool or machine.

A growing body of research suggests that such over-reliance on AI tools literally reduces the cognitive abilities of users. One 2025 MIT Media Lab study, for example, measured the brainwaves of students writing essays without the aid of technology, compared with those writing with the assistance of search engines or ChatGPT. The AI-assisted group showed a 55% reduction in brainwave activity.

Whatever the name, the signs are the same. A profusion of em-dashes, paragraph upon paragraph of empty, over-long sentences, the phrase “it’s not X, it’s Y.”

Cognitive offloading isn’t just an abstract (or aesthetic) problem for ad agency leaders, some of whom are recognizing that some marketing staffers have gone from using AI as an aid to relying upon it to prop them up. Bloated documents steal time from colleagues or clients, while many users’ loose attitude to the statistics AI tools conjure up demand for a closer-than usual supervision by fact-checkers. According to a GWI survey of 150 British marketers published last month, 29% admitted inaccurate or misleading insights had cost their companies financially.

“They have exported all of their thinking to AI,” said Tony Russo, vp, innovation and strategy at indie agency Mile Marker, during another DMBS session. “It’s a judgment problem.”

If there is a solution, it’s an offline one. Christina Riccitelli, chief innovation and analytics officer at media agency OpAD, said managers should intervene if they found junior staff leaning too hard on AI. “You have to reel them back in,” she said.

Bellan-White recommended more drastic action. “Turn off your laptop. Just literally shut it down. Turn off your phones,” she said. “I want you to look me in the eye and actually have a conversation. Do you know how uncomfortable that is for people today? Because if you actually have to have a conversation, that means you actually have to know what you’re talking about.”

#7240. Anthropic Strikes Its First Licensing Deal, With the AP, for Election Coverage

This story was originally published in On Background with Mark Stenberg, a free, weekly newsletter that explores the key themes shaping the media industry. You can sign up for it here.

If, by chance, you opened Claude on Election Night and asked it for an update on the latest polling data, you would have been treated to a novel sight: up-the-minute information, provided by and attributed to the Associated Press.

This new development was the result of a content and data licensing deal struck between the two organizations, which was struck just before the midterm elections, according to two people familiar with the matter.

Anthropic did not respond to a request for comment, and the AP did not provide a comment on the record. The financial specifics of the arrangement, as well as other critical details, such as its timeframe, were not disclosed by either party.

Nonetheless, the deal marks a dramatic evolution in strategy for Anthropic, as the artificial intelligence firm behind Claude had not, until Tuesday, struck a content licensing deal with any digital publisher.

In that regard, Claude differed dramatically from its primary competitor, ChatGPT, whose parent company OpenAI has negotiated more than a dozen content licensing deals with major publishers, including Axel Springer, News Corp., and The Atlantic, among others.

In fact, the reluctance by Anthropic to engage in any kind of content licensing discussion had become such a subject of intrigue within the industry that I explored the situation in August, in one of my more literally titled pieces.

At the time, digital media executives told me that the company genuinely believes that its scraping and use of publisher content is legally permissible under the concept of fair use. As such, it had not moved to enter into any such partnerships, nor had it indicated any plans to do so in the future.

So why change its tactics now, especially as the company prepares to go public in a matter of weeks?

FOR Scoops, Sharp Analysis, and Insider Context

The likely answer is that Anthropic finally admitted what publishers have long known to be true, which is that an information index without access to up-to-date data is dramatically limited in its utility.

Because Anthropic refused to strike content licensing deals with major news publishers, it lacked access to the kind of credible, timely reporting that consumers turn to in key moments like an election cycle.

Without the ability to intake data from premium publishers, whose websites block its crawlers, Anthropic could only cite what AI researcher Deana Burke calls the “unlocked web.” These low-credibility sites might serve as sufficient sources of information for low-stakes queries, but they cannot tell a user which politician won a Senate seat 10 minutes ago.

As such, Anthropic was facing down the prospect of its users finding its platform to be nearly useless as a source of real-time information on one of the most important days of the year for search queries.

Publishers had been blocking Anthropic, as well as other answer engines, to produce this very outcome. The tactic is effectively a digital embargo, with news outlets withholding their content in hopes of bringing answer engines to the bargaining table to strike licensing deals. And in this limited instance, the strategy worked.

Of course, the AP is a bit of an exceptional case: It generates the vast majority of its revenue via licensing and wire deals with other publishers, so much of its business is structured around striking these kinds of tie-ups already. The AP was also the first publisher with whom Google and OpenAI signed licensing agreements.

The AP also has the advantage of being a canonically non-partisan outlet—it positions itself as more of a service, like Getty Images for information, than a standard publisher, which often has an editorial slant one way or the other. For these reasons, the AP makes for an ideal partner for an AI firm looking to test out licensing opportunities.

This limited foray into licensing is by no means an indicator that Anthropic has irrevocably changed its licensing strategy. In all likelihood, the company will use the data from this experiment to determine whether it wants to go further down this path or not.

But the deal does make a compelling case for the value of credible, real-time information to answer engines. And if Anthropic wants to stake its claim as the dominant platform in the ecosystem, it is hard to imagine how it does so without premium publishers.

#7088. Want to improve the preschool-to-kindergarten handoff? Strengthen local connections

An early childhood expert says early education “zones” and “hubs” can connect fragmented communities, benefiting schools, children and families.

School districts often treat kindergarten as the education starting point. But they can better support young students — and strengthen family relationships — by building connections with early childhood programs and community partners before students reach kindergarten, according to David Jacobson, director of First 10, an Education Development Center initiative supportive of early childhood school-community partnerships.

Children, their families and teachers benefit when their experiences connect across settings and years. For example, pre-K and kindergarten teachers might share information about instructional practices, understand each other’s standards, and build on students' familiar routines and learning styles so there is some continuity as the students transition from pre-K, Jacobson said.

This proactive relationship building, he said, can also benefit school systems that are eager to enroll and retain more students but face declining enrollments due to falling birthrates, school choice competition, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

"Instead of waiting for children to arrive in kindergarten and then trying to persuade families to stay, districts can work with early childhood providers, libraries and family-serving organizations to build trust, to support transitions, to align practice and address barriers to attendance," Jacobson said.

Jacobson, in a paper published this fall through the Stanford Center on Early Childhood, said that a child's years from birth through elementary school "represent a uniquely important developmental window." However, early childhood programs, elementary schools, health providers and family-serving agencies often operate in isolation of each other, he said.

To build better alignments, Jacobson suggests school-early education "zones" and "hubs" that have a shared geographical area and shared responsibility for children and families in their areas.

A school-early childhood zone consists of multiple elementary schools, early childhood programs and community partners within a defined geographical area. A hub includes similar work around just one elementary school. Individual schools can be part of both a zone and hub.

High-quality programs alone “don't fully take advantage of the opportunity we have to support children and families, and so all of those organizations can be much more effective working together than working individually,” Jacobson said.

He acknowledges that this work can be challenging, especially as school systems and early childhood education programs strive to improve their own practices and outcomes. But, Jacobson explained, a zone or a hub can be a "feasible, practical, realistic structure that encourage school districts to shift their mindsets to see younger children as soon-to-be students, and to see early childhood programs as essential partners."

Building alignment between early education programs and elementary schools is the "final vertical leap" in education, he said.

Other levels of education, for instance, have vertical approaches: When a district notices its 9th graders are struggling, it may examine the 8th grade experience. And community colleges often adjust their course offerings to align with career pathways offered at feeder high schools.

But principals and district leaders may be less familiar with the broad array and operation of early education programs, including Head Start, community-based programs and family-run centers, according to the Stanford paper.

"Districts need to adopt a broader set of strategies to engage and support families early, and they need coordinated early childhood partnerships," Jacobson said. "That's what zones and hubs are: coordinated early childhood partnerships that help them build these relationships and help them build familiarity, trust and continuity."

Several communities across the country have already implemented zones and hubs with the help of First 10, which has supported 50 communities across six states with aligning school-early education approaches.

First 10, for instance, worked to develop and strengthen a zone for Maine's Sheepscot Valley Regional School Unit 12, a rural district that includes seven towns and five elementary schools. The work revealed gaps between pre-K and kindergarten instructional alignment and in communication about rising kindergarten students. As a result, the First 10 team expanded the use of transition meetings from serving only students with disabilities to serving all the pre-K children advancing to kindergarten.

In addition to those transition conversations, pre-K and kindergarten teachers visited each others' classrooms, "leading to a broader effort to incorporate Pre-K more holistically into the leadership structures and general systems of the elementary schools," the Stanford paper said.

In the hub community of Russellville, Alabama, one veteran pre-K teacher had never visited a kindergarten classroom and never had a kindergarten teacher visit her classroom. But in 2022, the pre-K and kindergarten teachers began annual reciprocal visits, which led to ongoing joint professional learning for community-based preschool, Head Start, district pre-K and kindergarten educators. "For the first time, teachers across settings analyzed standards together, identified teaching challenges, and aligned strategies across grade levels," according to the Stanford paper.
