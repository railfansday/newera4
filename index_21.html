#7337. Deloitte Digital takes the holdco operating system pitch straight to the CEO

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

CEOs have never cared much for holdcos. Enterprise solutions, on the other hand, get a meeting. Which makes Deloitte Digital’s new operating system for marketers an awkward one for the rest of the industry.

It’s sold as a license, though not the kind a CMO can approve alone. Designed eventually to stretch across sales, marketing, service and commerce, it’s the kind of deal Deloitte Digital’s U.S. CMO Mark Singer called a “more executive conversation.” That conversation happens in the C-suite, well above the marketing department where holdcos usually pitch.

The product is called Converge Front Office. It strings together AI agents, some built by Deloitte and others a client already runs elsewhere, to handle marketing and sales work from research and briefs through to campaign deployment and deal tracking. Two parts are live, a marketing operating system and a sales one, with a commerce version on the way. Singer pitched them as pieces of one front office system built around an idea the consulting firm calls “brand to cash” — every step of marketing and sales, from building a brand to closing a deal, should be traceable back to revenue.

“It’s not just about the agents, it’s about the task orchestration among them,” said Singer.

If that sounds familiar, it’s because holdcos have spent the better part of two years saying much the same. It carries more weight from a consulting firm, though, and Singer made a point of setting Deloitte’s version apart. The market is full of AI tools, he said, and “everybody’s doing a press release every 20 minutes on them.” Some exist to make an agency more efficient for its cleints. Others sell smarter work off the back of an owner’s media insights. Deloitte wants to build tools “that we hand off to our clients,” said Singer. From there, they can run them in-house or use them as a way of “rethinking their agency infrastructure,” he added.

A demo showed how that could play out. Umberto Castaldo, Deloitte Digital’s Converge marketing go-to-market lead, ran a campaign for a dummy sneaker brand. Agents passed the work along from market research and media modelling to the brief, the content, quality assurance and finally deployment into the brand’s existing mar tech. All those agents are routed into the consulting firm’s wider tech stack. Some of them draw on Deloitte’s own asset along the way. The research agent, for instance, can tap Deloitte’s own third-party data spine alongside client data.

Of those steps, content is doing a fair share of the selling, at least in the system’s current form. In the consulting firm’s view, brands have already paid for far more content than they’ve used.

Big brands have spent hundreds of millions, if not billions, on content over the past five years, Castaldo argued, and little of it has run recently. The system’s answer is to put that back catalog to work. Tagging agents can first relabel what sits in a brand’s asset library so other agents can find it. In the demo, those agents then built email and display ads for three audiences out of images and headlines the sneaker brand already had on file. Each asset is tracked from there, so the brand can see how it performs from one campaign to the next.

The sales side runs in parallel. Abe Awasthi, Deloitte Digital’s AI sales transformation leader, showed how its built around a seller’s day: the gap to quote, which deals need attention and where new demand might come from. That last job falls to an agent called Scout, which reads campaign results from the marketing side and matches them to a rep’s target customers. Administrators decide how far each agent can go, from suggesting to drafting to acting.

“Autonomy should be earned,” said Awasthi.

For now, most early clients are using the two halves separately, though Singer said some are starting to take both. Joint marketing and sales deals remain early, with one or two pitches in the past year, Singer said. As clients mature, he expects them to see the value in the “white space in between.”

The consulting firm shared one result. A client gave half of its 300 business development reps the sales operating system. Over a three-month pilot, the response rate on their outbound emails went from 17% to around 56%. Once the rollout scaled, that settled in the 30s — roughly double the starting point. Quotas for the team up 20%, said Awasthi, and the client is hiring more reps. Marketing teams are similarly seeing early gains in throughput, said Castaldo, who didn’t share any specifics.

Like everything the consulting firm has tried in advertising, Converge is designed to sit above it all, and Singer was blunt about where that leads. He expects “everything will be disrupted” across marketing services and said the shift is already underway. Four large clients he wouldn’t name are pulling their marketing into global capability centers, which he said is now possible without losing the personalization each market and language needs. Deloitte Digital already runs centralized functions like these for clients. They cover most of the marketing work, though brand advertising sits outside them. Setting up a function on that scale. Singer added, means building what he called “captive environments” for it.

In outsourcing, a “captive” is an operation a company owns and runs for itself. The brand keeps the people and the budget. The system they all work inside is another matter, and that’s what the largest marketing services groups are now chasing. Consulting firms like Deloitte have the advantage of already being in the room where that gets decided. The holdcos have been trying to get there for a while. At CES in January, Omnicom, Stagwell, Havas and WPP each unveiled agentic platforms built to sit on top of a client’s existing tools and run the work between them. WPP has since gone further, recasting itself as a single company with four divisions: WPP Media, WPP Production, WPP Enterprise Solutions and the newly-created WPP Creative. Publicis started earlier, paying $3.5 billion for Sapient in 2015 and $4.4 billion for Epsilon in 2019. Software companies are doing the same. Adobe used its conference earlier this month to introduce its end-to-end content supply chain product.

Increasingly, we see and hear from clients and agencies that the CFO is the critical piece of the new business puzzle, as marketing budgets remain flat, and CMOs are pressured to prove the value of marketing, including agency investments.”

#7332. How Bustle Digital Group is helping Pampers and Innisfree show up in AI recommendations

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Bustle Digital Group is using Profound’s AI measurement tools to help brands, including Pampers and Innisfree, show up in AI-generated recommendations.

The publisher’s GEO offering, called ECHO internally, uses Profound analytics and AI agents trained on content from BDG’s titles to generate creative briefs and guidelines. BDG’s creative team uses those materials to produce content designed to be cited by AI platforms. At least three brands have paid for the offering.

An uptick in client inquiries this year prompted BDG to develop the offering, said Graham Howes, svp of distribution and AI intelligence at BDG, which owns titles like Bustle, Nylon, Elite Daily and Scary Mommy.

BDG is the only publisher currently working with Profound to turn this process into an advertiser offering.

In one campaign, which ran between Aug 11–Sep 30, Korean beauty brand Innisfree’s visibility across 200 tracked skincare prompts grew 30%, while Bustle’s citation rank rose from #709 to #12, according to a BDG case study based on Profound data.

“By combining trusted editorial environments, answer engine optimization, and prompt-level measurement, we were able to demonstrate that AI visibility can be measured, optimized, and ultimately transformed into a meaningful competitive advantage,” said Julien Bouzitat, gm of Innisfree U.S., part of the Amore Pacific Group.

Baby care brand Pampers moved from #2 to #1 in AI visibility for fragrance-free diaper questions following a Scary Mommy explainer addressing Swaddlers’ fragrance-free switch. The explainer ran between Sep 4–17.

“There isn’t a defined playbook for how publishers should be approaching this. So we really had to come at this from a very DIY place of first understanding the space,” Howes said.

The process begins with an RFP and conversations with the brand on their AI visibility goals, Howes explained. Then comes a two- to four-week baseline research phase, where BDG and Profound analyze eight to 10 topics that the advertiser wants to increase its visibility against within an AI answer engine, and 25 to 50 prompts [on each topic] to understand the brand’s competitive standing and what sites the answer engines are currently citing.

BDG then uses its own AI agents, trained on its titles’ content, to develop an editorial strategy for the branded content. This includes potential article topics, headline structure, and tone, Howes explained. That strategy is then presented to the advertiser.

“The advertiser has no hand in the actual creative development at all. We leave that entirely up to our editors, partly because we want to make sure that we are following all of the best practices of what gets cited, and something that is very heavy-handed on the advertorial front is very unlikely to do that,” Howes said.

AI agents within Profound then design the creative briefs, which get handed off to BDG’s creative team. They write in their own voice, but follow guidelines set by the agents, such as including an FAQ section at the bottom of the article, or the type of article format, such as a listicle or an explainer.

“You’ve got to be able to connect analytics into action,” said Josh Blyskal, head of insights at Profound.

Once the content is live, it’s cross-promoted on Reddit, where BDG is running paid media. This helps build awareness among relevant communities of Reddit users, which “still matters a lot in the overall algorithm of what is being picked up and why,” Howes said. Reddit, he added, remains a significant citation source for answer engines.”

BDG tracks the performance of those posts throughout the campaign, he added.

This is the big question, especially given attribution is difficult because AI platforms offer limited insight into how people use their answers to make purchasing decisions, making it hard for publishers to attribute AI’s influence on traffic, engagement and conversions.

BDG tracks the performance of the GEO-branded content by analyzing metrics including the brand’s visibility on AI platforms, and BDG’s citation share and citation rank, mainly across ChatGPT, Gemini and Google’s AI Overviews.

Profound measures AI visibility by whether the brand appears in an AI response. Citation share measures the percentage of total citations in a given set of AI-generated answers that come from a particular publisher’s site, Blyskal said. (Citation share is typically between 0.1% and 3%, he noted.) Citation rank measures how a publisher’s content ranks against competitors based on how frequently each is cited in AI-generated answers.

“We want to really be able to prove to the best of our ability, that these two goals are interconnected when we run programs in this kind of way,” Howes said.

Using Profound, BDG identifies examples of its content appearing in AI answers, monitors which articles generate the most citations and makes real-time optimizations to improve performance, Howes said. Advertisers then receive a report detailing the results.

“Now the question is, does the influence layer – i.e., ChatGPT, where people find the conviction to decide what to buy – net cleanly into the transaction layer, which is often through Google into the website? And the answer is no, not right now,” Blyskal said.

Howes declined to share how much revenue its GEO offering was generating, or how much BDG was charging brands for this offering. It’s sold as a standalone offering, or bundled into existing custom content packages. Profound does not receive a cut of these deals.

“We see this more as an extension of [our] custom content opportunities, not a replacement to them,” Howes said. “Clients can still have those opportunities to reach our audiences on our sites, on our social channels, but also pair that with these types of opportunities to also reach similar audiences that are now choosing to engage an answer engine to have a question answered.”

Ashish Jacob, director of GEO at Go Fish Digital, said consistent measurement matters more than which analytics platforms a company uses to track visibility.

“The most valuable metrics include how often a brand appears in relevant AI responses, which sources are cited, sentiment, competitive share of voice, and how those results change over time. It’s also important to understand the prompts, AI platforms, and sampling methods behind the data, since different tools may produce different results. First-party reporting is becoming increasingly valuable as well,” Jacob said.

Profound is seeing some renewals for 2027, as well as exploring running always-on, yearlong campaigns, Howes said.

“A lot of advertisers realize that this content is very powerful, but it also has a half-life to it, and if you want to really make sure that you have an ongoing presence – especially when we think about our partners in beauty and fashion, where so much of what they’re doing is very seasonal – that they need to really have a more always-on approach to their content strategy,” Howes said.

These GEO offerings require publishers to be nimble, Howes said. Because AI answer engines are constantly changing their algorithms, GEO requires ongoing monitoring, research and content optimization, he said. Publishers must continually adapt their strategies to keep brands visible in AI-generated answers. But that’s an opportunity in itself, to act “not just as content partners” but as “a knowledge resource” to help brands understand what’s happening within AI answer engine recommendations, he added.

Howes sees GEO as an opportunity not just to improve its own visibility in AI search, but to grow its advertising business. Branded and custom content is BDG’s largest revenue source, he said. Measuring and demonstrating how GEO efforts benefit advertisers will be critical to strengthening brand partnerships and expanding these offerings as a revenue stream, Howes noted.

“I really want to better understand how this new paradigm we find ourselves in really plays a direct role in our business, not just our own editorial growth. To be able to have that kind of data and visibility, not just in our own efforts, but in our advertisers’ efforts as well, is fundamental to what we do because we need to be able to have that level of transparency to grow the kind of partnerships that we’re looking to grow into next year with these types of packages,” Howes said.

Musinsky: “We usually don’t include links, so we’re not tracking to that extent. Of course, we want to see a spike in sales. But there’s never this goal [of an] exact percentage-increase number. Bressler: “When it comes to paid usage, what is the perceived exclusivity there? Developments last week showed that Google faces another potential antitrust setback, this time in Europe, as it prepares for the next stage of its U.S. ad tech monopoly case.

On Nov. 12, an adviser to the European Union’s highest court recommended overturning a 2024 judgment that annulled an earlier €1.49 billion ($1.7 billion) fine against Google over its AdSense for Search business.

In her ⁠opinion, Advocate General Juliane Kokott argued that the EU’s General Court made legal errors when it overturned the European Commission’s original 2019 penalty. Specifically, Kokott concluded that the lower court had imposed excessive requirements on regulators when assessing whether Google’s contractual restrictions harmed competition.

The case concerns agreements Google used with publishers that displayed search advertisements through its AdSense for Search service; i.e., it involves a different business unit than the one under scrutiny in its most recent U.S. trial.

Per the European proceedings’ plaintiffs, between 2006 and 2016, Google imposed contractual restrictions that limited those websites’ ability to display advertisements from rival search advertising providers.

The European Commission identified three types of restrictions: exclusivity clauses preventing publishers from using competing services, placement clauses restricting where rival advertisements could appear, and provisions requiring Google’s prior approval before changes to advertising displays.

The Commission concluded that these practices constituted a single, continuous infringement of European competition law. Google challenged the decision, and in September 2024, the General Court annulled the fine, finding that regulators had failed to examine relevant circumstances adequately, particularly the duration of Google’s contractual arrangements.

Kokott’s opinion, published Nov. 12, argued that the General Court had placed excessive emphasis on the duration of individual agreements rather than considering their collective effect on competition.

In the accompanying press release, the court explained that regulators could assess contracts collectively to determine whether they could produce exclusionary effects. Kokott recommended setting aside the 2024 judgment and returning the case to the General Court for reconsideration.

Importantly, the recommendation does not automatically reinstate the original fine. The opinion is nonbinding, and the Court of Justice has yet to issue its judgment. Moreover, Kokott acknowledged that the penalty should ultimately be reduced because certain findings concerning Google’s conduct in 2016 are no longer being contested.

Nevertheless, the opinion creates the prospect of another prolonged legal battle over Google’s advertising practices, as the development comes two months after Google avoided a forced breakup of its advertising technology business in the U.S.

In September, federal judge Leonie Brinkema rejected the Justice Department’s proposed divestiture of Google’s AdX exchange and publisher ad server, DoubleClick for Publishers.

Instead, the court accepted most of the government’s proposed behavioral remedies, subject to modifications. The decision shifted the focus from Google’s ownership of its advertising infrastructure to the restrictions governing how that infrastructure operates.

The remedies are intended to address Google’s ability to favor its own advertising products, including through changes affecting interoperability, auction transparency and access for competing ad tech companies. However, the precise obligations and their enforcement remain central questions as the parties work through the final judgment.

Google has indicated that it intends to appeal the underlying liability decision, potentially extending the litigation beyond implementation of the behavioral remedies.

The European and American cases concern different markets and legal questions, but both illustrate the difficulty regulators face in challenging Google’s advertising dominance.

In Europe, the courts are still examining conduct that ended a decade ago. In the U.S., the argument has moved from whether Google violated competition law to whether the remedies will materially change market behavior.

#7256. A Crash Course in Marketing to Machines

Some years ago, one of us, Jim Lecinski, wrote a manifesto called the “Zero Moment of Truth,” or “ZMOT,” which argued that before the traditional point of purchase arrives — before a customer reaches the shampoo aisle shelf or the car dealer’s lot -— that customer goes looking for evidence to help them decide what products are the most credible and therefore what to buy.

Many marketers still begin the marketing process from the company out: Who are we? What do we want to sell? What message can we create to persuade people to buy? In effect, they are working their way from the product to the customer. Great marketers, however, work the other way around.

They begin by asking what job the customer is trying to get done, how that customer moves through the purchase journey, what questions arise along the way, and which sources shape confidence and choice. Then they align their go-to-market accordingly.

Digital marketing has made the customer decision process more complex. Rather than relying on a single message or source, customers consult multiple sources before deciding what to buy. They gather evidence. They search, compare, read what other customers say, watch experts and creators, and increasingly ask AI assistants for answers and recommendations.

The original three sources – search engines, social conversations, and ratings and reviews – remain important. But two others now deserve a place in the ZMOT framework: content creators and AI assistants. Both influence how customers evaluate choices, form preferences, and make purchase decisions.

And these sources also interact. Search results show online community discussions, reviews, and creator videos. Creator content influences people and AI-generated answers. AI assistants draw from information created across the web, synthesize it, and increasingly build or narrow the consideration set on the customer’s behalf.

The Zero Moment of Truth provides the clearest framework for understanding this change.

The concept of the Zero Moment remains as relevant today as when it was introduced: The Zero Moment of Truth is the critical pre-purchase period when customers seek information, compare options, reduce uncertainty, and form preferences. A stimulus creates awareness or activates a need. Before the traditional point of purchase arrives, the customer goes looking for evidence that will help them decide what to believe and what to choose.

What continues to evolve are the sources customers use to gather the information that shapes this decision-making zero moment.

Content Creators help customers understand categories, interpret differences, see products in context, and decide with confidence. AI assistants increasingly answer questions, compare alternatives, form shortlists, analyze options, and recommend specific brands.

The expansion of avenues from which customers acquire knowledge, requires marketers to rethink their approach. A customer considering a new suitcase may move among search, online community discussions, ratings & reviews, content creator videos, and an AI chatbot all during a single decision journey. These five sources therefore form an interconnected portfolio rather than separate or sequential channels.

Therefore, winning the Zero Moment still requires clear and persuasive brand messages across these various avenues. The marketer’s mandate now extends beyond the messages the brand creates and distributes. The expanded Zero Moment requires marketers to move from managing brand messages alone to managing the broader body of decision evidence that customers and AI systems use to understand, evaluate, and choose brands. These roles overlap, which is precisely why marketers must manage them together. Creator videos appear in search results. Online community conversations influence both human perceptions and AI-generated answers. Reviews shape search visibility, customer trust, and machine-generated comparisons. Brand content can support every source when it is useful, clear, current, and corroborated by evidence outside the brand.

Notice that AI assistants differ from the other four sources because they play two roles. They are a fifth source customers consult directly, and they synthesize information drawn from the other four. This becomes even more important as AI is directly embedded across categories, including social, ecommerce and productivity.

An AI-generated answer may incorporate search results, brand websites, publishers, social conversations, reviews, creator content, and other third-party evidence. AI increasingly assembles and interprets the consideration set on the customer’s behalf. It can identify contradictions, surface recurring criticisms, compare claims, and omit brands that lack sufficient credible evidence from the answers or recommendations it provides. In fact, Google recently introduced new ad formats including Conversational Discovery Ads, Direct Offers, and Highlighted Ads, which answer questions, supply analysis and offer suggestions right inside the ad creative.

Strong search rankings no longer guarantee visibility in AI-generated answers. A brand may perform well in search engines but be missing from the recommendations from AI assistants or overviews. It may have a polished website while customers, creators, and reviewers tell a different story elsewhere. It may appear across all five sources with inconsistent product information, claims, pricing, or positioning.

Each source can strengthen or weaken the evidence found in the others.

Marketers still need persuasive messages, which are an essential part of the evidence customers encounter.

Marketers have traditionally focused on the messages they create and distribute. Today, they also must understand and influence the evidence customers encounter elsewhere, including reviews and creator content and AI-generated recommendations.

Decision evidence is the accumulated body of information, experiences, claims, reviews, demonstrations, conversations, and third-party validation that shapes what customers and AI systems understand and believe about a brand.

This evidence includes useful and authoritative brand content, accurate product information, strong customer experiences, current ratings and reviews, credible third-party coverage, informed creator content, and consistent facts that both humans and machines can understand. YouTube becomes a valuable source of authority, based on the way large language models prioritize multimodal content and direct video citations. A recent BrightEdge report indicates that a brand’s YouTube presence represents a unique path to AI optimization. As the dominant video platform in AI search, YouTube is already capturing nearly 30% of AI citations in Google’s AI Overviews (source).

While brands cannot fully control every source they can influence the quality, availability, credibility, and coherence of the evidence customers encounter. The goal is to ensure that the evidence available across all five sources supports the position the brand wants to earn in the customer’s mind.

#7249. Amazon’s New Christmas Ad Is a Bet Against Itself

Editor’s note: Brave Bison, which acquired Mark Ritson’s MiniMBA in 2025, owns about 28% of System1 and sought to acquire the rest this year.

Joy is stuck at the airport on Christmas Eve, the snow coming down hard, and every flight cancelled. So she picks up her bag of presents and sets off for home the long way: a bus, a lift from some donkey farmers, a night in a boat cabin on a heaving sea, a taxi driven by a giant. At every stop, she thanks a stranger with one of the Amazon parcels meant for her family.

This is “Joy Delivered”, Amazon’s new holiday ad, which launched earlier this month and runs like a proper movie. A new version of the Beatles’ “Golden Slumbers” does a lot of the emotional heavy lifting and the hug at the end will get you.

There are two problems with the ad, however. One is peripheral. One central.

The peripheral one first. Amazon filmed its white Christmas in New Zealand, in and around Dunedin, to be specific. But it’s summer down there, and it will be even warmer by Christmas, when most of the country will be at the beach in a pair of shorts.

The central problem is bigger. Amazon already has a great Christmas ad: “Joy Ride.”

Three old ladies sitting on a snowy hill watch the local kids go sledding. One of them orders some seat cushions from Amazon. The box turns up and the next thing you know they are flying down the hill and, for a few seconds, they are girls again. There are no words. Just an instrumental of “In My Life.”

“Joy Ride” first ran in 2023 and got the maximum 5.8 stars from System1. Basically perfection.

Then Amazon did something very unusual for a big marketer. Last year, instead of making a new ad, it ran the old one again. It scored 5.7, again putting it in the rare company of less than 1% of all ads made all year.

None of this should surprise anyone. Coca-Cola first ran “Holidays Are Coming” in 1995. The trucks have rolled out every Christmas since, and System1 says the ad always scores five stars in its testing. When System1 looked at more than 50,000 ads, it found that scores barely move as ads get older, and that some of the very best actually improve as people get to know them.

Ads don’t wear out, at least not for consumers.

Amazon’s problem is that its advertising decisions are made by marketers — people who like to make new, shiny stuff. Jo Shoesmith, Amazon’s global chief brand officer, told ADWEEK that people want nostalgia but also “something they haven’t seen before.”

The average brand building campaign reaches its maximum impact about two to three years into execution. Seasonal stuff can often, as Coke has proved, live on for decades. But the average marketer is tired of their ad, and wants to make something new.

It’s the same story with slogans, with logos, with distinctive brand assets. Marketers forget they are not the consumer. They look at their ads for at least 9,000 hours before they release it into the wild, and are over it before most of the target market has noticed it.

There is an intermediate position. Amazon could pull a Coca-Cola: bring out their old ad every year but also make a crappy, inferior new execution alongside it. But why create a bat to beat yourself with? Better to remove the outstanding, already made, already free, already proven, already much-loved Christmas ad completely, and then run something less good, with lots more risk and cost attached. Makes perfect sense.

We read all the time that marketers need more support from their CEO, more budget, more seats in the boardroom.

But what they mostly need is patience. It takes years, not months, for a good campaign to work. Amazon knew that last Christmas, but it has already forgotten it.
