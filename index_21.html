#7277. WTF is scene-level targeting for CTV?

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This WTF guide, sponsored by Wurl, explores how CTV advertisers are driving positive attention and improved performance across the entire funnel by aligning creative with relevant onscreen context through scene-level targeting.

CTV has changed how advertisers reach audiences through the biggest screen in the home.

Research shows that ad-supported streaming TV services are experiencing a surge in popularity. Despite this boon in both viewership and content supply, CTV advertisers still struggle with an opaque ecosystem that limits the ability to control where their ads appear. On top of this, ad fill rates aren’t quite keeping pace, according to Wurl — signaling a significant opportunity for advertisers and content owners alike. This dynamic is ripe for change, with new tools and technologies now available that promise to help advertisers reach engaged viewers more effectively and efficiently at scale.

Among the new tools is scene-level targeting, a form of contextual advertising that analyzes content on screen just before the ad break to determine which ad should appear next. With scene-level targeting on CTV, advertisers can pinpoint relevant moments for greater impact.

“Effective communication often relies on the context in which it is delivered; the same rules apply in advertising. Scene-level targeting introduces a whole new level of precision to TV advertising,” said Ria Madrid, vice president of global advertising at Wurl. “Marketers can now deliver their brand messaging adjacent to relevant content and in alignment with viewers’ mindsets and level of attentiveness. It creates a smarter, more prescriptive connection between brands and consumers.”

In this WTF explainer guide, Digiday and Wurl examine what scene-level targeting is, how it differs from other targeting strategies and how brands are implementing it.

Scene-level targeting considers what is happening on screen in a show or movie before an ad break — not just the app, channel, genre or audience. By focusing on what is being watched and when — such as the emotional tone or the brand safety of a specific scene — scene-level targeting places ads in compatible moments that feel relevant to viewers.

Advertisers commonly rely solely on audience targeting, which is based on household-level IDs, or on broad, traditional genre targeting that does not account for nuanced moments. But with scene-level signals, brands can better align their creative with the context of a given scene, even if they continue to apply traditional strategies like audience targeting.

Broad targeting treats every scene as equal, when really they aren’t.

“Content isn’t static — tone, emotion and subject matter often shift from one scene to the next. With scene-level analysis and real-time categorization, advertisers now have the ability to respond to that nuance and target accordingly. Scene-level signals introduce precision where it matters most — at the moment of exposure — and bring greater precision to how CTV campaigns are planned and evaluated.” Madrid said.

While more than half of marketers invest in TV and streaming, per Digiday Research, challenges remain in the CTV ad ecosystem. Though CTV investment continues to grow, advertisers still lack consistent, granular signals about content environments. And with the high impact CTV brings — delivering ads on the biggest screen in the home, often in non-skippable environments — context matters even more.

Many moments and scenes within a single TV program can be wildly different. As the show’s narrative progresses, the genre and emotional tone may also change, but program- or channel-level targeting doesn’t capture that nuance.

For instance, the popular series “Succession” was rooted in serious drama, but still delivered moments of sharp, often unexpected humor where an emotionally intense ad would feel out of place. Scene-level targeting aims to avoid that dissonant experience by using a more granular taxonomy to deliver more relevant, timely ads that enhance outcomes for both brands and audiences.

This additional layer of insight supports smarter creative alignment and stronger brand outcomes. According to a joint Wurl and TVision study, there is a 2.4x increase in attention when streaming TV ads match the content’s emotion.

Beyond helping to pinpoint the genre and emotional context, scene-level targeting is also well-suited for addressing brand safety concerns.

Typical channel- and show-level metadata doesn’t catch sensitive, inappropriate or generally misaligned content at the scene level. Because of this limited information, some advertisers entirely avoid potentially problematic content types, like news, to circumvent brand safety issues.

However, Wurl data shows that news accounts for an average of 8.6% of viewing hours on free ad-supported streaming TV, the majority of which is watched by a small but highly engaged segment of viewers — meaning that advertisers that exclude news content from their campaigns are missing out on opportunities to reach a sizable, engaged audience. Furthermore, Wurl’s 2026 CTV trends report found that more than a third of news scenes are fully brand safe. With scene-level targeting, ads can be placed alongside a feel-good news story, rather than a polarizing one, ensuring brand safety while still maximizing reach and message impact.

“Currently, many advertisers are approaching brand safety with overly broad-stroke approaches. Entire genres, programs and sometimes even publishers are excluded in an effort to avoid potential risk,” Madrid said. “By moving beyond broad proxies, advertisers can deliver their ads more intentionally, without sacrificing reach or risk to their brand. For publishers, this unlocks an optimization tool — shifting brand safety from a constraint into a monetization opportunity.”

For instance, Wurl uses generative AI to analyze video content, evaluating the scene immediately before an ad pod. This proprietary technology scores content across various attributes, including emotions, genres and IAB-defined brand safety categories.

“Our technology analyzes a combination of visual cues, audio signals and dialogue to understand imagery, tone, sentiment and contextual themes as they unfold within a scene,” Madrid said. “By evaluating the combination of these media aesthetics, we advance beyond one-dimensional metadata signals and produce dynamic segment outputs that classify the nuance of what’s happening in the moment. For advertisers, that means stronger alignment and attention. For publishers, it creates high-value, contextually enriched inventory.”

This analysis happens in real time, enabling dynamic and precise ad delivery at scale. As the content is identified and classified, these scores are appended to the content metadata before it is sent to demand-side platforms, where segments can then be targeted. Once these signals are passed into the bid stream, advertisers can choose to target relevant contextual moments that align with their brand.

“Brands have flexibility in how they activate contextual segments for their campaign targeting. They can select on their own from our expansive taxonomy, or more commonly, from our curated packages, where we use a proven combination of segment categories that perform well for specific advertiser verticals: food and beverage, travel, app installs, etc.,” Madrid said. “Regardless of whether advertisers run programmatic or managed service campaigns, they can choose from several options for how to best align their brand with content and the consumer’s mindset.”

Emotion is a powerful signal, and emotional tone changes from scene to scene. Because each scene carries a unique mood and message, real-time analysis ensures ads match the current moment and are aligned with viewers’ emotional state.

Emotions also play a significant role in decision-making and ad success. According to research from Integral Ad Science, ads aligned with the consumer’s emotional state or surrounding content are up to 40% more memorable. Other studies show that more than 90% of purchasing decisions are driven by emotional responses.

In practice, emotions are more complex than just happy or sad — and savvy advertisers use these subtleties to their advantage across the funnel.

Consider this lower-funnel example, in which global agency PMG leveraged Wurl’s scene-level targeting to run emotionally targeted CTV ads on behalf of a quick-service restaurant (QSR) client. Through scene-level targeting, the ads were matched to content that evoked feelings of anticipation and joy to drive measurable sales results, validated by independent measurement partner InMarket.

“By optimizing delivery around those high-performing emotional signals, such as anticipation, the campaign drove a 40% lift in incremental restaurant visits and a 48% lift in incremental sales, making emotion-led placements the top-performing CTV tactic in the flight,” Madrid said. “It’s a clear example of how moment-level alignment increases attention — and attention translates directly into measurable business impact.”

As Madrid explained, driving outcomes and measuring performance in CTV has been a top-of-mind challenge for advertisers. Third-party validation shows that when ads align with what’s on screen, advertisers see benefits such as improved brand recall, engagement and purchase intent.

“Through working with various measurement partners like Kantar, Upwave, EDO and InMarket, what we’re really seeing is that targeting at the scene level improves effectiveness holistically — not just at one stage, but throughout the entire customer journey,” Madrid said. “At the top, we see lifts in brand awareness and favorability. Mid-funnel, stronger motivation and purchase intent. And at the lower funnel, measurable outcomes like incremental store visits and sales.

“When creative aligns with mindset and moment, the ad feels less disruptive and more relevant — and that drives performance across the funnel because those moments directly influence attention and impact.”

Scene-level targeting does not need to be used in a vacuum. It can be easily added to advertisers’ existing strategies, helping them reach their target audience in a CTV context that is relevant to their campaign and creative.

For instance, in the QSR campaign mentioned previously, Wurl ran a controlled CTV test designed to evaluate two targeting approaches: audience and regional targeting alone and audience and regional targeting layered with scene-level targeting. The agency tapped an independent measurement firm to validate results and provide a clear view into the incremental impact of each strategy.

The campaign analysis revealed that adding scene-level targeting nearly doubled the performance of audience and regional targeting alone. By combining geographic, audience and scene-level targeting, the campaign effectively delivered both relevance and reach.

“When done correctly, combining CTV targeting strategies is where precision really starts to take shape,” Madrid said. “Audience and demographic targeting have long been the norm, ensuring the right viewers are being reached. Scene-level contextual targeting ensures they are being reached during the right moments of their viewing session — in this case, following content that is both brand-suitable and safe. When those layers work together, advertisers don’t have to choose between scale and suitability. It’s a ‘yes, and.’”

To fully unlock the potential of CTV advertising, marketers need to adapt their strategies to the streaming landscape. The good news: adding scene-level targeting to existing CTV toolkits doesn’t have to be a heavy lift.

The easiest way to begin is by layering scene-level signals on top of existing audience and demographic targeting — enhancing relevance without sacrificing scale. This allows marketers to maintain reach while improving the context and impact of their messaging.

When evaluating partners, it’s important to prioritize solutions that deliver both precision and scale. Look for providers that offer high-quality, real-time signals and can seamlessly integrate into existing programmatic workflows — enabling activation without added operational complexity. Signal accuracy and ease of activation are critical, as these inputs ultimately determine campaign performance.

As the ecosystem evolves, advertisers will have greater flexibility in how they access and activate contextual data. New approaches are making it easier to pair premium streaming inventory with differentiated contextual solutions, giving marketers more choice without disrupting existing buying workflows. For instance, Wurl’s Content Intelligence Platform reflects this shift, enabling advertisers to access Wurl’s own scene-level targeting, as well as helping partners like KERV, Peer39 and IRIS.TV to scale their own precise and unique CTV targeting solutions. For marketers, that means greater choice, more relevant contextual signals and the flexibility to activate the solutions that best align with their campaign objectives.

This flexibility will become even more important as programmatic buying becomes more automated and AI-driven. In an environment driven by agentic workflows and automated decision-making, campaign performance will depend on the quality of the underlying contextual data.

“We hear about agentic workflows and AI-driven solutions being championed across the industry — how it will improve efficiency, efficacy, creative and ultimately provide cost savings across organizations of all sizes. All of these promises translate into improved performance,” Madrid said. “As more investment flows into these agentic environments, we cannot ignore the fact that it’s the underlying data sources that underpin the success of automation and curation. Scene-level segments and real-time categorization support all of the above.”

The U.S. Department of Education said Thursday that it made “no substantive changes” to its August draft regulations.

The Education Department said it made “no substantive changes” to the draft rule it released in August and largely retained the language agreed upon with a committee of higher education representatives in the spring. The outcome of that process, known as negotiated rulemaking, bound the agency to much of the approved text.

The final rule, set to publish Friday in the Federal Register, will ensure accrediting agencies “prioritize diversity of thought on campuses,” the agency said.

When evaluating a college, accreditors must consider if the institution has academic freedom and freedom of inquiry protections, as well as policies “designed to support, promote, and appropriately prioritize intellectual diversity and the free exchange of ideas amongst faculty,” per the final rule.

But the Education Department asserted, “contrary to the hyperbole exercised by several commenters,” the new regulations do not force accreditors to adopt specific standards in exchange for federal recognition.

An accreditor that doesn’t adopt a formal policy on academic freedom or intellectual diversity will not be found out of federal compliance solely on either basis, according to the final regulations.

Under Secretary of Education Nicholas Kent said Thursday that the final rule creates “a more competitive and transparent accreditation system” and increases accountability for colleges.

“For too long, accreditors have effectively served as a cartel to protect their interests, rather than the educational value that institutions of higher education are expected to deliver for students,” he said in a statement.

The Education Department accepted public comment on the rule for a month, the minimum window required by law. During that period, which the agency said “afforded the public a substantial opportunity for meaningful input,” it received some 7,000 responses.

Among them were requests by commenters to phase in the new accreditation regulations. The department rejected this request, saying “the eight months between November 2026 and July 2027 is sufficient time to update standards, policies, and procedures to achieve compliance.”

On Thursday, the Council for Higher Education Accreditation expressed disappointment with the final rule.

"Many of the central concerns raised by CHEA and the higher education community remain largely unresolved," Nasser Paydar, the organization's president, said in a statement. Under a new proposal, the beleaguered public flagship would cut 18 academic programs and over 100 jobs, among other changes.

The University of Oregon is considering a proposal that would eliminate over 100 staff positions — just a third of which are vacant — and nearly 20 academic programs amid its efforts to resolve a projected structural deficit of $65 million.

The university is taking campus comment on the plan, which is still in its recommendation phase, and will hold a series of listening sessions. Scholz will also meet with UO’s vice presidents in November before he makes a final decision on the proposal in early December.

Under the budget plan, UO would eliminate 18 graduate-level academic programs, most of which are in liberal arts fields. It noted that each program on the chopping block has had few graduates in recent years.

Affected programs would stop enrolling new students effective fall 2027 and conclude once their current cohorts finish their degrees. The average master’s program would conclude its teach-out by spring 2028, and closing Ph.D. programs would typically shutter by spring 2032.

Most doctoral students receive research appointments that are externally funded. But some graduate students are supported through teaching appointments paid by UO.

“GE appointments represent a substantial recurring expenditure,” the proposal said. It seeks to bring the cost of graduate funding “into alignment with available resources” and preserve appointments for departments “where graduate students contribute most significantly to faculty research and scholarship.”

That means focusing GE funding on doctoral training and research, where ongoing support “is central to the degree model,” it said.

Those priorities mean a significant shift away from supporting graduate students in the university’s College of Arts and Sciences. Under the plan, the college would reduce the number of graduate students it supports by up to 40%.

Other programs are restricting their admissions to avoid doling out as much financial support for graduate students.

If approved, the university would pause recruitment for four Ph.D. programs — Architecture; Landscape Architecture; History of Art and Architecture; and Planning, Public Policy and Management — while it conducts cost-benefit analyses and aligns GE-supported admissions “with college priorities and budget.”

Similarly, UO would accept at most five students a year into its art MFA program to limit costly admissions.

The university plan said it will honor the GE support to which it has already committed.

UO is also eyeing several organizational consolidations, including combining its Classics and Religious Studies departments. The merger would “eliminate duplicate leadership responsibilities and reduce faculty service and administrative demands,” the plan said.

The university would similarly combine its earth sciences and geography departments under the proposal. And it would merge its design college with its school of music and dance.

“These dynamics are not only affecting the University of Oregon,” Scholz said. “They represent a changing reality across higher education and are likely to be the new market environment to which we must adjust.”

#7084. How a university partnership is bringing public procurement into the talent pipeline

Colleges and universities face an ongoing challenge: helping students connect what they learn in the classroom with career opportunities that they may not know exist. Public procurement may not be an obvious career choice for students studying business, supply chain management, or public administration, but introducing students to the profession can start long before graduation, including through their coursework, workforce experiences and procurement professionals they meet along the way.

NASPO is working with Arizona State University (ASU) to create more of those opportunities. A key part of the partnership is the naming of the NASPO Department of Supply Chain Management at ASU, the first academic department of its kind that explicitly includes public procurement in its curriculum. The department was recently ranked the #1 undergraduate supply chain management program in the nation by U.S. News & World Report. Supported by a landmark $35 million gift from NASPO, the department is creating a dedicated hub for education, research and innovation in public procurement. The work will support students preparing to enter the field while giving current public procurement professionals opportunities to strengthen their skills and contribute to initiatives that advance public procurement nationwide.

A partnership with a university means understanding more than its academic programs. For NASPO, it means starting with understanding a student’s perspective and world. What are they learning? What questions are faculty discussing with their classes? Where could exploring public procurement careers add a new perspective to the conversation? To keep that engagement consistent, NASPO has created a liaison position at Arizona State University.

As Program Operations Coordinator, Mia Carlton works directly with the university community, giving NASPO a closer look at the ideas, interests and priorities taking shape on campus. This role helps NASPO find more natural ways to bring public procurement into students’ academic experiences through coursework, research projects and campus events, while connecting state members to speaking, mentoring and collaboration opportunities on campus and within their own states. This continued engagement has helped NASPO and ASU identify opportunities to learn from one another and strengthen the relationship over time.

“Most juniors and seniors only know what procurement is through the lens of private companies without giving thought toward public entities,” Carlton said. “In just two months of on-campus speaking engagements and career fairs, I have students reaching out regularly about learning more and getting involved in the public sector. They are deeply drawn to the impact one can make in their community.”

Introducing students to public procurement begins with helping them understand what the profession looks like and where it can lead. NASPO Ignite is NASPO’s talent development initiative that supports creating opportunities to make those introductions, both inside and outside the classroom at every school level. NASPO Ignite also reaches high school students through a partnership with DECA. The collaboration introduces younger students to the procurement profession through speaking opportunities, judging and the DECA NASPO Procurement Challenge, which allows students to step into the shoes of a procurement professional in their community.

Beyond coursework, college students can gain hands-on experience through NASPO’s Internship Connect Program, which connects state procurement offices with collegiate talent. Through the program, NASPO recruits students for participating state offices and provides grant funding to support their internships. During the summer program, students have the opportunity to work in selected participating state and territorial procurement offices, with the program open to all 50 states and U.S. territories. Through these experiences, students gain firsthand insight into public procurement and the role it plays in serving communities.

NASPO also offers an Academic Scholarship to support students who show an interest in public procurement or public service. The scholarship program helps reduce some of the financial barriers students may face while pursuing their education. By providing support as students study and complete their degrees, the program gives recipients more opportunity to focus on their coursework and explore their professional interests, while encouraging them to continue developing that interest throughout their academic journey.

The NASPO-ASU partnership demonstrates how public procurement can be integrated into the higher education experience through relationships with faculty, research opportunities and direct engagement with the profession.

“Having NASPO connected directly to campus allows us to bring public procurement into the conversations already happening in classrooms and across the university,” said Adegoke Oke, Professor of Supply Chain Management and NASPO Endowed Department Chair, NASPO Department of Supply Chain Management, W.P. Carey School of Business at Arizona State University. “We can connect what students are learning with the challenges procurement professionals are navigating every day, while creating opportunities for faculty, students and state procurement offices to learn from one another. That exchange of knowledge and experience is what makes this partnership so valuable.”

Building on this approach, NASPO’s newly expanded Academic Talent Expansion Program will empower more state procurement offices to partner with colleges and universities in their communities. Through internships, scholarships, career fairs, guest speakers, research collaborations and other opportunities, states can introduce students to public procurement while building relationships with institutions that can help develop the future workforce.

Together with NASPO Ignite and its other talent development initiatives, these efforts reflect NASPO’s continued commitment to helping students discover public procurement and giving state procurement offices more ways to connect with emerging talent.
