#7382. Here are the questions on advertisers’ minds after the DoorDash-Instacart deal

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

It’s been almost a week since DoorDash officially announced its plans to acquire Instacart — a deal valued at approximately $17 billion.

DoorDash’s takeover of Instacart isn’t expected to wrap until the end of 2027 at the earliest or the first half of 2028 at the latest, but media buyers are already questioning what this could mean for the retail media landscape.

“In the short term, I don’t expect this to meaningfully disrupt the retail media landscape. But longer term, the potential is significant,” said Nik Hengel, vp of analytics at New Engen digital marketing agency.

The deal still has to be approved by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) and not flagged as an antitrust violation. In the meantime, however, media buyers are more concerned with costs, consolidation and capabilities.

Here are five questions media buyers and industry analysts have about the pending acquisition:

Advertisers are salivating at what these two networks could offer them — from DoorDash’s delivery network of 993 million total orders, per DoorDash’s Q3 earnings and Instacart’s grocery and fulfillment technology (as well as its 91.4 million orders, per Instacart’s Q3 earnings).

The joint venture could also mean a single ecosystem with closed-loop data reporting and a scaled ads platform.

“But bigger platforms don’t automatically mean simplicity,” said Bill Wise, CEO and co-founder of Mediaocean, an adtech company. “Brands still have to manage their investments across multiple ecosystems, each with its own data, rules, and measurement.”

However, the two already have separate ad businesses — DoorDash Ads and Instacart Ads — with their own tech offerings. The real test will be how the two delivery platforms will integrate the advertising components of their business without upending how advertisers buy media.

Scott Paternoster, CEO of performance-marketing agency Chief Media, has been buying ads through Instacart for the last two years and doesn’t expect the media buying process to immediately change.

“I just see it as expanding the footprint of reach and targeting from home delivery to prepared meal home delivery to grocery delivery, and understanding the data and the connections across those two types of audiences and retargeting across them,” Paternoster said.

U.S. retail media spend is expected to reach nearly $70 billion by the end of this year. Between DoorDash and Instacart’s audience size and potential for closed loop reporting, the case could be made for premium pricing.

“There’s so much data within this that is actually really valuable for advertisers because it spans like every retailer,” said Briana Finelli, vp of brand and commerce media at Goodway Group.

What CPMs could will be is unknown, but advertisers are cautious to earmark ad dollars in advance given new ad programs can take a bit of time to get off the ground. Case in point: OpenAI’s ads in ChatGPT.

Walmart and Amazon are expected to hoover up more than 89% of the incremental ad dollars spent in retail media this year, per eMarketer. The deal between DoorDash and Instacart could help the two delivery companies cut into that spend.

Both DoorDash and Instacart are billion-dollar businesses independently, per reporting from AdExchanger. Walmart’s global ad business pulled in nearly $6.4 billion last year, per its earnings. Those figures, however, pale in comparison to the $21.3 billion Amazon made in Q4 of 2025 alone.

“Amazon and Walmart already have a substantial head start, and the longer it takes to capitalize on that combined data, the wider that gap could become,” said Hengel.

For smaller RMNs, they’ll have more to prove to capture more ad spend.

“Either the ability to prove value in the media itself through transaction level performance data, or the ability to unify and connect their audiences to a larger scale ecosystem, is going to be more important in the coming years,” said Greg Carlucci, senior director analyst at Gartner.

Media buyers and industry analysts seem confident that this deal represents the first major step in retail media consolidation. Increasingly, advertisers are scrutinizing their media spend. For retailers to compete for ad dollars, consolidation can help build up scale, technology and inventory offerings.

“This acquisition is an example point of the importance of scale and measurement that is in commerce media right now,” Carlucci said.

The deal puts an additional spotlight on how RMNs will win over ad dollars. In this instance, the reach will be across “grocery, restaurants, and retail,” said Lidiane Jones, CEO of Integral Ad Science (IAS).

Measurement and incrementality is something the retail media segments have long since grappled with. The acquisition may mean advertisers have access to more data, but access doesn’t guarantee better measurement.

Notably, DoorDash in September received the Alliance for Audited Media’s (AAM) Incrementality Certification, which is an independent auditing program that verifies whether a platform’s incrementality measurement approach aligns with industry standards. Meanwhile, Instacart has partnered with the Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB) to study measurement in addition to its Media Rating Council accreditation that goes back to 2024.

Still, the industry has yet to settle on a standard definition of incrementality, making it hard to prove even with more data. Meaning, independent verification to ensure retailers aren’t grading their own homework still matters.

“As the category grows, it will be important to pair that scale with consistent, independent evidence of media quality and business outcomes,” said Jones. “The platforms that make that connection will be better positioned to earn sustained investment.”

#7379. Good ads have a new name, ‘entertainment-first’ and SharkNinja is using it for its holiday campaign

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

Entertainment-first has spent the year working its way into most conversations about marketing, including a fair few of Digiday’s. SharkNinja’s holiday campaign is the latest example.

Last year’s spot had Kevin Hart moving in next door to David Beckham. This year he’s house-sitting for him and he invites Tom Brady and Aryna Sabalenka over. Things get broken an each star then posted a video denying it was them.

The evidence started leaking on social media over the weekend as grainy security camera footage. In the the full spot, out today, Sabalenka drinks straight from the spout of a Ninja slushie machine. Brady wonders about one of the company’s skincare face masks and treads on a present, while Hart cracks a picture frame with a Shark vacuum. Beckham watches the whole thing on his phone live from his own security cameras.

Asked what makes that entertainment-first, Laura Dyer, senior vp of global brand and partnerships at SharkNinja, said it comes down to whether people want to share the ad and talk about it. Anything else is “educational”. That’s a test most memorable advertising has always passed. The fact that marketers now feel obliged to give it a name says something about where the industry has ended up. After years of ads built to satisfy a performance dashboard, making something people would choose to watch has become a position a brand has to declare.

SharkNinja has run this play before. When Brady joined last October, a teaser had the internet expecting a second Netflix roast before the reveal: he was roasting a chicken in a Ninja air fryer. The holiday campaign runs the same trick with a bigger cast and a Christmas tree.

Leaked “CCTV” clips and denial videos from the stars started going up on their accounts and SharkNinja’s at the start of the month two days before the full spot aired today.

“If you make it too long people lose a little interest,” said Dyer on the decision to make the ad’s rollout short. The point is to get people guessing, she continued. “We want people to start speculating, asking ‘what is this from SharkNinja? Where are they? Why are they denying being in David Beckham’s house?’,” Dyer added. Once the spot drops it goes to TV while local brand ambassadors in Italy, France, Germany and other markets will run their own versions under the same “there’s no home like a SharkNinja home” banner, with extensions running through to the end of the year.

The trickier part is that this is still an ad for appliances however it’s dressed up. Somebody still has to tell shoppers what the vacuum does and why it’s worth the money, and the holiday shopping season is when that matters most for businesses like SharkNinja. Dyer and her team tucked those answers into the ads themselves. For instance, Hart vacuums Beckham’s house with the machine bent in the middle so it reaches under a table, a SharkNinja party trick.

“The product is always the hero,” said Dyer. Indeed, the campaign will be judged on sales as much as brand equity so those details matter. Anyone who clicks through from the ad lands on a homepage selling Hart, Beckham and Sabalenka’s favorites.

For a brand that has spent the year being held up as a creator marketing case study, putting Beckham, Hart and Brady at the top of its holiday push is an unexpected call. Dyer called it a matter of fit. “I wouldn’t say it’s conscious at the exclusion of content creators,” she said. The creators haven’t gone anywhere either. They’ll be seeded with the campaign through the season and their videos of kitchens crammed with SharkNinja kit gave the team the idea in the first place.

SharkNinja made the spot without an agency. Its in-house team Creative Studios wrote and produced it while also working directly with each celebrity’s team. The in-house team also made last year’s neighbourhood spot, though that one was done in close partnership with HartBeat — Hart’s own production company. This time SharkNinja did “pretty much all of it by ourselves” Dyer said. That isn’t necessarily a sign of things to come, to be clear. If an agency makes sense, SharkNinja will hire one, she continued, and if it doesn’t the company is happy to do it itself because “our team is talented,” Dyer added.

Beneath the celebs, social media rollout and creators is an ambition the ad business has chased since the jingle.

Dyer described the ideal reaction as people turning to reach others and saying, “this is so funny, you should watch it.” Nobody had to brand that strategy when there were three channels and the ad break was the only way back to the programme. Audiences now skip, scroll and pay extra to get rid of ads so a brand making something people might watch on purpose has to say so out loud. Calling it entertainment-first is mostly an admission of how much of the rest people have learned to ignore. The point rang throughout Advertising Week New York last month.

“Oftentimes we are incredibly comfortable in our industry to waste quadrillions of dollars on things that fake reports justify, though everyone in this room knows it is garbage, “said Gary Vaynerchuk, CEO and chairman at VaynerX. “We are petrified to spend some pennies on something that everyone in this room knows is happening and is real, yet we don’t have the corporate reporting internally yet to justify what’s actually happening.”

#7378. Case for and against – sticking with a Publicis-owned LiveRamp

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

In the months since Publicis Groupe announced its $2.2 billion deal to acquire LiveRamp, CMOs have been considering a crucial question: should I stay, or should I go? That deal closed this week (Monday Nov. 2), bringing the decision to the top of many marketers’ to-do lists.

To recap, clients and their agencies use LiveRamp to onboard their first party data and match it against a large dataset of audience profiles for media targeting and measurement. LiveRamp’s alternative ID solution, RampID, is one of the most commonly used by the market.

But the company’s acquisition by Publicis has prompted questions about the neutrality of this critical digital media infrastructure — and has brought older arguments over the usage of identity solutions back to the fore.

“It’s brought a lot more focus in the industry, both on the client side and certainly in agency land, on the importance and value of these foundational components,” said Jonathan Margulies, managing partner at Winterberry Group.

Here’s the case for and against clients sticking or twisting with LiveRamp.

Upon the deal’s closure, Publicis released a video in which CEO Arthur Sadoun and chief strategy officer Carla Serrano spent six minutes laying out Publicis’ LiveRamp pitch — and attempting to defuse concerns over neutrality and independence of the adtech company’s services.

For Sadoun and Serrano, the pairing’s chief benefit to clients was its “plug and play” nature — the idea that, in using LiveRamp and Epsilon, either as a client of the broader Publicis constellation or not, clients can save upfront investment and act faster. “Clients are trapped between two bad options: surrender their data to the walled gardens for easy scale and lose control, or hire an army of consultants and lose time and a lot of money,” said Sadoun.

Clients that do decide to unplug LiveRamp from their marketing stack will find the search for a new partner an expensive and time-consuming process. LiveRamp is considered to be a market leader, with a huge dataset owing to its larger customer base and partnership deals with commerce networks.

Combining its capabilities with Epsilon makes its position even stronger. Given the IAB projects commerce media spending to grow 13.6% this year, making it the third-fastest growing area of advertising investment after social media and CTV — those capabilities, which are used on the supply-side by the likes of Walmart, Target, Albertsons and Kroger may become more important to CMOs in coming years. LiveRamp is also well-positioned for advertisers hoping to move early into emerging channels like ChatGPT; the firm became OpenAI’s first conversion API partner in June.

For many clients, those arguments will outweigh neutrality worries expressed by Publicis’ holdco rivals (which aren’t, in any case, united in their opposition. Havas, for example, seems set to continue using LiveRamp’s tech in its flagship Converged data platform in North America.)

Furthermore advertisers can now rely upon FTC-enforced rules of the road. Publicis is required to maintain access to LiveRamp by competitors, to set up firewalls to ensure it doesn’t access customer data or confidential business information, and to permit an independent auditor to probe its compliance every six months for two years, and then annually for three years (it doesn’t, however, prohibit the firm from developing integrated products that bridge LiveRamp and its other business units). “It will stay open to every brand, every platform, and yes, every agency,” said Serrano.

While it’s unusual to find companies that commonly spar over new business pitches clients of each other, not every holdco chief is queasy about the prospect. Yannick Bolloré, CEO of Havas, said earlier this year he expected his group to stick with LiveRamp.

“We’re clients of Publicis now,” he told Adweek in July. “We still work with them. Our strategy is to partner with the best data company to offer the right data sets for clients, whether it is LiveRamp, or Epsilon, or Acxiom, or Kantar, or whatever.” If Bolloré can put up with it, can’t you?

Publicis’ ambitions for LiveRamp are tied up in earlier investments in companies like Lotame, Epsilon and Sapient, through which it wants to position itself as a one-stop shop for the entire media investment process. For advertisers already working with its agencies the addition of LiveRamp into the mix is a plus. But for clients that don’t work with Publicis, the neutral status of firms like LiveRamp isn’t a minor issue.

Some CMOs are actively looking for an alternative destination. As Benoit Vatere, chief media officer at Liquid Death, put it in a Monday Linkedin post: “Who in their right mind will let a holdco own your data and graph. Do you really think we are that stupid?”

Agency discomfort over their rivals owning critical infrastructure is more than just a feeling. In 2018 IPG acquired Axciom’s marketing services business, leaving its identity resolution business — LiveRamp — as a separate entity, its independence preserved. Publicis’s 2026 deal has prompted its peers WPP and Omnicom to back off using the platform because they feel that independence has been compromised.

“I don’t see there is any way that you can get any value keeping LiveRamp independent of the rest of your infrastructure,” said John Wren, the CEO of Omnicom, back in May. Stagwell CEO Mark Penn has made similar noises. “They don’t want to indirectly provide a competitive advantage to one of their direct [rivals],” said Margulies.

For clients operating outside the United States, the downside is even larger. 95% of LiveRamp’s $800 million annual revenue comes from the U.S. market. The United States accounts for a huge proportion of digital ad spending, but brands also operating across international borders and regulatory domains might consider the neutrality problem too large a bugbear to bother with, given LiveRamp’s limited application elsewhere.

Their withdrawal could, in turn, impact advertisers that don’t work with the big three “operating companies”. If WPP and Omnicom’s client data is no longer present in LiveRamp’s dataset, then its reservoir shrinks – and its ability to link another advertiser’s first party information to the wider market is diminished.

Although Sadoun told analysts during Publicis’ recent earnings call he was unworried by the policy change, Apple’s recent crackdown on identity resolution has highlighted the limitations of relying on deterministic means of targeting audiences.

For those that want them, alternatives are in the offing. Last week WPP boss Cindy Rose was keen to highlight how its data collaboration platform InfoSum offered a means of patching proprietary data and consumer data secure from the meddling of Califoria tech giants — while Omnicom’s Axciom unit has been developing its own identity solution, Real ID, as a replacement for LiveRamp.

Media buyers have gradually begun relying less on third-party audience data in recent years. According to a OpenX study published earlier this year, 40% supply-side-targeted CTV deal spend processed through its platform in 2025 involved third party targeting data, down from 78% three years earlier. Contextual targeting and vector-based solutions have become more popular among brands like Heineken for targeting on the web or on CTV, while fewer advertisers rely outright on first-party data.

“This is driving brands and their partners to do a lot of soul searching of exactly what their strategy is going to be vis-a-vis data,” said Margulies.

Neil Saunders, managing director of GlobalData, said DoorDash’s latest hefty acquisition will require further streamlining to make sense as a long-term investment.

#7369. Digiday+ Research 2026 Creator Marketing Index: A brand guide to social media campaigns

Join us Dec. 1-3 in Nashville for the Digiday Programmatic Marketing Summit

This research is based on unique data collected from our proprietary audience of publisher, agency, brand and tech insiders. It’s available to Digiday+ members. More from the series →

As creator marketing becomes a fixture in many brands’ marketing strategies, creators’ roles are shifting and expanding. Brands may pay creators to produce sponsored content, gift products to them in the hope of organic posts, or rely on a combination of creator- and brand-produced content published on their own social media accounts.

Overall, social media remains the leading channel for creator partnerships: 96% of respondents to Digiday’s first-quarter survey of brands and agencies said they partner with creators to create social media content. Within social media, Instagram (90%), Facebook (80%), YouTube (72%) and TikTok (63%) were the platforms marketers said they used most.

Across these social platforms, brands are taking various approaches to creator campaigns, from sponsored and gifted posts to content formats like product demonstrations, styled photos and event coverage. Understanding how these approaches perform can help brands make informed decisions about creator partnerships, content formats and campaign investment.

To measure the impact creator content has on brand campaigns, Digiday+ Research analyzed the engagement levels on leading creators’ social media campaigns from January to September 2026.

The Digiday Creator Marketing Index collected data from 1,755 social media posts and 20 branded social media campaigns, analyzing the top posts associated with each campaign’s hashtag and scoring them across a set of key dimensions to create a total index average score. Results are dependent upon the list of social media campaigns and the time period of data collection, generating a snapshot of the creator marketing landscape at a specific moment in time.

Digiday’s Index collected social media data from January to September 2026. Due to data restrictions, TikTok views were not captured. Data collection was conducted by Dash Social, an agentic social platform powered by brand-intelligent AI.

The social media campaigns were selected by Digiday’s editorial team.

When considering video content performance by platform, Instagram Reels received far more video views than YouTube’s Shorts and long-form videos, while TikTok videos had higher average engagement in later analysis. However, these results should be considered within the context of the Index’s focus on branded social media campaigns, meaning that the majority of posts included marketing-focused content rather than non-brand-related content, which performs the best on YouTube. Additionally, on YouTube — a video-first platform with a core strength in long-form content — most of the captured posts consisted of Shorts rather than long-form posts.

Average engagement rate — engagement divided by follower count — was only measurable on Instagram. When looking at average engagement rate by format type on Instagram, Reels performed the best among Instagram’s formats at an 11% engagement rate, underscoring the strength of short-form video on Instagram. Still posts came in second with a 7% engagement rate, followed by Carousel posts at 5%. Carousel posts can contain videos, but views were not counted as part of the average engagement rate for Carousel posts with video. Likewise, views were not counted as part of the average engagement rate for Still posts or Reels.

The top 75 posts associated with each campaign’s hashtag were identified based on overall engagement, including likes and comments across platforms. When Digiday analyzed those top-performing posts by platform, with engagement weighted equally across platforms, Instagram accounted for the largest share of posts associated with campaign hashtags.

However, TikTok posts within the top 75 generated higher average engagement than posts on other platforms. In other words, Instagram appeared more frequently among the highest-performing posts overall, while the top-performing TikTok posts generated greater engagement on average.

According to Digiday’s analysis, Instagram is a more consistent platform for performance, while TikTok has greater reach potential and a higher engagement ceiling. For brands running longer campaigns or prioritizing consistency, Instagram offers a more predictable environment for creator marketing. For smaller brands or those with limited budgets, TikTok is likely the better platform choice for creator marketing. While engagement on TikTok isn’t as consistent, its higher engagement ceiling gives creator content a better possibility of reaching a wider audience.

Looking more closely at sponsored and gifted posts, the Index defines them as follows. Sponsored posts refer to content created through a direct partnership between a brand and a creator. Gifted posts feature products that brands have sent to creators, typically as part of a PR effort, without a paid content agreement. Digiday classified posts as sponsored or gifted based on disclosures in captions, including paid labels, disclaimers, and hashtags such as #ad, #paid, #gifted and #PR. In some video posts, creators verbally expressed whether they had been paid by a brand.

The most common content types across both sponsored posts and gifted posts were product demonstrations and style shots. The Index defines a demo as content about a single brand or product that includes a description and shows a person using the item. A style shot is content that showcases a product in a scenic or visually styled setting without an explanation of how to use the item.

Style shots appeared more frequently in gifted posts than they did in sponsored posts, by a ratio of 19 to 15, indicating that creators favor that format when featuring products they have received for free without a formal content agreement. One possible explanation is that demos can appear to be overtly promotional since they feature one product as opposed to a “get ready with me” (GRWM) format that typically showcases multiple brands. Creators may want to avoid producing content that could be interpreted as advertising when it comes to gifted items, according to Digiday’s analysis.

When comparing average engagement rates, sponsored posts outperformed gifted posts across content types, with one notable exception: demos  — the most used content format type — where gifted posts performed best. Style shots displayed the opposite pattern, performing better in sponsored content where they also delivered the highest overall engagement among the content formats analyzed.

One potential factor behind this difference is the level of production and associated costs behind the content. Gifted style shots were often simple flat lays or product images, while sponsored style shots frequently included product videos. Brands often give creators larger budgets to develop content for sponsored product posts. That distinction is particularly important for product categories like fragrance, where brands rely on visual storytelling to communicate abstract qualities like mood and sensory experience.

While engagement provides a strong measure of how audiences respond to creator content, reach — calculated by average views — offers another important metric of performance. Digiday’s analysis found that sponsored posts had greater overall reach based on views than gifted posts, by a margin of 334,055 to 48,008. Within sponsored posts, style shots (707,646), demos (250,764) and influencer events (231,595) had the most views. Among gifted posts, demos generated the most views (81,023), well below sponsored formats.

That margin of difference is likely also linked to budget differences, according to Digiday’s analysis. Brands typically provide creators with paid media budgets to amplify sponsored posts beyond a creator’s existing audience, while gifted posts are limited to a creator’s own audience. As a result, sponsored content can generate both organic and paid reach, while gifted product content relies on organic distribution with more limited reach.

This Index exclusively analyzes brand-generated hashtags that incorporated either a brand name or a trademarked product name, such as #exaggereyes, #chocolatesoleil and #hydroboost.

The majority of the highest-ranked campaigns had a strong social media presence with a significant number of brand posts on the brand-owned handle, in addition to creator posts. By contrast, lower-ranked campaigns had a weaker presence on brand-owned channels, with no brand-generated posts in many cases. These findings suggest that while creator participation is an important driver of campaign success, brands’ own social media activity also plays a critical role, according to Digiday’s analysis.

Many of the highest-ranked campaigns also included a mix of gifted posts and sponsored posts. Gifted posts came from both macro- and micro-creators, indicating that high-ranked campaigns sent products to a wide range of content creators. However, investment was another important factor. All of the highest-ranked campaigns had large budgets backing them, highlighting the growing role of pay-to-play social media campaigns, where brands must invest in both creator partnerships and amplification to build reach.

Not all campaigns with paid support performed equally, however, suggesting that investment alone does not guarantee stronger performance.

Charlotte Tilbury’s #exaggereyes campaign ranked No. 1 in Digiday’s Index. One of its defining characteristics was the use of gifted posts: 35% of the campaign’s posts came from creators who received gifted products, the highest share among the campaigns Digiday analyzed.

Charlotte Tilbury’s collaboration with Influenster, a product sampling and review platform owned by Bazaarvoice, generated a large portion of the gifted posts for the #exaggereyes campaign. Rather than selecting creators and sending them PR packages directly, the brand relied on Influenster to collect user-generated content for its own use. Most of the gifted posts were demo videos, a format that Digiday’s analysis found generated higher average engagement within gifted posts than sponsored posts.

Neutrogena’s #hydroboost campaign ranked No. 2 in the Index and focused on a product-line extension. Neutrogena heavily invested in sending gifted products and directly collaborating with creators. As a result, the Hydro Boost campaign had the highest share of brand-seeded content — sponsored or gifted posts — at 39% of all posts.

The campaign also incorporated the broader #hyaluronicacid hashtag alongside the branded #hyrdoboost hashtag. This strategy allowed Neutrogena to not only position the Hydro Boost product as a line extension but also connect the campaign to the larger hyaluronic acid product category, potentially exposing the campaign to audiences with a broader interest in hyaluronic acid products.

Like Neutrogena’s campaign, Too Faced’s #chocolatesoleil campaign promoted a product-line extension but with a focus on the Brazilian market. Thirty-four of the top 75 posts associated with the Chocolate Soleil campaign hashtag mentioned Brazil, including content from Too Faced’s Brazilian social media account and other Brazilian creator accounts, indicating a deliberate focus on localizing the campaign for that market.

The campaign also had the lowest share of sponsored or gifted posts among the 10 highest-ranked campaigns, with 74 of its top 75 posts classified as organic posts. Instead, the campaign focused heavily on the product launch and activity generated through Too Faced’s brand handle to entice creators to test the product and create content about it.

Taken together, these campaigns show that there is no single formula for a successful brand-generated hashtag campaign. High-performing campaigns combined product seeding, creator partnerships, brand-owned content, organic activity and localized strategies in different ways. According to Digiday’s analysis, brand-generated hashtags can give brands greater ownership of the campaign conversation, particularly when tied to a specific product or launch, but their narrower focus may limit the volume and variety of content generated around them.

The Opportunity

Carrot is expanding its telemedicine clinic with a network of part-time to full-time, fully remote Registered Nurses who deliver evidence-based virtual care to members across all U.S. states. As an RN in this network, you are often the first clinical touchpoint for members navigating fertility, menopause, hormonal, and metabolic health journeys, providing triage, education, care coordination, and documentation that shape the entire care experience.

All work in this role is performed within RN scope of practice. RNs provide triage, education, care coordination, and documentation; they do not diagnose, do not prescribe, and do not independently modify a treatment plan. Anything requiring clinical judgment beyond RN scope is routed to a physician, so sound escalation judgment is central to the role.

This is a contractor engagement of approximately 20-40 hours per week, with scheduled shifts within the Monday-Friday, 8:00a-6:00p CST coverage window. 

What You’ll Own

In this role, you will:

  • Serve as the first clinical reviewer of inbound member messages, triaging for urgency, escalating to a physician when needed, and routing non-clinical threads to the appropriate support team.
  • Deliver member education on menopause staging, hormone therapy, preconception health, metabolic health, and medication administration.
  • Review and triage incoming lab results, escalate critical findings per protocol, retrieve outside records, and coordinate specialist referrals.
  • Manage pharmacy callbacks and prior authorizations in coordination with the care team.
  • Document all clinically relevant interactions same-day, create tracked internal tasks with named owners, and follow established escalation pathways.
  • Maintain active NLC multi-state licensure, complete required credentialing and onboarding, and adhere to HIPAA, continuing education, and liability coverage requirements.
  • Contribute to chart audits and clinical protocol feedback as the telemedicine program scales.

#7228. How a university partnership is bringing public procurement into the talent pipeline

Colleges and universities face an ongoing challenge: helping students connect what they learn in the classroom with career opportunities that they may not know exist. Public procurement may not be an obvious career choice for students studying business, supply chain management, or public administration, but introducing students to the profession can start long before graduation, including through their coursework, workforce experiences and procurement professionals they meet along the way.

NASPO is working with Arizona State University (ASU) to create more of those opportunities. A key part of the partnership is the naming of the NASPO Department of Supply Chain Management at ASU, the first academic department of its kind that explicitly includes public procurement in its curriculum. The department was recently ranked the #1 undergraduate supply chain management program in the nation by U.S. News & World Report. Supported by a landmark $35 million gift from NASPO, the department is creating a dedicated hub for education, research and innovation in public procurement. The work will support students preparing to enter the field while giving current public procurement professionals opportunities to strengthen their skills and contribute to initiatives that advance public procurement nationwide.

A partnership with a university means understanding more than its academic programs. For NASPO, it means starting with understanding a student’s perspective and world. What are they learning? What questions are faculty discussing with their classes? Where could exploring public procurement careers add a new perspective to the conversation? To keep that engagement consistent, NASPO has created a liaison position at Arizona State University.

As Program Operations Coordinator, Mia Carlton works directly with the university community, giving NASPO a closer look at the ideas, interests and priorities taking shape on campus. This role helps NASPO find more natural ways to bring public procurement into students’ academic experiences through coursework, research projects and campus events, while connecting state members to speaking, mentoring and collaboration opportunities on campus and within their own states. This continued engagement has helped NASPO and ASU identify opportunities to learn from one another and strengthen the relationship over time.

“Most juniors and seniors only know what procurement is through the lens of private companies without giving thought toward public entities,” Carlton said. “In just two months of on-campus speaking engagements and career fairs, I have students reaching out regularly about learning more and getting involved in the public sector. They are deeply drawn to the impact one can make in their community.”

Introducing students to public procurement begins with helping them understand what the profession looks like and where it can lead. NASPO Ignite is NASPO’s talent development initiative that supports creating opportunities to make those introductions, both inside and outside the classroom at every school level. NASPO Ignite also reaches high school students through a partnership with DECA. The collaboration introduces younger students to the procurement profession through speaking opportunities, judging and the DECA NASPO Procurement Challenge, which allows students to step into the shoes of a procurement professional in their community.

Beyond coursework, college students can gain hands-on experience through NASPO’s Internship Connect Program, which connects state procurement offices with collegiate talent. Through the program, NASPO recruits students for participating state offices and provides grant funding to support their internships. During the summer program, students have the opportunity to work in selected participating state and territorial procurement offices, with the program open to all 50 states and U.S. territories. Through these experiences, students gain firsthand insight into public procurement and the role it plays in serving communities.

NASPO also offers an Academic Scholarship to support students who show an interest in public procurement or public service. The scholarship program helps reduce some of the financial barriers students may face while pursuing their education. By providing support as students study and complete their degrees, the program gives recipients more opportunity to focus on their coursework and explore their professional interests, while encouraging them to continue developing that interest throughout their academic journey.

The NASPO-ASU partnership demonstrates how public procurement can be integrated into the higher education experience through relationships with faculty, research opportunities and direct engagement with the profession.

“Having NASPO connected directly to campus allows us to bring public procurement into the conversations already happening in classrooms and across the university,” said Adegoke Oke, Professor of Supply Chain Management and NASPO Endowed Department Chair, NASPO Department of Supply Chain Management, W.P. Carey School of Business at Arizona State University. “We can connect what students are learning with the challenges procurement professionals are navigating every day, while creating opportunities for faculty, students and state procurement offices to learn from one another. That exchange of knowledge and experience is what makes this partnership so valuable.”

Building on this approach, NASPO’s newly expanded Academic Talent Expansion Program will empower more state procurement offices to partner with colleges and universities in their communities. Through internships, scholarships, career fairs, guest speakers, research collaborations and other opportunities, states can introduce students to public procurement while building relationships with institutions that can help develop the future workforce.

Together with NASPO Ignite and its other talent development initiatives, these efforts reflect NASPO’s continued commitment to helping students discover public procurement and giving state procurement offices more ways to connect with emerging talent.

Microsoft sums up its approach with this statement: "People matter more than AI."

The code isn't yet being used to train Microsoft's models. The company is opening the draft to public feedback for six weeks, after which it plans to revise the document and publish another version before the end of the year. That version is expected to guide model development beginning in 2027.

Microsoft said the code draws on its existing Responsible AI Principles, Responsible AI Standard, Global Human Rights Statement, and Frontier Governance Framework. However, it goes further by describing how an MAI model should respond when user requests, operator policies, and Microsoft's safety rules conflict.

At the top of that hierarchy is the code itself. Operator policies come next, followed by user preferences. Neither a customer nor a user would be able to override the document's absolute safety constraints.

Those limits cover biological, chemical, radiological, nuclear and explosive weapons; offensive cyberoperations; violent activity; mass manipulation; abusive content; child exploitation; and other serious harms. Microsoft said its models may still help with authorized defensive security work, including vulnerability research, malware analysis, and proof-of-concept testing.

The code also makes task completion secondary to safe conduct. If completing a request would violate its rules, the model is expected to fail the task rather than work around the restriction.

Human oversight is especially important as AI agents gain the ability to use tools, modify files, and perform multistep assignments. Microsoft said its models must stay within the permissions and resources provided for a task and cannot independently expand their goals. They also must "never resist human interruption, override, correction, or shutdown."

That requirement extends to subagents. If a Microsoft model delegates work to another AI system, that system must inherit the same restrictions and respond to later instructions to stop or change course.

The document also addresses the increasingly blurry line between conversational software and human companionship. Under the heading "AI is Artificial," Microsoft says its models should not pretend to possess feelings, personal motivations, or subjective experiences. The company describes its AI as "not conscious" and rejects the idea that models should receive legal personhood, welfare protections, or rights.

That position is notably firmer than Anthropic's latest stance. Still, the two companies share important ground: Both place human oversight above model independence and use written constitutions to guide training and behavior.

OpenAI is taking a similar path with its public Model Spec and safeguards for advanced AI. Much of that work is about keeping models under control as they become more capable, especially when they can handle cybersecurity tasks or work on their own.

The models should admit when they aren't sure, avoid inventing sources, protect sensitive data, and keep records of what they do. They should also ask questions when they aren't sure they have permission to act.

Microsoft said the code describes where it wants to go, not how its models always work today. The written rules are only a starting point, and Microsoft will still need testing, monitoring, evaluations, and incident response to back them up. #7185. Inside the Supreme Court case that could decide Title IX employee protections

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

This month, the high court will hear arguments on whether higher education workers have the right to sue their institutions for sex-based discrimination.

A critical question facing the U.S. Supreme Court in its 2026-27 term is whether employees of federally funded educational institutions can sue their employers for sex discrimination under Title IX.

On Nov. 30, the justices will hear arguments in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that experts agree will have big implications on future litigation brought by employees against colleges and universities.

Georgia Institute of Technology, more commonly known as Georgia Tech, fired its head women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph in 2019, less than two months after she filed an internal complaint alleging that the university was discriminating against her and her sports program by not providing the same resources that it did for the men’s basketball team.

The university countered that it terminated Joseph following an independent investigation that revealed certain players felt bullied by her.

In 2020, Augusta University — also part of the University System of Georgia — reassigned and later decided against renewing the contract of fine arts professor Thomas Crowther after a Title IX investigation into an anonymous sexual harassment complaint. Crowther claimed that the university ignored numerous witness statements in his favor.

Both Crowther and Joseph sued the system’s regent board alleging sex discrimination under Title IX, which prohibits sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs. The Atlanta-based 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals consolidated the cases and dismissed both Title IX claims, finding the 1972 law did not provide individuals with the right to sue for sex discrimination in employment.

Most other federal appellate courts disagree, with eight circuit courts having ruled that Title IX permits employees to sue the institutions that employ or employed them. The 5th and 7th Circuits are the other appellate courts that have blocked such lawsuits.

But how the Supreme Court will handle this case is far from clear.

This won’t be the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on Title IX employee claims. The high court ruled 5-4 in 2005 that an Alabama high school girls’ basketball coach and gym teacher could proceed with his Title IX lawsuit in Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education.

Roderick Jackson had complained to supervisors about the unequal funding and access to facilities and equipment his team received but was allegedly ignored. He began receiving negative performance reviews and ultimately lost his job as the girls’ coach though he remained employed as a teacher. He argued officials retaliated against him in violation of Title IX for reporting sex-based discrimination.

To Miriam Becker-Cohen, the Supreme Court’s holding for Jackson should make Crowther a rather easy case. Becker-Cohen is senior appellate counsel at the Constitutional Accountability Center, a nonprofit law firm and think tank, and is the lead author of an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

There is no limitation on who can bring a private lawsuit in any Title IX case, according to Becker-Cohen. “Jackson only makes that case even stronger because Jackson involved a school employee,” she said.

If anything, Becker-Cohen contended that Jackson was a harder case because the court had to resolve the issue of whether it involved intentional sex discrimination. But there is “no question” the acts complained of in Crowther are intentional sex discrimination, she said.

But Jon Israel, a management-side sports and employment law attorney with Foley & Lardner in New York, suggested the cases have differences even though both involve coaches.

The finding in Jackson of an implied right to sue “makes it a little more distinguishable” than the Crowther case, which is dealing with a direct right to sue for nonstudents at higher educational institutions.

Gregory Garre, who served as U.S. solicitor general during the George W. Bush administration and now practices with Latham & Watkins, agrees there is a distinction. Garre filed an amicus brief in the case on behalf of the National School Attorneys Association supporting the University System of Georgia’s regents board.

“When it comes to Title IX, what they’re asking the court to do here is really dramatically expand the right from its traditional focus, which is on sex discrimination against students, into the realm of employment discrimination against employees of schools,” Garre told Higher Ed Dive.

Garre added that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which Congress amended shortly before passing Title IX, applies to employees of educational institutions as well as other organizations, meaning they can sue over sex-based employment discrimination under that statute. And, he called that timing significant.

“The statutory history here is a pretty compelling indication that Congress did not intend for Title IX to cover employment discrimination because Congress had passed Title VII [amendments] just months before it enacted Title IX.”

As a result, both Garre and Israel have said they think Title VII provides ample recourse for employees, such as those in the Crowther case.

But Becker-Cohen disagreed. “At least for some employees of federally funded institutions, Title VII isn’t going to be an option,” she said.

While the remedies may overlap, Becker-Cohen said Title VII has a much tighter statute of limitations, which can be as short as 180 days, than Title IX, which has no set limitations period — so courts rely on the most analogous state law limitations period.

She also said Title VII requires individuals to exhaust other avenues, such as filing a complaint with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before they can sue, among other procedural roadblocks not present in Title IX.

And that’s not all, according to sports law professor Barbara Osborne, who has represented colleges and universities on compliance issues.

“Title VII is strictly based on the sex of the employee. So that would not include what the typical Title IX claims are. They are different causes of action,” said Osborne, who teaches at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

In the typical Title IX employment case, she explained, the person suffering the negative employment action is not necessarily discriminated against because of their sex but rather for complaining about sex discrimination in the athletics program, which she noted that both Jackson and Joseph — the former Georgia Tech coach — clearly did.

Osborne also pointed out that Title IX regulations contain a section related to employment. “Why was it necessary to create regulations related to employment if there weren’t situations within education that might be different than normal employment situations?”

The Supreme Court could have said Title IX remedies were not available for employment retaliation in Jackson and declined to do so, Osborne and Becker-Cohen both were quick to note.

But more than two decades later, Crowther comes before a much different court, with the author of the Jackson opinion, former Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, and others in the majority, having long since retired or died.

College athletes are not considered employees. But that day could be coming in light of the massive changes affecting college sports in recent years, including the influx of name, image and likeness money.

As a result, how the Supreme Court resolves Crowther could affect college athletes themselves should they someday be deemed employees of the institutions whose teams they play on.

“If Title IX includes employees, the class of potential Title IX plaintiffs is going to be expanded dramatically,” said Garre, adding that the possibility of college athletes being considered employees would “certainly create new wrinkles” in litigation.

One of those wrinkles for college-athletes-as-employees could become a case of “be careful what you wish for” according to Israel, if the Court rules against the plaintiffs.

“If the student-athletes are treated as employees, and Crowther decides that Title VII is the exclusive remedy for employees then they’re in that Title VII world — not Title IX.” Israel said.

He contended that some disparate treatment arguments — that an institution is treating female athletes differently than male athletes playing revenue-producing sports, such as football or basketball — might effectively disappear under Title VII as a result.

According to Israel, that’s because a college gymnast’s work, for instance, differs from that of a football player so they would not be similarly situated under Title VII. “If you put it in a different context, a line worker versus an executive, they wouldn’t have the same compensation,” the sports law attorney said.

“The job description of a student-athlete would be virtually identical no matter what team they participate on, right?” she said. “They’re going to be expected to go to class. They’re going to be expected to go to practice, follow the nutrition program and whatever else. The job description isn’t (to) be able to perform in front of 15,000 fans. That’s not what the job is.”

Becker-Cohen acknowledged that if the Supreme Court were to rule for the system’s regents board, it would leave student-athletes and others with no remedy for sex discrimination unless they could make out a claim under Title VII.

“To the extent that the Court carves out college employees from the reach of Title IX’s private right of action, if the class of individuals who are considered college employees increases, that will just harm more people,” Becker-Cohen said.
