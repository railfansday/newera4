#5845. From Callback to Confidence: How Engineering an ESS for Field Reduces the Costly Callbacks

Installer on-site callbacks are the silent margin killer in residential energy management and storage. They consume labor, fuel, new project scheduling capacity, and homeowner trust in ways that rarely show up in the original bid. Across the industry, callbacks come from three sources that often overlap: system designs that are too complex to cleanly install, hardware failures after the system is in service, and challenges that arise in the field during installations. Most contractors only feel the cumulative cost years after an install have rolled past. Conversely highly reliable system hardware allows a contractor to confidently “right-size” the project price bid, helping to bring in more business in a period of industry transition.

Preventing callbacks is not only the installer’s responsibility. How the system is engineered, built, and supported all impact callbacks. FranklinWH has focused on quality products and the simplest install, with hardware held to an independently verified reliability standard. The company provides support before, during and after the installation. All of this helps to minimize callbacks. Service data shows once system complexity and hardware failure are engineered down, the dominant driver of post-install activity that remains is the installation stage itself, the real-world variability of the field. That, too, is a problem to solve at the source, not one to pass to the installer.

This paper walks through the anatomy of a callback, the hidden cost stack it creates, the actual root causes industry-wide and in our own data, and the engineering, support, and service decisions made to reduce callback risk before the truck rolls, and to absorb the cost when it can’t be avoided.

#5824. Outer Spaces takes solar beyond the rooftop with integrated backyard energy system

Residential solar is finding a new place to call home: the backyard.

Outer Spaces, a new company founded by the entrepreneurs behind outdoor living brand Outer, is introducing a solar-powered pavilion that combines photovoltaic generation, battery storage and usable outdoor living space. The company’s first product, OS1, can be designed for multiple functions, including an outdoor kitchen, office, gym or gathering space, while generating electricity and providing backup power for the home.

Outer Spaces is planning to produce outdoor structures that integrate solar PV and energy storage. Credit: Outer Spaces

The California-based company is positioning OS1 as a new category of residential energy infrastructure that goes beyond conventional rooftop arrays and garage-mounted batteries. It builds on the growing interest in solar structures that also serve a secondary purpose.

OS1 combines a solar pavilion with a finished outdoor power deck. Solar panels integrated into the overhead structure generate electricity, while a thin battery storage system is housed beneath the deck. According to Outer Spaces, the combination allows the structure to produce energy throughout the day and provide backup power during outages.

Rather than competing exclusively with traditional rooftop solar, OS1 offers homeowners an additional way to use available property for energy production, particularly when roof aesthetics, space limitations, or homeowner preferences become obstacles to conventional installations.

Outer Spaces describes OS1 as a modular system designed for rapid on-site assembly, with minimal to no excavation required. The company says installation avoids traditional construction steps such as concrete pouring and lumber cutting. Once the structure is assembled, a licensed electrician then connects its energy system to the customer’s home.

The product’s first availability is scheduled for 2027, beginning in Southern California and select additional markets; pricing is expected to be announced in early 2027.

Solar canopies and pergolas have increasingly incorporated features such as EV charging, lighting, fans and weather protection, giving homeowners additional reasons to invest in solar outside the traditional rooftop model.

Outer Spaces represents another potential avenue for expanding residential solar adoption through design-driven products. The opportunity will ultimately depend on pricing, installation economics, permitting requirements and the system’s measurable energy performance.

#5769. Solar lease holdouts still see value in privately-owned residential projects

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act drastically reduced the lifespan of federal subsidies available to certain segments of the solar market, and among the first to go was the investment tax credit for residential projects. The ITC is a historic renewable energy subsidy that provides a 30% tax credit against the total cost of an eligible project, and it is currently available to the commercial and utility-scale solar segments.

A residential solar project in New York installed by Nickels Energy Solutions. Credit: Nickels Energy Solutions

After 2025, homeowners hoping to install solar on their house couldn’t subsidize the project’s cost with this federal subsidy if it was privately owned. However, a loophole in the tax credit language has allowed leased or “third party-owned” (TPO) solar projects to still receive the ITC (25D) on residential solar projects. In this scenario, it matters who owns the solar project, not necessarily where it’s built. The project can be subsidized because it is owned by a commercial company, and not the homeowner.

Some residential solar installers are still strictly pursuing and installing privately-owned solar projects. Nickels Energy Solutions (NES), a residential solar contractor working in Central New York believes a privately-owned PV system is still the better deal for the customer.

“We don’t have the same offers as some of the bigger companies,” said Kevin Nickels, VP of sales with NES. “If you can offer leases, it’s like the ‘haves and have-nots.’ I don’t have that loophole or that trick up my sleeve, so it forces me to get the project sold, while I’m as sharp as I can be with margins to make it somewhat competitive.”

TPO solar transactions are typically arranged at a lower up-front cost than a wholly owned solar project. Lease contract lengths vary by provider, and financiers have surety in these transactions because the projects will be built with products from an approved vendor list (AVL). Products on an AVL can qualify for a domestic content subsidy, another tax credit in addition to the ITC, because they are manufactured using a certain percentage of domestic components.

These are premium products vetted for AVLs. But it can also mean that installers are paying premium prices for components in building TPO solar projects.

Nickels believes that, while a privately owned residential solar project will cost more at the start, the combination of expensive components and interest rates for financing over a lease term — with factors like rate increases — can cost a customer more in the long run.

“I don’t think all leases are bad,” he said. “I just don’t think it’s what’s best for the industry or the homeowner.”

Rudy Wright, owner of Wright-Way Solar of Tyler, Texas, has shifted the company’s focus from building new projects to servicing existing systems. As the ITC exited the residential solar market, so did many of the lenders financing these projects. Without lending options, Wright-Way’s installation output has slowed down, and those clients who can afford solar are paying with cash or using a home equity loan to cover the cost.

Despite that, Wright said he isn’t very interested in building leased solar projects.

Wright-Way Solar built a solar plus storage project at a Ranch in Texas featuring arrays that are facing east, west and south. Credit: Wright-Way Solar

“I do think it cost me some of those entry level clients, but every time I analyzed the financing end of it, I felt like I was benefitting my company and the industry more than the customer,” Wright said. “I haven’t seen the angle where bringing a pre-purchase agreement or bringing a lease agreement has enough ironclad return for the customer.”

Tyler is a unique market for solar because many of Wright-Way’s customers are trying to make their properties more resilient, and less reliant on the grid, by installing solar and storage, and not just seeking cost savings for their energy use. Wright has installed solar plus storage systems on Texan ranches at a scale close a small commercial project.

That promise of resilience is still enough reason for some people to commission a solar project in Texas, where the state’s independent electrical grid operator has faltered and experienced dangerous outages in recent years.

“Folks realize money in the bank doesn’t do you any good if the power goes out,” Wright said.

NES is still building a steady pipeline of residential solar projects in New York. Nickels said that has been possible in part because many of its clients are upper-middle class; but New York also maintains an incentive program for solar projects, NY-Sun, which includes a 25% state tax credit for residential systems.

“The OBBBA caught a lot of states flat-footed,” Nickels said. “Without federal support, it puts a lot of pressure on what the state is offering.”

Some states’ solar subsidy programs have stepped in where federal support has diminished since the OBBBA was passed. The North Carolina Clean Energy Technology Center maintains the Database of State Incentives for Renewables & Efficiency, which analyzes which states currently have policies and incentives supporting clean energy developments. Every state has policy enabling solar projects, but not necessarily cost incentives for individual projects.

While traditional residential solar projects have lost a significant subsidy, the reality is that energy costs have risen in response to new grid demands from hyperscale data centers and oil scarcity from the United States’ war in Iran. Sure, a privately owned residential solar project won’t receive the ITC, but it can still stabilize energy costs without them.

“We lost a big incentive, but the rate increase is proportional,” Nickels said.

#5757. Three Routes to VPP Revenue for Installers, EPCs, and Developers Utility programs, wholesale markets, and bilateral contracts each come with different eligibility rules, payment structures, and commercial responsibilities.

By Intersolar & Energy Storage North America

In June 2026, Google and Voltus announced a three-year agreement to unlock up to 100 megawatts (MW) of distributed capacity annually in PJM. Under Voltus’s Bring Your Own Capacity model, Google funds procurement while Voltus aggregates qualifying distributed energy resources (DERs) and compensates participating customers.1

The deal is one of three distinct paths to earn revenue from DERs. Across the U.S., utility programs, wholesale electricity markets, and bilateral contracts each have their own eligibility requirements, payment structures and commercial responsibilities.

Utility programs offer an established pathway for compensating customers and asset owners who reduce demand, shift consumption or discharge stored energy during periods of grid need. Payments may be tied to enrollment, committed capacity, measured performance or a combination of these factors. Massachusetts, for example, offers residential battery customers $275 per kW of average summer performance through ConnectedSolutions2.

Beyond customer incentive programs, utilities also procure distributed flexibility through contracts to address local grid constraints or broader capacity needs. Unlike standardized incentives, these arrangements can establish specific delivery requirements and compensation terms, creating opportunities for developers and service providers to assemble and manage DER portfolios. During a July 2026 heat event, Green Mountain Power dispatched 90 MW of stored energy capacity, helping customers avoid an estimated $6 million in peak-related costs during one peak hour3.

Aggregated DERs can earn revenue from energy, capacity, and ancillary services in wholesale electricity markets, subject to regional market rules and resource qualification. Unlike utility-specific programs, wholesale markets can provide access to broader regional revenue opportunities and multiple grid services. For developers and installers, participation typically occurs through an aggregator or other qualified market participant rather than through direct market registration.

While Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Order 2222 established a broader framework for DER participation, uneven implementation across regional markets continues to constrain commercial opportunities for aggregated resources. Market access does not automatically translate into revenue, as individual resources must still meet qualification requirements and comply with market-specific rules. New York Independent System Operator (NYISO), for example, requires DER aggregations to meet minimum capacity and real-time telemetry requirements, which can increase participation costs and limit the economic viability of smaller resources.

Wholesale participation can also expose VPP portfolios to fluctuating market prices, performance obligations and restrictions on receiving compensation for overlapping services. In Texas, ERCOT’s Aggregate Distributed Energy Resource (ADER) pilot allows aggregated DERs to participate in wholesale energy and ancillary services markets, subject to qualification, telemetry, and dispatch requirements.

Bilateral contracts allow utilities and large electricity consumers to procure distributed flexibility directly from aggregators and service providers. Growing interest from data center operators and other large energy buyers is expanding the potential market for VPP capacity beyond traditional utility programs. Unlike standardized programs, bilateral agreements can be tailored to a buyer’s specific needs, with negotiated terms for capacity, delivery periods, compensation, and performance obligations.

The appropriate route depends on existing customer relationships, market access, and the responsibilities each partner assumes. Developers and EPCs working on commercial storage projects may find opportunities through utility procurement or capacity contracts, while residential installers with established battery customer bases may be well positioned to incorporate VPP enrollment into utility programs. Wholesale markets can expand revenue opportunities through aggregation partners, and bilateral contracts can create opportunities to develop resources around specific buyer needs.

#5719. enSights brings AI-powered business management to solar and storage operations

By Marie DeFreitas, Contributor | November 16, 2026

As solar and energy storage portfolios grow in both size and complexity, managing performance is just one part of the equation. Owners and operators must also keep track of power purchase agreements (PPAs), warranties, service contracts, billing, compliance requirements and project-level profitability.

enSights is looking to bring those responsibilities together with the launch of its AI Studio, a new addition to its Energy Business Management (EBM) platform. The company announced the launch Nov. 10.

The AI Studio is designed to give solar and storage owners, operators, and asset managers access to a customizable team of AI specialists that can support business and operational workflows. Through no-code tools, users can create AI dashboards and reports using natural-language instructions, while still establishing their own workflows, routines, data-access permissions and approval requirements.

The approach here reflects a broader shift toward renewable energy software, it’s moving beyond monitoring individual assets toward managing the financial and operational performance of entire portfolios.

According to enSights, its EBM platform brings together energy management systems (EMS), production data, billing, PPA information, operations and maintenance, contractual obligations and compliance on a unified data foundation. That integration is central to the company’s AI strategy.

Rather than limiting AI applications to identifying equipment issues or answering generation-related questions, the platform is designed to connect energy production with the contracts and financial obligations associated with each project.

The AI Studio includes an orchestrator that coordinates specialized AI agents focused on areas like monitoring, operations, revenue, warranties, permits, lenders, insurers, and asset management. For portfolio managers, those capabilities could help reduce the time spent reviewing disconnected systems and manually reconciling information across departments. Automated reviews of contractual obligations, revenue performance and operational alerts could also help teams identify issues earlier and direct attention to matters that still require human intervention.

The enSights AI Studio dashboard provides solar and energy storage operators with AI-powered tools for asset management, operational workflows, and financial oversight. Credit: enSights

enSights emphasized that people remain in control of the AI Team. Users determine which workflows the agents handle and where approvals are required. Physical asset controls remain governed by established EMS and site-level systems.

The AI Studio also builds on an expanded financial management offering within the EBM platform. Its Financial Asset Management Module includes revenue assurance, contract, and obligation management, and profit-and-loss reporting.

The module is intended to provide project-level financial visibility, helping companies identify and prevent revenue losses while generating reports for investors.

The platform also includes real-time EMS, weather and tariff forecasting, along with dispatch planning intended to optimize revenue while maintaining grid safety and local compliance.

enSights says its platform is deployed globally across more than 100,000 sites, representing over 5 GW of assets. The company plans to demonstrate the AI Studio and its broader EBM platform at RE+ 2026.

As AI gains traction across the clean energy industry, the next competitive advantage could come from connecting operational intelligence with the financial and contractual decisions that determine how successfully renewable energy portfolios perform.

#5713. Rocket Fuel: The Last Frontier For Natural Gas

Fossil fuels are slowly beginning to lose their grip on the global transportation sector, with one key omission: rocket fuel. The rocket fuel conundrum has earned an uptick in attention in recent years, now that rocket launches, once rare, are becoming as commonplace as rush hour traffic. The Elon Musk venture SpaceX, for example, is building new natural gas pipelines in order to produce its own methane-based propellant, needed to sustain the launch of many thousands of rockets in the coming years.

Not all rocket fuel is methane-based, but that’s where some aerospace firms, including SpaceX, have landed in the pursuit of space domination. Methane is processed from natural gas, stripping away nitrogen and other impurities. Liquefied and mixed with liquid oxygen, it becomes a powerful propellant.

With its future volume of launch operations in mind, SpaceX aims to exercise as much control as possible over its fuel supply. The company is investing in its own natural gas pipelines and on-site fuel production facilities to help ensure that the taps don’t run dry, though it still relies on the gas industry to find the stuff and bring it up from the ground.

SpaceX has already been sucking up a copious amount of natural gas for its existing operations in Texas and Florida. “SpaceX (SPCX) is emerging as one of the largest new customers for natural gas,” reported the oil and gas analytics firm East Daley in October.

“Unlike Falcon 9, which burns rocket-grade kerosene and liquid oxygen, Starship creates a direct link between launch activity and natural gas consumption. SPCX plans to process and liquefy pipeline gas into liquid methane to fuel the rockets,” East Daley elaborates.

As described by East Daley, the SpaceX subsidiary Coastal Connect Services is planning a 32.4-mile natural gas pipeline to hook up SpaceX operations in Cape Canaveral, Florida with existing infrastructure jointly owned by Kinder Morgan and Energy Transfer.

Meanwhile, at the company’s launch site and newly incorporated (as of 2025) City of Starbase in Cameron County, Texas, plans are in motion for SpaceX affiliate Lone Star Mineral Development to connect the new 8-mile “Starpipe” natural gas pipeline with another 145-mile pipeline currently being built by the firms WhiteWater, Enbridge, and MPLX. Starpipe is expected to begin shuttling natural gas into Starbase Texas early next year.

The two pipeline projects come alongside word that SpaceX has nailed down approvals for parts of a new $100 billion project that expands the company’s US footprint into another combination city-and-launch-site under the name of Starbase, Louisiana. The new city will presumably also require a reliable supply of natural gas for on-site rocket fuel production.

For the record, Starbase, Louisiana is not part of the educational non-profit organization STARBASE Louisiana, an affiliate of the national STARBASE program of the US Department of Defense, aimed at stimulating interest in STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, Mathematics) topics among 5th grade students. The Louisiana branch of STARBASE is located Barksdale Air Force Base in Bossier Parish, in the northwestern part of the state.

Starbase, Louisiana will take up 125,000 acres of land at Pecan Island in a marshy region along the southern coast in Vermilion Parish, where plans are being laid for 30 launches per day. More than a dozen launch towers will eventually sprout up at the site, where the operation will include producing rocket fuel.

If you’ve gotten wind of the project under the name “Osprey,” you’re not dreaming. The new city was reportedly code-named “Project Osprey” during initial discussions with public officials. As described by the Gulf State News affiliate WWNO (New Orleans Public Radio) on November 11, Project Osprey was shrouded in secrecy during the initial negotionas, with “dozens” of local officials signing non-disclosure agreements that limited what they could say about the project.

The Osprey code name also reportedly enabled Vermilion Parish officials to sidestep public participation in ostensibly public hearings on the Starbase, Louisiana project earlier this year. Louisiana Governor Jeff Landry finally let the cat out of the bag in a public announcement in August. Local residents and the nonprofit organization Vermillion Citizens coalition have already filed a lawsuit against the project, alleging that parish officials approved parts of Starbase, Louisiana in violation of the state’s Open Public Meetings Law.

If and when shovels hit the ground on Starbase, Louisiana, the Trump administration is taking steps to help ensure that natural gas producers in the US come through with the goods — not just for SpaceX, of course, although the firm has become a market-mover in the gas industry as noted by East Daley.

On November 10, for example, the US Department of Energy announced the finishing touches on a $480 million loan in support of Project Elkhorn, an endeavor under the umbrella of the oil and gas firm Arena Energy. Project Elkhorn covers the modernization of 22 existing platforms off the Louisiana coast — coincidentally or not, the same state that expects to host the new Starbase city.

Arena launched in 1999 with a focus on revitalizing mature oil and gas fields. According to the Energy Department, Project Elkhorn will ramp natural gas production up to 77 million cubic feet per day at peak production among the 22 platforms, alongside up to 38,600 barrels of oil per day. By way of contrast, the 22 platforms currently produce 30,000 barrels of oil per day. Over the lifespan of Project Elkhorn, the collective total is expected to add 25 million barrels of oil and 250 billion cubic feet of natural gas to the US fossil energy roster.

As CEO of the leading EV maker Tesla, Elon Musk has earned his share of credit for rescuing electric cars from the dustbin of automotive obscurity and vaulting them into the mainstream mobility market of today — if not so much here in the US, then certainly overseas. Musk’s other ventures, on the other hand, threaten to offset, if not override, whatever sustainability gains are to be made from EVs.

In addition to SpaceX, Musk’s xAI data centers have also contributed to spiking demand for natural gas. Last year word dropped that Musk’s Colossus data center in Memphis, Tennessee trucked in approximately three dozen industrial-sized gas generators to power the site, at least 15 of which were already in operation without an air quality permit. The Southern Environmental Law Center has been among those raising the alarm over toxic emissions from the generators, which lack the pollution controls required of permanent power plants.

Natural gas also factors into a new data center planned for the SpaceX-backed City of Starbase, Texas, which sits adjacent to Brownsville. The city of Brownsville recently imposed a temporary moratorium on new data center construction. According to ABC News affiliate KVUE and other sources, earlier this year Elon Musk asked Brownsville officials to de-annex a parcel of land at its outskirts, evidently in order to building a $24 billion data center at the site along with a new gas-fired power plant for a new data center to be located in Starbase. The request was granted, enabling the project to skirt around the Brownsville moratorium.

Starbase city officials are scheduled to conduct a public hearing on the new data center on December 9, where presumably they will decide whether or not the project can make due with gas generators until a full gas power plant with appropriate pollution controls can materialize.

Over and above the rocket fuel issue, grassroots activists, environmental organizations, and researchers have been raising the alarm over the impact of Starbase in Texas for years, tracking significant harm to local public recreation resources as well as wildlife habitats. Similar concerns have surfaced around SpaceX’s plans for Starbase, Louisiana.

Of course, nobody seriously expects the whole domestic rocket business to stop in its tracks. The issue is one of mitigating climate and biodiversity risks, developing projects that avoid pummeling vulnerable communities with additional public health risks, and investing in more sustainable options including alternative rocket fuels.

Keep an eye on the aviation industry for some hints at the future of sustainable propellants. Airline stakeholders have been focusing on SAF, generally meaning a bio-based equivalent of kerosene jet fuel. However, the going has been slow, and it’s even slower in the field of propellants. The European Space Agency, for example, has been exploring green propellants for space operations for 25 years, with little to show for its efforts.

More recently, though, aviation stakeholders have begun turning to the emerging electrofuels or “solar fuels” industry, meaning fuels synthesized from captured carbon and green hydrogen, with solar and other renewables powering the operation. The green hydrogen industry has been struggling for traction, but signs of scaleup have finally emerged, with e-kerosene jet fuel in play along with green hydrogen. Honda, for one, is envisioning a fleet of reusable rockets propelled by renewable fuels, and at least two Chinese firms are also working the reusable rocket angle.

#5709. Sonnen, SOLRITE, & ADAPT Energy, Integrating VPP Breakers to Turn Home Loads into Flexible Grid Assets

SOLRITE Unveils Next-Gen Virtual Power Plant Node with sonnen and ADAPT Energy, Integrating VPP Breakers to Turn Home Loads into Flexible Grid Assets

ADAPT Energy’s control of VPP breakers will add dispatchable load flexibility to the SOLRITE sonnen VPP, while paving a simpler, faster, and lower-cost path to whole-home backup

SOLRITE, a company specializing in developing, financing, and owning Virtual Power Plant Agreement (VPA) projects, together with Sonnen, a leader in Virtual Power Plant energy storage technology, announced they will now digitally integrate VPP smart breakers, managed by ADAPT Energy, into the SOLRITE  sonnen VPP. The integration marks the next evolution of sonnen and SOLRITE’s industry-leading partnership in VPP innovation, expanding beyond battery storage by transforming residential electrical loads into flexible, dispatchable grid assets for both the VPP and smart backup power applications.

Unlike traditional approaches to whole-home backup, often requiring hard-wired circuit relocation, including exclusive loads panels, the newly expanded VPP Node combines the sonnenHub with ADAPT Energy’s VPP smart breaker technology to intelligently manage major household loads. This creates a more streamlined approach to whole-home backup while unlocking an entirely new source of flexibility for the SOLRITE sonnen VPP. With homeowner permission, the VPP smart breaker—controlled by ADAPT Energy—can also participate in on-grid VPP events, enabling the same electrical infrastructure to be flexible for both backup and grid participation.

“Our work with ADAPT Energy is a meaningful step forward in how we think about residential battery storage and VPP participation,” said Regan George, CEO and Founder of SOLRITE Energy. “We have already demonstrated the tremendous value of connecting sonnen batteries together as a pooled grid asset. Now, we’re going inside the home and unlocking another layer of flexibility that has been sitting there all along. By combining the battery with additional load management made possible by the VPP smart breaker technology, we’re creating a more valuable node for the grid while also giving homeowners a clever backup solution.”

The integration addresses one of the longstanding challenges of whole-home backup: how to provide broad electrical coverage without having large household loads drain available battery capacity too quickly or cause the nano-grid to immediately fail after an off-grid transition due to excessive inrush current. With the newly expanded VPP smart breaker technology digitally integrated with the sonnen ecosystem, the system can intelligently shed large loads without requiring those circuits to be relocated. ADAPT Energy keeps the smart breakers synchronized with the sonnen battery during backup mode and automatically restores the breakers when grid power returns. This significantly reduces installation complexity and tedious inspections for installers, allowing crews to complete more jobs per week.

For homeowners, the system provides the convenience of a whole-home electrical configuration that intelligently manages the loads that have the greatest impact on backup duration. This work is all done in the background, meaning homeowners do not have to worry about resetting individual circuits during an outage. Instead, participating homes will see more value from their VPA program in the form of an enhanced backup power experience. Furthermore, at any time in the future, if a homeowner is interested in altering which of their VPP smart breaker-controlled loads are shed during an outage, the process to make such a change is quick and easy, requiring no electrical reconfiguration.

“This is about making whole-home backup work the way homeowners expect it to,” said Troy Morgan, President of PanTech Design, the developer of ADAPT Energy. “Instead of putting the pressure on homeowners to change the way their home is wired and to manually manipulate loads during an outage, we’re putting intelligence into their energy system, which provides a seamless and automatic backup power experience for their home. Our technology empowers home loads to supply optimal backup power during an outage or provide valuable flexibility to the grid.”

The permission-based architecture aligns the interests of the homeowner, battery owner and utility. The real opportunity comes when homeowners choose to opt in to allow the breakers to participate in on-grid VPP events.

When a VPP event is dispatched, the sonnen battery can discharge or charge in concert with direct load shedding via ADAPT. ADAPT Energy will strictly shed pre-approved household loads, and by using Eaton AbleEdge breakers, ADAPT achieves this right where the breaker sits, without relocation. A customer who has opted to include a pool pump, for example, can have that load temporarily turned off while the sonnen battery is discharging to the grid. That way, the VPP can respond through both energy stored in the battery and energy that would have otherwise been consumed by the home, thereby increasing the dispatched injection into the grid, by the VPP.

“With the addition of ADAPT Energy’s VPP smart-breaker technology to the SOLRITE–sonnen ecosystem, we are expanding the virtual power plant from a battery-centered resource into a fully orchestrated home-energy platform,” said Blake Richetta, Chairman and CEO of sonnen Inc. “By synchronizing energy that has been harnessed in our battery with intelligent, real-time control of household electrical loads, we can deliver more flexible, responsive capacity to the grid precisely when it is needed most. This integration materially strengthens the value of SOLRITE’s virtual power plant—supporting grid reliability, creating greater customer value, and enabling SOLRITE to continue delivering industry-leading solar-plus-storage VPA solutions to homeowners.”

The PJM Interconnection proposed revamping its interconnection process by studying large load and generation interconnection requests in a single, one-year review.

Most major U.S. grid operators have now submitted their highly anticipated proposals for interconnecting data centers and other large loads to the grid in response to directives issued by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission.

FERC in June issued “show cause” orders saying that it appeared that regional transmission organizations and others lacked adequate rules for adding large loads to the grid. FERC asked them to propose reforms to address the agency’s concerns or to explain why their existing rules were just and reasonable.

#5687. A Radically Different Approach to AI

About forty years ago, I decided I would develop artificial intelligence in my spare time. I knew about the Turing test, and I believed that a computer tricking people into thinking it was human did not indicate intelligence. There was a program called ELIZA that did that already, and it was clearly not intelligent at all.

I wanted to produce real AI, a type of thought that might take some effort for a human being to figure out. Maybe I would have to ask the computer how it reached the conclusion it did. But it would do so using logic a human could follow, examining each step. It would be a process a person could examine.

My goal, I believed, would best be reached by first inventing something I called Artificial Cognition. This would be a native way for computers to deal with data in the form of bit-fields, which were native data for computers, as translations of human words, in the same way that computers use IEEE bitwise representations of real numbers. Once this step was done, it would be easy to string words together in sentences, which could be directly translated into and out of human language. And the sentences would tell computers things they could “understand.”

With such translations, a computer could translate a work, such as Shakespeare’s Henry V, into its own language. Then it could translate the text into whatever language we might want. Some subtleties would be lost, but human translators do the same thing.

My idea was that computers should be able to work on ideas natively. At first, I thought this would require a very wide CPU, with registers that might be 256 bits, or even wider. In time, I came to believe that it would be best to stick to CPUs that were in use. As 64-bit CPUs were clearly coming soon, when I was working on this in 1986, I decided to use that data size. I also thought I might have to design a CPU with an additional instruction set, but I never got to the point of needing one.

So my design goal ended up having a word to translate almost any word (or even phrases) of English, occupying 64 bits of memory. And these could be understood and manipulated in standard registers of a standard computer.

I believe that I did achieve that goal, though the project never went into commercial use. A patent on the final design was issued by the US PTO, but that was long ago, and as I understand it, the whole thing is now in the public domain.

My reason for writing about this is that AI is a very contentious issue today. Anthropic has even warned potential stockholders in its IPO documents that its product could have catastrophic effects if things go wrong, possibly to the point of terminating all human life. I think it even points out that a major part of the value of its stock would be lost if there were no human beings left to buy it.

So I started to work on what I called Artificial Cognition in about 1986. Doing this in my spare time was a long slog. I went through a bunch of design iterations, starting, as I said, with 256-bit words. I won’t go through the work that had to be done determining how many designs there were and why each would not work. Each attempt took about a year or more, but eventually I got to the point of having a design in my fifth try.

I will give a simple explanation of the design in this article. Before I do, I will describe what the design could do at the time the patent was applied for, over twenty years ago. To reiterate, it could do these things:

Eventually, I defined about 10,000 words for this design. Doing this was a full-time project that took two years, during which I also tested the design. At a point where I had about 2,500 words, I built a simple program that took two inputs of English words, translated them into the computer words, compared them to see that they were valid for compares, and held them for the record. Then it went through the entire vocabulary, taking each word (or set of words) in turn and finding any word that compared to it in precisely the same way the first two words compared.

This might sound like it did far more work than it needed to do. Because most words are not comparable, most compares produced faults and were rejected immediately. So the process did not have to do the millions of compares one might imagine. This was even easier than one might suppose, because whole huge sets of words could be rejected together. So if the input words were a pair like “train” and “airplane,” all words for colors or pieces of clothing could be rejected as sets.

One thing about this that is interesting is how it is done in the computer CPU. With a pair of 64-bit registers loaded, an XOR instruction will result in the difference between the two, or it will produce a fault. The XOR requires a single clock cycle. Then, with the word from the dictionary loaded into a register, a single XOR with the difference produces the word that gives the analogy, or it will produce a fault.

If there is a fault, we go on to the next word in the vocabulary. Otherwise, we have an analogy. In practice, for example, I ran this using original inputs of “car” and “small passenger plane.” The results came back, including the examples following, for “as a car is to a small passenger plane” (please note that each set of words in quotes is a single 64-bit word for the computer):

There were many other results, but the combination of lion and eagle stood out so clearly that I looked specifically to see what the comparison entailed. Why “lion” and “eagle,” but not “tiger” and “owl,” or “house cat” and “hawk”? There were about half a dozen predatory land animals and at least four predatory birds in that small dictionary, but “lion” and “eagle” were the only pair returned by the program. What gives?

It turned out that things had gone precisely the way they should have. The reason that “lion” and “eagle” were chosen while others were not is that the definitions of these two had connotative values as symbols of authority. That information was in their 64-bit definitions. You can do a lot with 64 bits, if you know how.

This work was done in a 32-bit machine, simulating the 64-bit machines just about to come to market. With registers loaded (in a 64-bit machine), each compare would take one clock cycle. So the whole thing, with computers pre-loaded, would take two clocks. In a 2-GHz machine, the set of compares took one billionth of a second. One billionth of a second in a 64-bit machine anyone could buy. One billionth of a second in a Raspberry Pi costing $45.

AI companies are bringing products to market that they warn could end the lives of all human beings. Anthropic says its IPO, which comes out on Thursday, will establish the value of the company at $1.5 trillion (NOTE: with a “T”). But Anthropic is warning that a risk for stockholders is that it could kill all humanity, including you, and your entire family, even if you live in a modest home and get no benefit from the company in any way. And everyone else could go with you. That, I read, is what the IPO papers say, but some people believe it could be very worthwhile to do.

I believe this is not artificial intelligence. It might be artificial neglect. Maybe even artificial willful ignorance. Maybe, with artificial rational thought, we could produce artificial due diligence, but AI is not known for rationality.

I wonder how high the value of Anthropic stock will go if only computers could buy it because there were no people left. Of course, without a Fed made up of humans, they might figure out a way to have infinite money to play with.

The iteration of my invention that works depends on 64 bits being divided into categories, each represented by 8 bits. These are organized into eight supersets (3 bits), each with four groups of subsets (2 bits), with each group having eight basic subsets (3 bits). The total is 8 bits per set value, so there are 256 categories.

Of the eight supersets in each category, one is “agent.” This is anything that does anything. It is divided into four groups of subsets, two of which are “living thing” and “mechanism.” These are divided by type into subsets, so “living thing” includes “animal” and “plant,” among others.

The category “location and time” is similar. This is divided into groups of subsets, one of which is “geography.” Within this are eight subsets, among which are “air,” “land,” “water,” “space,” and four others.

We can see the basic way the vocabulary works by taking these for the most significant 8 bits in a word, the field I call “BAS,” and the next most significant field, which I call “ALT,” because I seem to be unable to come up with a better 3-letter name for it. (Sorry, I do get a bit dull sometimes. Often.)

Using just these two fields, we can see that a car, a train, a taxi, a bus, and a bicycle are all in a group, “land vehicle” (for ALT and BAS). An airplane of any type is an “air vehicle.” So are a blimp and a kite carrying prayers to the clouds asking them to make rain.

Similarly, a lion, tiger, bear, or dog would be a “land animal,” and most birds are “air animal.” Please note that I say “most birds.” They are not divided into biological groups, but according to where they function. Some birds only function on land. Similarly, a bat is an “air animal.” And Melville was right, Moby Dick was a “water animal,” just like any fish.

This broadly divides our dictionary into potential word values using 16 bits. This would limit us to just 65,536 potential words, if they were 16 bits long. Fortunately, we are only a quarter of the way through the 64 bits of our potential word definitions.

The next parts of the definition use 8 bits for each of several fields. A SRC field could represent where something comes from, why it has come into existence, or even what fuels it. For a car, this could be a value that decodes as “chemical,” or a value meaning “electricity.” For a sail-powered car, this field might mean “air.” For just “car,” with no motive force specified, this would be left to the value 0, meaning “any” or “whatever.”

An MTD field represents how it is operated. For a car, this could be decoded by a word meaning “engine” or “motor.” That is, unless the car is powered by sail, in which case neither of these values would work, but something other than 0 would be used, because 0 could mean a superset that engines and motors would match to. In the case of a buggy, this field would say “animal.”

A DST field may mean a destination or purpose. For a car, this could be a value meaning “human being,” for what it carries.

In addition to these, there is a CXT field, which can carry context. In the case of a lion or eagle, this is a value meaning “authority,” which is why they matched to each other but nothing else in the test.

So far, I have described six fields. They provide us with far more potential words than any human language has by a huge factor. That should be enough, but there are other uses for bits. For example, I decided some time ago that there should be eight bits assigned to negation bits, each corresponding to a single field in the word.

Clearly, the values that make up the fields in the word are very fuzzy in their meanings. However, in the design, applying how these are to be interpreted has to be done strictly according to the sets of words involved. Implementing such a design means rules have to be developed according what part of the vocabulary is being worked on.

Altogether, I had a bit over 10,000 words defined about twenty years ago. I believed that these could be built up just a bit for software that could read a dictionary. As it happened, no one was willing to look at the invention to evaluate it, so funding of my invention ceased.

Before I finish up, I should mention that this sort of AI does not do images or other types of jobs that require pattern matching. That isn’t what I set out to do. I always assumed that such things would come from people interested in them. But I wasn’t interested.

I have a problem with the AI being produced commercially today. All of it looks to me like it is based on the same sort of pattern matching that is done by a human mind to some extent. And this is a problem because human beings are not naturally rational creatures. So AI companies are basically building irrational products.

As for Anthropic and the other organizations that claim to have products that threaten human life, that looks like artificial insanity requiring artificial restraint. Or artificial constraint. At least until someone can develop artificial ethics and artificial caution. I don’t think Anthropic has that yet. But I think there is a way to do it.

Batteries are often described as a foundational solution because they respond instantly, start and stop without thermal plants’ mechanical constraints, and uniquely shift energy from off-peak to on-peak periods. That mandate expanded the range of products storage can provide, with more options for revenue production.

In December 2025, ERCOT implemented Real-Time Co-optimization Plus Batteries (RTC+B), which co-optimizes energy and reserves in real time while accounting for what battery resources can actually deliver. One month later, during Winter Storm Fern, ERCOT credited RTC+B with helping redistribute ancillary services across the system during a localized transmission emergency. The same change that helped ERCOT lean on batteries during Fern also changed how those batteries earn revenue. As these rules open new ways for storage to participate, the mix of market products that pay off keeps shifting.

#5665. The grid will fall short of AI’s power needs. These 6 resources can close the gap.

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

No single option is fast, firm and large enough on its own, writes Simon Irish, CEO of Terrestrial Energy.

Simon Irish is CEO of Terrestrial Energy, a developer of small modular nuclear reactors.

Most planning for artificial intelligence’s electricity demand starts with a forecast and asks whether it is right.

I would start somewhere else: Assume the grid will fall short, then ask how to close the gap — and how fast.

The evidence supports that starting point. McKinsey & Company estimates the U.S. will need 30 GW to 55 GW of additional capacity by 2030 as data center demand grows and aging plants retire. PJM Interconnection, the largest U.S. grid operator, has already seen the impacts of this trend. Its latest capacity auction fell about 6.6 GW short of its reliability requirement and cleared at the price cap.

Forecasts can be wrong, and requested load can be speculative or duplicative. But the PJM result is a market outcome, not a forecast. The useful question is which tools can close the gap, by when and at what cost.

The first four options below address serving data centers through the grid. The final two consider generation at or near the data center, with an optional grid connection. These categories overlap; on-site generation is a delivery strategy, not a generation technology, and can use multiple technologies.

Flexible demand. Duke University’s Nicholas Institute estimates the grid could absorb 76 GW of new load if that load accepted curtailment averaging 0.25% of annual consumption, in events of about 1.7 hours. It is the fastest option because it needs no new generation. It makes better use of capacity already on the grid.

But that capacity is finite, and beyond it, further load growth requires new supply. There is also a practical hurdle; to date, many data centers have shown little willingness to curtail AI workloads, because the value of computing far exceeds the cost of power.

Existing nuclear plants. Increasing output at operating plants through uprates and restarting closed plants can add reliable electricity supply, while extending operating licenses keeps existing plants running. A U.S. Department of Energy initiative targets 5 GW of added capacity at existing nuclear plants by 2029, and DOE has closed a $1.9 billion loan to restart Iowa’s Duane Arnold plant, returning 615 MW to service.

The limit is opportunity: Few operating plants remain candidates for significant uprates, and restarts are underway at nearly every plant where one is practical.

Natural gas. Gas offers firm power and a familiar path, but gas turbine supply has become the constraint. GE Vernova’s gas turbine backlog and slot reservations reached 116 GW at midyear 2026, and the company has said a heavy-duty turbine ordered today will not arrive until 2031. Buyers also take on fuel-price exposure and decades of emissions.

Wind, solar and storage. These resources can be deployed quickly and at scale. Their limit is that data centers require dependable power around the clock, so matching that requirement takes substantial overbuilding, energy storage and transmission infrastructure to deliver the same capacity as a firm resource.

On-site generation. Building a power plant at a data center can bring electricity online sooner by avoiding some grid upgrades and connection delays, where permitted.

However, it does not speed up equipment deliveries. An on-site gas turbine plant faces the same turbine shortages as one supplying the grid. Reliable operation also requires sufficient on-site backup capability or access to grid power during outages and maintenance. If a project relies on the grid, developers need clear terms defining that access and its cost.

Advanced nuclear energy. Advanced reactors offer firm, clean power and can be built as a fleet. They can serve data centers directly, with an optional grid connection. They will not close the gap this decade, and their real test is cost at fleet scale.

No single option is fast, firm and large enough on its own. Three principles follow.

Flexible demand, uprates at existing nuclear plants, and new wind, solar and storage can help meet near-term electricity needs.

Natural gas and on-site generation can help fill the gap while longer-term resources are developed, but only if equipment deliveries, permits and grid interconnection align with the data center’s schedule.

Advanced nuclear energy can contribute in the 2030s and beyond.

Utilities must plan now for both natural gas and advanced nuclear energy. DOE’s long-term modeling indicates 550 to 770 GW of additional firm, clean generating capacity could be needed by 2050, with advanced nuclear energy providing about 200 GW.

Infrastructure built to meet an immediate shortfall should also have a useful role later. A gas plant could provide firming capacity or operate during periods of peak demand as longer-term supply becomes available.

Make flexibility the price of speed. Utilities can offer earlier interconnection to customers accepting curtailment under defined, metered and enforceable terms. For data center operators, that is a choice between some power sooner and firm power later. Customers that can curtail load may get power sooner; those that cannot must wait for firm supply. This converts flexibility from an assumption into a contract and rewards developers whose flexibility is real.

Secure the long-lead items for firm, clean power now. Buyers who reserved gas turbine production slots early secured earlier deliveries; those ordering now may have to wait until 2031.

Firm, clean power has its own long-lead items, including fuel supply and equipment manufacturing capacity. Utilities and data center operators can sign reservation or offtake agreements that give suppliers the demand signal to build capacity before it is needed.

These principles also address the forecast debate. Some investments — flexibility, uprates and transmission — remain valuable across demand scenarios. Others are options worth buying, such as reservations for firm supply in the 2030s and beyond.

And some are bets to avoid: irreversible commitments sized to a demand number that may not arrive. If forecasts prove too high, the cost is the price paid to preserve an option. If they prove right and no one bought the option, the cost is the kind of shortfall PJM has already seen.

Planning for a shortfall is not pessimism. Of all the inputs AI needs, reliable power may prove the scarcest. The race to scale AI is a race to deliver power. Utilities, regulators and developers need tools that arrive on time, hold their value if forecasts change, and keep electricity reliable and affordable for existing customers.

#5658. BMW In South Carolina: A Free Trade Success Story

I recently attended BMW’s “Beyond The Machine” event in South Carolina. The largest facility of the German company globally is Plant Spartanburg in Greer, South Carolina. As part of the event, I saw the opening of BMW’s new battery plant in nearby Woodruff. We stayed in the nearby city of Greenville, which was transformed by BMW and the businesses that followed.

Beyond the vehicles and manufacturing, which I plan to cover in more detail in future articles, one of the most striking aspects was the human impact. From the “Woodruffian” Mayor fighting back tears at the opening of the battery plant to the plant employees to the people I met exploring the town, they clearly appreciate what BMW has done for the community. Throughout the presentations and discussions, there was support for free trade along with the local benefits. People in the area pride themselves in their resilience. They have reason to be proud. And they were optimistic about the growth of the increasingly clean manufacturing and electrified products from this global company.

The area around Greenville, South Carolina the rose to prominence in the 1870s on the textile industry. Cotton from throughout the South that had previously been shipped to other areas for processing was processed in Greenville and Spartanburg counties. However, as global competition increased in the 20th century, the mills became less competitive. The last textile mill closed in Greenville in 1977, although a few textile operations continued in the region into the 1980’s. As that industry left, the local economy declined. If the story ended there, many might use it as an argument against free trade.

When BMW came to South Carolina in 1992, they saw a lot of potential. Michelin had already started making tires there in 1978. Local leaders were enthusiastic to work with BMW. The people were also enthusiastic to receive training for the new jobs. The deep-water port of Charleston on the other side of the state facilitated shipping, while nearby rail links connected to much of the US.

Since 1992, BMW has invested over $15.8 Billion. The new Woodruff battery plant is their most recent major investment. In the process, an area that could have been considered a casualty of global trade became one of its greatest beneficiaries.

The first model, an E36 3 series, rolled off the line in 1994. BMW also produced the Z3 in the plant, including the first “German” car driven by James Bond on camera. However, the big breakthrough came with the introduction of the X5 in 1999. At the time, relatively few SUVs were being sold in Europe. BMW Spartanburg as the center of SUV production was intending to largely for the US. However, global markets shifted.

Now, Spartanburg is the “Home of X.” It currently produces the X3, X5, X6, X7 and XM, including electrified versions. It is the sole manufacturing plant for a number of those SUV models globally. A little over half of their production is exported, which also makes it consistently the top US overseas automotive exporter. Export volume topped $9 Billion in 2025 over roughly 200,000 X vehicles.

BMW designed those vehicles to be able to use ICE, PHEV and BEV powertrains. While some may want them to make just pure BEV, the approach gives BMW the ability to shift rapidly with the market. The different models also give the plant a level of market resilience. However, to fuel that resilience they need a very flexible supply chain that can respond quickly. Suppliers being close to the plant creates greater resilience.

BMW is designing its vehicles to be globally competitive. While 400+ miles of EPA range and charging speeds approaching 500 kW may be at the top of the US market, they are also competitive in global markets. While BMW developed the cells and packs, they use multiple suppliers to manufacture the cylindrical cells to their specifications. Packs at the plant currently use AESC cells, but they will shift to LG in the future and use other suppliers in other markets.

BMW also worked with the state of South Carolina to develop people. Nearby Clemson University provides a source of engineering talent, and BMW partners with the university on the Clemson University International Center for Automotive Research, where both students and BMW benefit from the development of new innovations. They are also developing potential future suppliers with the BMW Venture funds. One such company will be supplying flax-based composites to future M models.

A heavy emphasis is placed on technical training at the factory. In addition, they collaborated with community colleges to provide technical training. But the development programs start much earlier, with a heavy emphasis in middle school and sponsoring the First Robotics Competition for elementary school students.

BMW also worked with the South Carolina Department of Transportation to build infrastructure to support the plant and its suppliers. Moving forward, that infrastructure will increasingly include electrification and EV charging. BMW also worked with the SC Ports Authority to develop an “inland port,” which is essentially a direct rail link to the Port of Charleston. By moving freight by rail, emissions are greatly reduced. In addition, a lot of emphasis is going into transferring freight quickly, as every minute a truck sits idling is also wasted emissions.

While BMW employs 11,000 workers, over four times as many jobs are supported by the plant, according to a Clemson economist. That multiplier is much higher than most companies due to how BMW operates. Part of the impact on the local economy beyond employee count comes from the strategy behind the manufacturing. Overall, more than 70% of the value of the vehicles comes from outside suppliers.

Plant Spartanburg receives 3.3 million parts per day delivered by 1200 truckloads and 50 sea containers, 6 days per week. All those parts then go into over 1500 vehicles produced per day. The plant is laid out a bit different than some other manufacturing plants to facilitate the supply chain. Multiple fingers go up off the main line, along which parts are delivered to be assembled nearby. There isn’t much in the way of warehousing. Trucks come to the plant each day and the parts can end up on vehicles driving off the line the same day.

In the initial stages of manufacturing, more of the supply base came from overseas. However, as the plant established itself, more suppliers localized. That growing supply chain then attracted additional companies to the region, like Mercedes, Volvo and Scout. Those companies then create greater scale for the suppliers, who can reduce costs and expand operations.

Overall, for heavy, bulky components, localization will tend to  happen naturally. For a company that relies on suppliers to consistently and almost constantly deliver parts, that localization tends to increase. The more localized the supply chain, the more capable it becomes to respond to market dynamics. Once scale is attained for heavy, bulky components like batteries, that production will naturally become more localized.

BMW has a growing supply chain footprint across the Americas and especially across the Southeastern US. There will be over 850 Tier 1 supplier locations in the Americas by 2027, with multiple times as many Tier 2 and Tier 3 suppliers providing materials, parts and components. BMW focuses on value creation, competitiveness and collaboration in their supply chain. In addition, while they are bringing economic benefit, they are also focused on decarbonization.

Of course, all this economic activity has brought “green” in terms of money to the local communities. As I was stranded in Greenville due to flight interruptions, I had time to walk around the town and speak to some of the locals. Streets that were once desolate and unsafe are now vibrant with shops and restaurants. An area that people once wanted to leave is now growing rapidly and attracting people from around the world. I started conversations  with several friendly people in town about trade. I heard stories of transformation from past struggles to current successes.

Even though it was clear that they were benefitting from the economic impact, I was still surprised how strongly and openly they supported free trade and realized the benefits. A local guide pointed out major buildings and businesses that were new. Downtown is now pleasant and walkable. Part of that is due to the restoration of a river that runs through the city, surrounded by shops and artwork, where I saw some locals fishing in the clear water. This gets to the other kind of “green.”

While there are a lot of trucks transporting materials and components, shortened travel distances and efforts to streamline intermodal transfers reduce emissions. The proximity enables packaging to typically be sent back to the supplier to be reused. BMW is also putting a lot of effort into reduced CO2 emissions steel produced locally, which I plan to follow up on. Over half the steel in their vehicles is reduced CO2. Solar panels and landfill gas provide power for their facilities.

As the company relies heavily on its supply chain, the supply chain is a major focus of its decarbonization strategy. While EVs reduce emissions overall, the proportion of emissions from the manufacturing phase tends to be higher than for ICE vehicles, adding emphasis to decarbonization of manufacturing. At the same time, they strive to create regional strength, resilience and value creation while reducing emissions. Close collaboration is the key to meeting these goals. BMW plans to have net zero CO2 emissions throughout their supply chain by 2050 at the latest.

The new Woodruff plant will produce their sixth-generation battery. BMW develops the batteries first and then outsources the cell manufacturing, but they will now be manufacturing the packs locally. Initially, this will be for the iX5, to be delivered domestically and to global markets. However, additional EV models may benefit in the future.

Overall, politicians in the 1970’s could have tried to block global competition to protect the textile mills. That might have extended the life of those plants for a few years. However, in the process, those plants would become less globally competitive and less globally relevant. Protectionism would have only extended a slow, painful decline.

However, by keeping the market open and staying open to new business opportunities, Greenville, Greer, Spartanburg and Woodruff created the circumstances for their current success. People were open to new companies from other countries. They were open to industries that were new to the area. And they were open to investing in retraining to better take advantage of the new opportunities.

In the initial site selection, South Carolina could have imposed strict regulations and tried to force localization before the factory was built, production was ramped up and scale was attained to support that localization. If that happened, the plant would have undoubtedly been built elsewhere, and the economic benefits would have gone to someone else. By taking a more collaborative approach, rather than being confrontational, both BMW and local suppliers benefitted greatly. Building on that approach, more companies came to the region, building scale and amplifying the benefits.

BMW is not being hypocritical in their support of free trade. They are not promoting free trade just when it benefits them and then putting up barriers for other competitors. They are not shying away from global competition. They are seeking to make globally competitive vehicles for global markets. While global competition is fierce, by facing that competition, rather than hiding from it, their products become more competitive.

In the process, one of the brightest spots in US automotive trade globally, as well as one of the leaders in electrified product and supply chain decarbonization, comes from a German company.

Change and globalization can be scary things. Especially in the short term. The transition to EVs can also be scary, especially for those tied to the past. However, hiding behind political and trade walls rarely works out in the long run. A willingness to be open and collaborative with people who might come from within different lines on a map or sound different than you can have long term benefits. And the courage to engage with the technology transition and compete globally can put you ahead of many established competitors. That cannot only transform how we power our vehicles, but also transform the lives of people in communities for generations to come. As Woodruff Mayor Kenneth Gist put it at the battery plant opening:

BMW and the City of Woodruff wanted the same thing. Success in business, success in our community, and success in building the next generation of BMW electric vehicles to be the best in the world…

…When I think about this plant, I think about our children, our grandchildren. Educational partnership that will open the doors for them, the career that will be waiting for them, and a Woodruff they would be proud to call home for decades to come. BMW, you have been a true partner to me personally, to every person in this community.

That wasn't always the case, though; back in the day, an automaker's option sheet let a buyer craft a car to fit their needs, but within limits. At General Motors, that option list has long been denoted by three letters: RPO. They stand for "Regular Production Option," and have given rise to many package codes denoting now-venerated performance options like the WS6 group on this Trans-Am.

First introduced in 1978, the WS6 Special Performance Package was originally positioned as a handling upgrade. The package included snowflake alloy wheels fitted with fatter-than-stock tires and a rear anti-roll bar. Later editions added four-wheel disc brakes. For our car, WS6 still means bigger wheels and tires and more aggressive suspension tuning, but it notably adds a ram-air induction intake fed by two functional nostrils fronting the car's blistered hood.

That ram-air induction feeds an LT1 V8, kindly provided by the capable engine builders over at Chevrolet. This was the last year for the LT1, as Pontiac introduced the LS1 the following year. That engine had previously been exclusive to the Corvette. With the big gulp air intake, the 350 CID LT1 still puts out a very healthy 305 hp and 335 pound-feet of torque, significant increases over the standard engine's 285 hp and 325 lb-ft.

Here, that mill is mated to a 4L60E four-speed automatic, something the seller touts in the ad, claiming this to be one of only 347 auto-equipped WS6 cars produced this year. The ad also boasts that "Everything works like it came off the showroom floor."

It's not all stock, though. The ad claims a replacement quad-tip exhaust with Flowmaster mufflers, new poly suspension bushings, and KYB shocks as notable updates. There are 79,778 miles on the clock, a modest number, but still called out in the ad, with the seller explaining, "While it has changed hands among enthusiasts, the low 79k mileage and remarkable preservation proves it has spent its life as a garage-kept weekend toy rather than a daily driver."

Back in 1997, a corral of 300 horses was a lot. Today, even a four-cylinder Mustang offers more than that and will easily outdo this Trans-Am in any dimension of performance. That doesn't mean this cool red-over-white convertible isn't worth a look. First off, when did you last see a classic Trans-Am like this? I bet it was at a Cars & Coffee or other enthusiast enclave. That makes it interesting simply because of its uniqueness. Then there are the pop-up headlamps. We love those guys! This is also, as the ad touts, an incredibly well-maintained example of the breed, needing nothing but the next owner to agree not to mess it up.

Yes, the interior is gawd-awful, with miserable hard plastic surfaces, wide gaps between panels, and buttons that look like they came straight from the bargain bin at Temu, but that's just how Pontiac (and pretty much all of GM) rolled in this era. At least the seats look comfortable as all get out, and the car has all the niceties one could want, including manual climate, cruise, power windows, locks and top, and a CD stereo. As an added incentive, the title is as clean as the car.

Okay, so if anyone is in the market for a last-of-the-Mohicans Trans-Am, this is a strong candidate. Yes, later cars with the LS do perform better and have more aggressive styling, if that's your thing. On the other hand, they also tend to command substantially higher prices. This car's $13,000 asking price might make up for that performance gap.

#5654. 2027 Subaru Forester Wilderness Hybrid Is The Happy Wanderer

Making things even sweeter is the fact that choosing the hybrid only adds $1,000 to the Forester Wilderness' MSRP, a delta that Subaru says will be overcome in just 2 years, or about 30,000 miles.

They're fed by a 1.1-kWh battery that lives where the spare tire once was, which may be disappointing to some off-road enthusiasts who also love busting tires. Either way you slice it, the numbers are stronger than the 178 lb-ft of torque from the normal Forester Wilderness. In typical Subaru fashion, the symmetrical all-wheel-drive system sends internal-combustion power to all four wheels — unlike the RAV4, which has no mechanical connection between the front and rear wheels — and that makes a huge difference off-road.

Gas mileage has never been a Subie strong suit, but the hybrid's fuel economy is also much improved to go along with its added performance. According to the EPA, the Forester Wilderness Hybrid will get 33 mpg city, 29 mpg highway and 31 mpg combined, improvements of 9 mpg, 1 mpg and 5 mpg, respectively. The EPA-expected driving range is also bumped up from 432 miles to 515 miles. Happy wanderer, indeed.

The Forester Wilderness' lovably ugly looks are still present, and the hybrid maintains the same 9.3 inches of ground clearance as its gas-only counterpart. Hill descent control and Subaru's X-Mode system with Snow/Dirt and Deep Snow/Mud settings mean it's pretty much impossible to get this thing stuck; those two modes can basically be summed up as stuck and absolutely biffed. The majority of the time I drove the Forester Wilderness Hybrid was without any pavement below me on the seemingly endless acreage of Rancho Quien Sabe, and I've got to say, it was nearly boring in the way the SUV saw any obstacle in front of it and just traversed it without fuss or hesitation. The Forester happily trudged along, unbothered by what sort of terrain it was forced to deal with. Aiding in that was the 180-degree front view monitor that helped me see where I was going, but while it was undoubtedly helpful, I can't help but think the resolution left a bit to be desired.

Outside of a few optional paint colors, there aren't any packages you'll be able to add to the Wilderness Hybrid. What you see is what you get. Nice and easy. And as I mentioned before, it's just $1,000 more than the standard Forester Wilderness. There's a hard deal to beat.

The Forester Hybrid Wilderness has a real can-do attitude to it. It's a real billy goat of a car — one that takes a licking and keeps on kicking, happily coming back for more. When driving, it was as if the Forester was whistling a little tune to itself as I mashed the throttle, forcing it to traverse up a hill a compact crossover has no business going up.

No, it doesn't have the best infotainment in the world and it's not a classically "beautiful" vehicle, but it has something most cars don't: character. The Forester Wilderness Hybrid wants to get lost with you, and all you need to do is make sure you've got the time to get lost with it, because with over 500 miles of driving range, you can get lost for a long, long time.
