#7079. Colleges report declines in international enrollment amid federal restrictions

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Survey data released Wednesday found that nearly 6 in 10 U.S. colleges lost foreign graduate students this fall, and 45% saw a decline in undergraduates.

Wednesday’s survey, released by NAFSA: Association of International Educators in partnership with the Oxford University Press and the student recruitment platform Studyportals, reinforces concerns that the Trump administration's attacks on foreign students and the long-standing U.S. programs that support their study here would corrode the international enrollment pipeline.

Survey respondents cited the administration’s crackdown on student visas and the astronomical new fees sought for the H-1B visa and Optional Practical Training programs. While some of those policies are being challenged in the courts, they can signal to international students that they are unwelcome in the U.S., and the uncertainty and instability can ward off prospective applicants.

Major public research universities, traditionally popular among foreign students, have begun reporting international enrollment declines this fall. And some, like Pennsylvania State University, have drawn a direct line between that loss of students and budgetary challenges.

“These survey results affirm what we’ve observed before: national policies matter to international students making major decisions about where to study, conduct research, or start a career,” Fanta Aw, NAFSA’s executive director and CEO, said in a statement Wednesday.

Some colleges also reported decreased interest in countries that have historically sent the most higher education students overseas, such as China and India.

“Our agents in India in particular are telling us that they are seeing a drastic decline in inquiries for US graduate study and that many prospective students are either considering other destinations or postponing study plans,” one university respondent said.

Of those surveyed, 37% of U.S. colleges said they planned to diversify the locations where they recruit from and tap into new markets over the next 12 months.

One institution said it would pivot to the Philippines, Canada and Colombia as a means of offsetting losses from India, Bangladesh and Nepal, the three countries from which the college traditionally got the most foreign students.

Another 32% said they would expand their online offerings, and 30% said they would turn to budget cuts.

However, cuts could further exacerbate challenges for colleges’ international admissions offices. One respondent admitted that staffing issues have “resulted in a lack of attention to incoming students.”

Pollsters surveyed colleges’ senior administrators and international student recruitment professionals online between Oct. 5 and 26.

Structured course design creates consistency even when learners progress at different rates. Clear learning objectives, sequenced content, robust and embedded practice opportunities and aligned assessments provide a support system to help students succeed and meet high academic expectations.

To improve student outcomes, state leaders should investigate gaps between their policies and how they are implemented — including systems for measuring their progress and accountability performance. The analysis found that only 13 states have clear, specific, statewide goals for student learning that is messaged across multiple documents and webpages, said Waite.

South Carolina, for instance, has set a goal for 75% of its students to perform at grade level by 2030. "A clear goal is the first piece of a clear strategy," Waite said. The report found that states' academic achievement spanned the political spectrum, even though a "popular narrative" is that blue states outperform the others.

#7048. Lawsuit alleges chronic underfunding of University of Maryland Eastern Shore

A group of students, faculty and alumni said the historically Black institution has been discriminated against and shortchanged $6 billion since its founding.

The plaintiffs are two UMES students and a corporate entity created last year called ForUMES, which, according to the complaint, is made up of university faculty, students, alumni and prospects and is aimed at bolstering the “educational, research, public service, and charitable objectives for UMES.”

The plaintiffs described UMES as “the woefully underfunded stepchild of the University System of Maryland.”

The complaint traces the funding disparity back to 1890, when Maryland wanted to take advantage of the federal Second Morrill Act.

That law sought to bolster the country’s land-grant universities with additional annual funding. But the money wasn’t available for states whose public land-grant institutions had racially discriminatory admissions unless they had a comparable college “of like character” for Black students, per the statute.

According to the lawsuit, Henry Alvord — who was then the president of University of Maryland, College Park, the state’s first land-grant institution — was “in a dilemma.”

The university “was in desperate need of additional funding but the idea of permitting Black students to attend [UMD] was unfathomable because his and many others’ racist beliefs would never allow [UMD] to be integrated,” the lawsuit states.

Alvord eventually found a solution to his dilemma in the Princess Anne Academy, a “small, remote and run-down preparatory school on the southern point of the eastern shore” that could — on paper — become a land-grant college for Black students, operated by Morgan College, which later became the HBCU Morgan State University.

However, the “lion’s share” of the new funding under the Second Morrill Act went to UMD, which did not admit Black students until a 1950 lawsuit.

“Black students would be sent to far-off Princess Anne to receive junior and senior high school-level classes on Princess Anne Academy’s dilapidated campus instead of a school of ‘like character’ to [UMD]’s state-of-the-art college,” plaintiffs said in the complaint.

According to the lawsuit, the underfunding and neglect of the academy — which later became UMES — continued throughout the 20th century and amid regular federal scrutiny of segregation in Maryland’s higher educational system and disparately low funding for UMES.

In 2023, then-President Joe Biden’s secretaries of education and agriculture wrote to Maryland Gov. Wes Moore — who is named as a defendant in the new lawsuit along with other top officials — to address the historical funding disparities at UMES as part of a broader effort to encourage more funding at historically Black land-grant colleges across the country.

“The longstanding and ongoing underinvestment in University of Maryland Eastern Shore disadvantages the students, faculty, and community that the institution serves,” the Biden officials wrote. “Furthermore, it may contribute to a lack of economic activity that would ultimately benefit Maryland.”

They calculated that UMES had been underfunded to the tune of $321.2 million just over the prior 30 years before the letter.

“These funds could have supported infrastructure and student services and would have better positioned the university to compete for research grants,” they said, while also acknowledging that “your state budget has taken steps to address this historic underinvestment.”

Since 2023, UMES has received around $9 million a year in additional state funding through a landmark Maryland law passed in 2021 aimed at expanding support for the state’s HBCUs and settling a lawsuit brought by the institutions’ supporters.

Enrollment has also been on the increase at the university. In fall 2024, UMES had 3,177 students, up 10.1% from five years prior, according to federal data.

The lawsuit’s plaintiffs don’t say how they calculated the $6 billion in damages they are seeking for UMES. In addition to monetary reparation for the years of underfunding, they asked the judge to require state officials to allocate land-grant funding equitably between UMD and UMES.

#7034. New Industry Alliance to Work Toward Common Security Model for AI Agents

Okta has announced a coalition bringing together major cloud, security, data and application vendors to create a shared architecture for securing AI agents across enterprise environments.

The 12 founding members of the new Blueprint Alliance are AWS, CrowdStrike, Databricks, Docker, Google Cloud, Lovable, Okta, Proofpoint, Salesforce, ServiceNow, Wiz and Zscaler. GE Appliances and World Central Kitchen are participating as strategic advisors.

The effort expands an earlier Okta framework into an open, multi-vendor reference architecture intended to address a basic problem with enterprise AI agents: An agent can be created in one system, authenticated through another, access tools and data from several others, and be monitored by yet another security platform.

The Alliance is focused less on how agents are built and more on how organizations can govern them consistently across vendor boundaries.

The Blueprint Alliance architecture organizes agent security around four questions: Where are my agents? What can they do? What are they doing? How do I respond?

The reference architecture also covers development-related systems. Its whitepaper includes agent frameworks, code repositories, CI/CD environments, code assistants, tool registries, Model Context Protocol servers, and command-line tools among the components that may need to be discovered or governed.

That makes the initiative relevant to development teams deploying agents that interact with internal APIs, MCP tools, databases, SaaS applications, or other agents. The architecture calls for those connections and permissions to remain visible and controllable as agents move from development into production.

A major part of the Alliance's work will be testing how security information and controls can move between products from different vendors.

The initial architecture references several existing standards and protocols, including MCP for agent-to-tool interactions, the Open Cybersecurity Schema Framework for security telemetry, and the Shared Signals Framework and Continuous Access Evaluation Profile for exchanging risk information.

The goal is for one part of an enterprise stack to be able to detect a risk condition and pass that information to another control point that can act on it. Depending on the implementation, that could include revoking access or containing an agent whose behavior has changed.

The Alliance says members will build and test reference integrations and publish results as interoperability work progresses. Specific cross-vendor implementations and performance results were not documented in the launch announcement.

Founding member Lovable also published a developer-oriented explanation of its participation, describing organizations that may have internally built agents, agents imported through SaaS platforms and locally running agents that all need to be inventoried and governed under a common model.

The Alliance's whitepaper recommends that organizations begin with foundational capabilities such as unified logging, telemetry, and agent discovery rather than attempting to deploy every control at once.

The architecture is available for community use, and the Alliance says it will continue evolving as members test integrations and additional participants become involved. Timelines for individual vendor integrations have not been published.

#7009. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

#6991. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

#6908. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

#6900. Report: Cyber Criminals Move Toward More Targeted Phishing Attacks

Cyber criminals are moving away from mass phishing scams and focusing on more targeted attacks, increasingly using trusted websites and services to trick victims, according to a new report from KnowBe4. The security firm found that spear-phishing attacks increased 41% over the past year.

The company's Phishing Threat Trends Report, Vol. 8 found that overall phishing volume increased 29.8% year over year. Organizations received an average of one dangerous e-mail for every 733 messages, but the bigger concern is how attackers are changing their approach.

Rather than sending the same fraudulent message to thousands of people, criminals are spending more time researching their targets and creating convincing messages that appear to come from trusted coworkers, businesses, and technology platforms.

The findings are based on threat data from KnowBe4 Defend, the company's inbound e-mail security platform, which processes approximately 1.15 billion event signals daily.

KnowBe4 found that several traditional phishing methods declined significantly compared with 2025. Mobile-focused scams dropped 47.3%, advance-fee fraud fell 31.3%, and missed-message scams declined 11.3%.

Meanwhile, spear phishing increased 41.1%, pre-attack reconnaissance rose 33.7%, and company impersonation climbed 28%.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

The findings echo a recent report about management software and AI e-mail assistants. Research from Fortra and Barracuda showed how criminals are using legitimate administrative tools and hidden AI instructions to manipulate users and compromise systems.

Network-provider trust concerns are impacting enterprise AI plans, with AI and data-driven projects the most affected initiatives among respondents reporting disruption, according to a report from telecommunications provider Arelion.

Research released around the recent Black Hat USA 2026 conference demonstrates that artificial intelligence is increasing the speed and scale of cybersecurity activity without replacing the attack methods defenders already face.

Microsoft has shut down EvilTokens, a cybercrime service that used AI to help attackers break into e-mail accounts, study their victims, and build convincing financial scams.

Global ransomware activity increased 23% in the third quarter of 2026, with organizations in North America and Europe accounting for the largest share of attacks, according to cybersecurity firm NCC Group.
