#7149. More students are homeless as districts face enrollment and budget losses

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

A report from SchoolHouse Connection shows 1 in 32 students were identified as homeless, the highest share on record.

Between the 2018-19 and 2024-25 school years, the portion of students experiencing homelessness increased from 2.7% to 3.1%, with the overall population of students experiencing homelessness increasing 12%. During that same period, overall public school enrollment fell by about 1.3 million students.

Some of the largest increases in the share of homeless students were seen in states where homeless student identification increased alongside drops in overall public school enrollment. Those states include West Virginia, Montana, Arkansas, Hawaii, Maine and Vermont.

West Virginia had the largest share of homeless students compared to its overall public school population at 1 in 16.

Districts in that state have also closed around 140 since 2011, according to the West Virginia Center on Budget and Policy. Thirteen school closures have been proposed or approved in just the 2026-27 school year, according to the center's last update in mid-October.

Overall, the state has lost 3.5% of its students since last school year and 17% over the past decade, according to an October count by the West Virginia Department of Education.

"The drop in enrollment raises two concerns for students experiencing homelessness: tighter school budgets, and children who may not appear in school records at all," said the SchoolHouse Connection report about the issue nationally.

The budget squeeze, drop in overall enrollment, and increase in students identified as homeless come on the heels of federal pandemic relief funds drawing to an end in September 2024. The American Rescue Plan's Homeless Children and Youth funds provided a historic $800 million to identify and serve students experiencing homelessness. The additional funding allowed over half of U.S. school districts to access Education for Homeless Children and Youth funds.

In 2025-26, the first full year without ARP-HCY funds, many schools no longer had the staff and resources that helped them find these students, said SchoolHouse Connection. The report noted that the lost funding is a potential reason most states identified fewer homeless students despite a national increase in that population.

In fiscal year 2026, Congress appropriated only $129 million for the EHCY program, which means about 1 in 5 districts receive dedicated funding for homeless students. The EHCY program was established under the McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act in 1987.

However, regardless of whether these funds are provided to districts, all districts are required under the McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act to serve homeless students and remove barriers to their education.

#7119. How to build an ‘attitude of gratitude’ across grade levels

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Helping students build skills around gratitude can have benefits ranging from daily mindset to overall classroom culture, a UC Berkeley professor says.

Building skills and habits around finding and expressing gratitude can improve students’ day-to-day mindset, their longer-term emotional and mental health, their interpersonal interactions and overall classroom culture.

Educators can help bring this about, although they need to be schooled in how to do so at each developmental stage, according to Vicki Zakrzewski, education director at the Greater Good Science Center at the University of California, Berkeley.

Gratitude is a relational virtue that requires recognizing that someone has done something for you, and that there was a cost to what that person did, Zakrzewski said.

“Gratitude communicates that you really matter,” she said. “Students have to have that ability to recognize that somebody else has done something for you. Gratitude can be fairly complicated when you break it down, cognitively and emotionally.”

For the youngest students, that means teaching what gratitude is in the first place by reflecting on what the emotions surrounding gratefulness feel like, Zakrzewski said.

“You’re helping them define it,” she said. “When is a time somebody did something kind for you? How did that feel? … Students don’t really get a sense of pure gratitude until they have the ability to empathize with others. You’ll see it become more relevant to students who are around 2nd or 3rd grade.”

By middle school, students have the ability to think larger, beyond their own circles, about people who have made a difference in society. When addressing people they know personally, Zakrzewski suggests having them write a gratitude letter.

“That’s one of the most powerful activities,” she said. “If they can, have them deliver it to the person and read it out loud.”

However, educators might have to take a different tack with high school students, who can tend to roll their eyes at a gratitude-related activity. Zakrzewski suggests starting by involving them in a conversation about what gratitude is and how to express it, and then launch an activity so it becomes part of their daily life.

“Of course, there’s the traditional gratitude journal, every day or once a week, where you say what you’re grateful for,” she said. “A powerful thing with high school students in particular is to do self-science: have them notice how they feel before and after they do a gratitude journal."

Students could then decide how often they want to keep a journal, and keep notes about how they feel in week one compared to week four.

Research has shown that when somebody does something for you, it enhances your self-worth and improves relationships, Zakrzewski said. And improved relationships are important in building classroom culture.

Another activity Zakrzewski recommends is transforming complaints into gratitude.

"If you find your students are starting to go down the complaining route, and you want to help change that mindset, take the complaint, but turn it into something you feel grateful for,” she said.

For example, if there’s a math test on Friday, a student might reflect that they are grateful they get to exercise their brain and how that can help them in the future. Cultivating the habit of gratitude and seeing the world through that lens removes a feeling of entitlement, which research has shown provides mental, emotional and physical health benefits for adults, Zakrzewski said.

“That’s a beautiful way of going through the world,” she said. “If you can cultivate that as a student, the benefits are tremendous.”

Research also has shown that not all cultures practice gratitude in the same exact way, and educators should delve into that aspect, as well, Zakrzewski said.

“That’s really important for teachers, if they’re going to do gratitude activities,” she said. “Have conversations with students’ parents, or students themselves, about how gratitude shows up in your culture.”

#6981. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6974. 9 Interior Trends Designers Are Officially Ditching in 2027

Some of this year’s biggest design moments may not age well.

The end of the year is a great time to look back and reflect. When it comes to home design, there’s a lot to cover, from new trends to furniture collaboration drops to specific internet-breaking interiors. One of our favorite things to do around this time is figure out what’s coming next in terms of interiors.

We love predicting what’s going to be hot in home design for the upcoming seasons; however, you can’t talk about what’s going to be “in” without also touching on what’s going to be “out.” We asked a handful of designers to share what design choices and interior trends they saw a lot of in 2026 that they’re more than happy to leave behind in 2027. Keep reading to find out the nine things they said.

We spend so much time making our interiors warm and reflective of our personalities, it would be a shame to ruin the look of these spaces by opting for one-dimensional lighting.

“It's time to move beyond relying on a single overhead fixture or wall-to-wall recessed lighting to illuminate an entire room,” designer Kristen Mendoza of Four Brothers Design + Build says. “Flat, overly bright lighting can make even the most beautifully designed spaces feel sterile and uninviting.”

Instead, incorporating layers of lighting to add dimension and create a warm atmosphere is much better for your home. Sconces, picture lights, table lamps, and thoughtfully placed accent lighting at various heights throughout the room, Mendoza adds, give a space a more intimate touch.

“I also recommend using warm 2700K light sources on dimmers to flatter people, highlight architectural details, and bring out the natural beauty of materials,” she explains. “Lighting should be considered early in the design and construction process, not treated as an afterthought.”

Part of the appeal of “grandma chic” decor is that it’s been collected over time. The pieces show their age, the interior is imperfect, and it’s very clearly reflective of the homeowner’s personality and life. However, “there is a difference between a room that feels ‘collected over time’ and one that feels inherited all at once,” designer Stephanie Kraus explains, and she’s tired of seeing this false presentation on display in people’s homes.

“Heavy Roman shades with lots of fabric, chintz on top of chintz on top of chintz, fringe on every edge, etc.,” she adds. “One of these details in a room can be charming, but if you use them all together, the room can start to feel like a period movie set.”

It’s often clear when this eccentric aesthetic is manufactured rather than effortless. Instead of trying to make the “ideal” interior in the span of a month, be patient and work on your home over a greater span of time. That way, the end product will look authentic.

Though curved sofas are massively popular right now and look gorgeous in a photograph, Kraus is ready to leave them in 2026. “A curved sofa rarely seats more than two people comfortably,” she says. “Once guests settle in, everyone ends up sitting awkwardly, and conversation feels harder than it should.”

She’s all for a sofa or couch being something that invites people in to linger over conversations and stay late into the night, and a kidney-shaped seat isn’t going to do that.

While designer Maggie Vollrath of Provision Studios loved seeing the return of decorative, playful details like ruffles and scalloped edges, she thinks we’ve gone a bit overboard in 2026. These touches are being applied to just about everything: upholstery, pillows, lampshades, bedding, furniture—the list could go on.

“These details can be incredibly charming, but when they're repeated throughout an entire room, they begin to lose the very character that makes them special,” she says.

Rather than treat every piece of a room with one of these details, Vollrath suggests opting for a scalloped lampshade or a beautifully tailored ruffled pillow instead to still capture that charm without drowning the space in them.

“Limiting these details to one or two thoughtful applications allows them to feel intentional rather than overly thematic,” she adds. “Sometimes, the most interesting interiors are as much about what you choose to leave out as what you include.”

“I'd love to see us move away from selecting materials simply because they imitate the appearance of something more expensive,” Vollrath explains. This goes beyond marble-printed contact paper and burl wood veneer; the designer means on a larger scale, like mass-produced tile that’s made to replicate the imperfections of handmade zellige.

“Part of what makes authentic zellige so beautiful is the craftsmanship and natural variation that comes from its production,” she says. “When those qualities are artificially reproduced, I think we sometimes lose the very thing that made the original material special.”

She would rather see a greater appreciation for beautiful, honest materials at every price point, and if the exact thing a homeowner wants is out of budget, then her solution would be to pivot the idea altogether rather than find something cheaply made that looks like what they desire.

“A classic Carrara marble paired with beautifully considered cabinetry can create an incredibly sophisticated interior, even when something as dramatic as Calacatta Viola isn't within the budget,” Vollrath explains.

We could probably say this every year, but it bears repeating: Don’t solely follow trends! “Just because something is trending doesn't mean it's right for your home,” designer Drew Michael Scott of Lone Fox says. “Polka dots are having a moment right now, but they don't make sense in many homes, or dare I say, most homes.”

He suggests thinking of home trends as prompts that should get you to brainstorm new ideas for your space that actually fit your style. Take polka dots again. If you want to introduce a new pattern to your home, but you don’t particularly mesh with polka dots, try a stripe instead.

“If you try to force something you don't love into your home just because it's trending, you're going to drive yourself crazy,” he adds. Let’s leave this line of thinking back in 2026 once and for all.

While we love a good built-in bookshelf and wooden ceiling beam, those types of architectural details don’t always make sense in every single home. In 2026, as personality-filled interiors became more popular for everyone, a lot of homeowners looked into adding this type of millwork to their spaces as a way to add more visual interest and texture.

“Decorative beams, arches, and elaborate millwork can feel forced when they don't make sense with the architecture of the home,” designer Dani Flax of Pearson Design Group argues. “I think there's a tendency to add these elements to make a space feel more interesting, but the result can actually take away from the architecture itself.”

A home in a modern farmhouse style, for example, would look a bit odd with an intricate ceiling medallion and excessive picture frame molding. Design your interior around the existing architecture and the style of your home, don’t incorporate these elements simply because they’re everywhere right now.

The next year is not about playing it safe with your interiors—designers are tired of the stark white home. While ubiquitous gray and white color schemes had their time, they can often make a home feel generic and devoid of character.

“In their place, we're embracing more intentional, tonal palettes and color drenching,” Mendoza says.

You can still have a mostly white house, of course, but take a little risk and mix in some pops of color and pattern here and there, or choose a beautiful neutral wood tone for your cabinetry instead of a bright white paint.

“Taking a considered color, whether it's a deep green, warm clay, or earthy mushroom, and carrying it across walls, trim, and cabinetry creates a cohesive, enveloping effect,” Mendoza adds. “The goal isn't necessarily to introduce more color, but to use it with greater confidence and purpose.”

This is definitely a hot take, but designer John Maloney McNamara of Coosa Interiors is ready to leave velvet upholstery behind. “I’ve tried, but velvet almost always reads as either a 1990s prom limo or a hotel lobby trying very hard,” he explains. “The sheen shows every crush and bruise, and it ages into something that looks tired rather than loved.”

If you love the look of velvet, he suggests going with a mohair fabric instead, saying it has all of velvet’s depth and none of its shine. It also only gets better with age. And though he knows creamy bouclé has been overdone, Maloney McNamara thinks it deserves a reprise, but only in a rich color that can replace velvet still.

Denmark’s Queen Mary is looking posh and joyful on day two of her historic state visit to Colombia—a trip which makes her the first Danish queen to travel to the country.

Today, the monarch was given a tour of the Gold Museum in Bogotá, and later walked through the city’s main square, Plaza de Bolívar, with Mayor Carlos Fernando Galán.

The official visit runs through November 26, and the queen’s agenda includes learning about the country’s post-earthquake reconstruction efforts (three months after a 7.4-magnitude earthquake struck Colombia) and ongoing sustainability projects.

To arrive in the country, Mary went sharp in a black Forte Forte single-breasted blazer with a frilly sky-blue Stenströms Darya blouse, which she paired with black trousers, Malone Souliers Marion 70 pumps, and black Gucci glasses, plus some gold jewelry.

Then, for this afternoon’s engagements, she slipped into a sophisticated business look, the highlight of which was a black-and-white tweed pencil skirt from Dolce & Gabbana and a black collared top from Red Valentino. To round out the ensemble, she reached for sheer black tights, plus matching stilettos and a leather handbag.

The royal performed another quick change for her evening engagement: a reception in the Biodiversario greenhouse at Bogotá’s Botanical Garden. The look was a breezy wine midi dress with a fun dotted print by Saloni. In semi-sheer fabric, it featured a high neck, long sleeves, a cinched waist, and a long skirt that reached her ankles. Black-and-white heels added the perfect contrast to the colorful maxi.

#6970. Survey: Most Organizations Are Not Consistently Measuring AI Value Against Costs

A new study from consulting firm KPMG found that while AI investment continues to grow, relatively few organizations consistently measure whether the technology's benefits justify its costs. The company's Global AI Pulse Q3 2026 report found that 89% of organizations report measurable value from AI, while just 12% consistently assess that value against costs across the organization.

Average planned AI investment for the next 12 months has risen to $210 million, compared with $186 million in the first quarter of 2026.

The findings are based on responses from 2,131 senior leaders across 20 countries and jurisdictions, surveyed between July 23 and August 26, 2026.

Most organizations have established ways to track AI spending: 61% review costs during project approval, and 59% monitor them once systems are operating.

Consistently connecting those costs to value remains less common. Beyond the 12% that do so across the organization, another 26% assess value against costs for most initiatives.

Organizations reporting established AI returns have stronger measurement practices. Among this group, 48% consistently assess value against costs, four times the proportion in the overall survey group.

Cost-monitoring dashboards are also more common among organizations reporting established returns: 77% use them, compared with 43% of those still experimenting.

"The organizations seeing a return did the implementation and the transformation properly: guardrails in place, workforce ready, data cleaned up," said Priya Emmanuel, global head of aIQ at KPMG International. "That is a sequence, not a secret."

Organizations are also developing more formal systems to manage AI deployment.

Fifty-five percent operate a formal AI management layer, which KPMG calls a "harness layer," comprising controls and tools between AI models and business applications.

Security and identity controls, along with data access controls, were the most common capabilities, each reported by 43% of respondents. Model routing, which determines which model handles a particular task, was reported by 23%.

Meanwhile, 53% assign accountability for AI-informed decisions to a named executive, the CEO, or an executive committee.

The proportion reporting significant employee adoption of AI agents rose from 25% in the first quarter to 34% in the third quarter.

Cybersecurity concerns were more common among organizations with mature AI programs.

Half of those reporting established AI returns identified cybersecurity as a barrier, compared with roughly one-third of those still experimenting.

Overall, 86% of respondents said their organizations are adapting their cybersecurity operating models to address AI-accelerated threats.

The report identifies accountability, operational resilience, and cost management as priorities as AI becomes more embedded in business operations.

"Many organizations have made AI models widely available and track their use," said Swaminathan Chandrasekaran, head of the US AI Center of Excellence at KPMG. "But consumption alone is not a strategy."

#6963. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

#6962. Who Is Malin Akerman’s Husband, Jack Donnelly?

The Hunting Wives star married the British actor in 2018

Malin Akerman may stir up romantic trouble as Margo Banks, the notorious ringleader of The Hunting Wives, but off screen, her love life is much less chaotic. The actress has been happily married to fellow actor Jack Donnelly since 2018, and over the years, the two have even found ways to merge their professional and personal lives.

Akerman and Donnelly have collaborated on several projects, including four movies (and an NSFW sex scene, but more on that below). In a 2023 interview with People, the actress opened up about their relationship, crediting her husband with helping her become a better communicator.

“He’s allowed me to be vulnerable and lean into him a bit, and it’s been beautiful to be able to communicate with someone who’s your partner,” she said of their marriage.

Ahead, everything you need to know about Jack Donnelly, his career, and his marriage with Akerman.

Donnelly was born in Bournemouth, England, on October 28, 1985, and later attended the Central School of Speech and Drama in London. He made his acting debut in 2011 on the Nickelodeon series House of Anubis. Two years later, he landed a breakout lead role in the BBC’s series Atlantis, as Jason—which remains one of his best-known roles to date.

In addition to acting, Donnelly has branched out behind the camera as a television writer and director. He wrote, directed, and starred in the short comedy series The Players, which debuted in 2023.

The couple’s love story began with a family connection. Donnelly first met Akerman through her younger sister, whose boyfriend was friends with him at drama school. During a 2018 appearance on Live With Kelly and Ryan, the actress explained that she had actually known Donnelly for several years before their relationship turned romantic.

“I had known him for the past four years but didn’t see him,” she recalled. “Then all of a sudden he came back from England like a year-and-a-half ago, and I went, ‘Wow, have you always been this cute?’”

Akerman announced the couple’s engagement via Instagram in October 2017. Sharing a selfie with Donnelly and flashing her new engagement ring, she captioned the post, “This sweet and loving man has stepped into our lives and stole both our hearts,” she wrote, referring to her and her son. “I think we’re gonna keep him around for a while,” she continued with a wink.

Akerman and Donnelly tied the knot a little over a year later, on December 1, 2018, in a beach ceremony held in Tulum, Mexico. “It was all about being authentic...not adhering to somebody else’s standard of what a wedding should be,” Akerman told Brides.

“We wanted something beautiful and lush,” she continued, sharing more about their wedding venue. “When we visited Papaya Playa Project [in Tulum], we fell in love with the boho vibes and beach setting.”

In 2023, Akerman shared new photos from the beachside wedding on Instagram to celebrate their five-year anniversary.

When discussing her engagement on Live With Kelly and Ryan in 2018, Akerman also shared how excited her son, Sebastian, was about the news. The actress shares Sebastian with her ex-husband, musician Roberto Zincone, and recalled his excited reaction when she told him that she and Donnelly were getting married. “He jumped up on the couch and went, ‘Yay, we’re getting married!’” she said.

In another 2018 interview with Us Weekly, Akerman gushed about the close bond Donnelly had already formed with her then-five-year-old son. “They’re inseparable,” she said. “It makes my heart warm. I’m very glad to be bringing Jack into our lives for good.”

Over the years, the three have also made several red-carpet appearances together, offering a glimpse into their life as a family.

Akerman and Donnelly have shared the screen in four films so far: Friendsgiving, A Week in Paradise, Slayers, and Us or Them. Their first movie together was the 2020 comedy Friendsgiving, in which they played a couple—and even had to film a steamy sex scene. During a 2020 appearance on The Talk, Akerman opened up about filming the “crazy S&M scene.”

“[It was] just like being in our home,” she joked when asked what it was like filming the sex scene. She added that she was glad to have her husband as her costar. “Any chance I get to have him do a scene like that, who better than to do it with than your husband?” she said. “It’s a really, kind of, intimate, crazy scene, and thank God we know each other well enough instead of just meeting somebody on the first day and [doing] this crazy S&M scene. It was lovely to have him there.”

“We call them all the Hunting Husbands,” Akerman added, sharing their nickname for all the boyfriends and husbands of the cast.
