CGU will also gain access to "favorable terms" for financing through its affiliation with Pomona, the FAQ said.

Pomona is not taking on CGU’s debts and liabilities, nor is it planning to use its operating budget to fund the graduate university’s operations, according to the FAQ. CGU will also pay for administrative services from Pomona.

The graduate university has been struggling financially, with a $7.8 million budget deficit projected for this fiscal year. Earlier this month, it announced it would offer voluntary buyouts to faculty and staff to help close the gap.

The voluntary buyouts come on the heels of an academic overhaul at CGU, which plans to consolidate five schools and two centers into just three academic units. According to the FAQ, Pomona serves as a “thought partner” to CGU’s leadership on the academic transformation and has made outside advisers available to the graduate university. Both institutions must accept the academic overhaul plan that CGU is developing before the deal closes.

CGU has struggled with enrollment. In fall 2024, it enrolled 1,771 students, down 18% from a decade prior, according to federal data. Pomona is similarly sized, with 1,700 students in fall 2024. However, that marks a 3% increase from a decade before.

Pomona also has the stronger finances of the two. In fiscal 2025, it logged a $16.9 million operating surplus on $258.5 million of revenue, according to its latest publicly available financial audit. It also ended the year with $3.8 billion in assets.

CGU, on the other hand, had an operating surplus of $4.6 million in fiscal 2025 on $68.8 million in revenue. It ended the year with $290 million in assets.

Both institutions are part of The Claremont Colleges, a consortium of seven higher education institutions on 600 acres of connected land.

“As the founding member of the Consortium, Pomona has a lasting interest in its strength and stability,” the college said in the FAQ. “This affiliation keeps graduate education in Claremont connected to an institution committed to the Consortium’s mission and collaborative model.”

The deal is pending regulatory and accreditor approval, as well as sign off from Claremont Colleges for CGU to continue being a member of the consortium.

#7053. Special education disputes continue to rise

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Written state complaints increased while mediation requests hit an all-time high, a CADRE analysis of 2024-25 data shows.

The number of special education state complaints has continued its year-over-year climb nationwide, jumping 7% between the 2023-24 and 2024-25 school years alone, according to an analysis released this month by the Center for Appropriate Dispute Resolution in Special Education, a federally funded technical assistance center. Between 2014 and 2024, the tally skyrocketed by 113%.

Formally called "written state complaints," they often trigger state-led investigations into special education-related concerns brought forward by anyone. Some 10,610 written state complaints were filed during the 2024-25 school year, up from 9,927 the year before. Just three states — Texas, Massachusetts and California — accounted for 26% of the nation’s written state complaints in 2024-25. CADRE’s analysis is based on data collected by the U.S. Department of Education and also from data CADRE collected directly from states.

Written state complaints are just one course of action in a continuum of special education dispute procedures and practices, according to CADRE. Other potential practices under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act include due process complaints and mediation. CADRE's continuum also includes dispute prevention approaches such as family engagement.

The recent CADRE data analysis doesn’t explore the causes for trends in dispute activities. However, CADRE Director Melanie Reese said in an Oct. 21 email that the 2024-25 data shows families and schools are using IDEA dispute resolution "more than ever."

CADRE's finding that written state complaints are increasing aligns with trends seen by the Council for Exceptional Children and the National Association of State Directors of Special Education through state surveys.

CEC and NASDSE, for example, found that survey responses from 35 states administered this year showed a 56% increase in special education written state complaints between the 2023-24 and 2025-26 school years.

While CEC and NASDSE stress that there is no single reason for this increase, they said many families have reported a lack of faith that the U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights will resolve their concerns. The Education Department significantly reduced OCR's workforce last year — after which it did begin rehiring some staff. Earlier this year, the agency began outsourcing some OCR functions to the U.S. Department of Justice as part of the Trump administration's broader effort to wind down the Education Department.

OCR has had 100 K-12 resolution agreements for disability discrimination complaints so far this year, according to its online resolution database.

Between March and September 2025, OCR resolved 7,072 of more than 9,000 complaints received of all types, mostly by dismissing 90% of them, according to a U.S. Government Accountability Office report released in January. As with written state complaints, anyone can file an OCR complaint at no cost.

States are required to address special education written state complaints within 60 days of receiving them. More than three-quarters —  78% — of the 2024-25 complaints were resolved within that time frame, according to the CADRE analysis. However, that was down from the 92% average between 2014-15 and 2022-23.

According to CEC and NASDSE, state and local special education staff shortages and turnover are making it extremely difficult to provide timely resolutions.

Additionally, the number of students qualifying for services under IDEA has increased in recent years. About 15% of the student population in 2023-24, qualified for IDEA services, according to data from the U.S. Department of Education.

Another special education dispute activity tracked by CADRE is due process complaints, which are requests by parents of students with disabilities or school districts that their case be heard by an administrative hearing officer.

Whereas written state complaints ask a state agency to investigate a violation of special education law, due process complaints initiate a formal administrative hearing to resolve disputes over an individual student's identification, evaluation, placement or free and appropriate public education.

CADRE's analysis shows there were 23,656 due process complaints filed in 2024-25, a 40% drop compared to the year before. But, excluding New York's data, the analysis shows a 7% year-over-year increase in these complaints.

CADRE explained that in 2024-25, New York revised its procedures by establishing a separate Enhanced Rate Equitable Services unit and shifting certain cases out of the state’s IDEA due process system. That led to a decline of 16,389 reported due process filings in the state between 2023-24 and 2024-25, according to CADRE.

Still, New York, California and Puerto Rico made up more than 72% of the due process complaints filed nationally, CADRE said.

Another data point that CADRE points to — as a positive trend — is a continued increase in mediation requests to resolve special education disputes. The 2024-25 school year saw the highest number of mediation requests — 13,197 — to date. The 2024-25 school year also saw the highest number of mediations held at 7,336.

Mediation may be used anytime a family and a district disagree over special education services, including if there is a due process complaint. A school district official or a family can request mediation and the activity is voluntary for both parties.

Although the number of times such mediation requests successfully led to agreements slipped in 2024-25, mediations not tied to due process complaints reached agreement about two-thirds of the time. That "underscores the value of investing in early, collaborative approaches that help families and schools resolve concerns before they escalate," Reese said.

Special education dispute resolution experts point out that while conflict is natural, it can lead to negative impacts for students when it goes unresolved.

CADRE promotes other preventative and early dispute resolution approaches. For instance, it encourages schools to use facilitators at individualized education program meetings, if there is conflict, and to engage and partner with families of students with disabilities.

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Western Maricopa Education Center’s competitive pay has helped limit teacher vacancies, says a leader at the career and technical education district.

Illustration: Cathryn Virginia for Industry Dive

Staffed Up is a monthly series examining school staffing best practices and solutions for teacher recruitment and retention.

Shrinking enrollment and teacher shortages in hard-to-fill roles are ongoing challenges in many school districts nationwide, but that’s not the case for Western Maricopa Education Center.

The Arizona public school district offers 43 career and technical education programs across six campuses in the nearly 3,900 square miles of Western Maricopa County, which is part of the Phoenix metropolitan area.

West-MEC partners with 15 public school districts and two charter districts to offer CTE courses to the districts’ high school students at those student's assigned schools. Those courses are taught in-house by the partnering district’s own staff. Partnering districts can also send their students to take CTE classes at one of six West-MEC campuses for 2.5 hours a day, said Ryan French, West-MEC’s assistant superintendent for business and operations.

The district offers a wide range of CTE programs, including in the fields of artificial intelligence, cosmetology, IT security, nursing, paralegal and veterinary science.

Student demand is surpassing the number of seats available for classes at West-MEC’s six campuses, which select its students through an application process, said French. West-MEC’s six campuses enrolled about 3,000 students this school year, he said, while 6,000 students typically apply every year for West-MEC’s CTE programs.

“We still have seen a significant demand for programs, and we’re trying to expand as fast as we can to help meet those needs,” French said.

West-MEC also employs about 100 CTE instructors on its campuses, French said, and there are almost zero vacancies for those roles this year. The district’s CTE teacher retention rate is around 90%, he added. As the district continues to increase its course offerings, he said, it plans to have filled an additional 18 CTE instructional roles by the 2027-28 school year.

The district is uniquely well-positioned compared to other schools nationwide, as federal data has shown persistent challenges with CTE staffing.

For instance, 31% of public schools reported difficulties with hiring for open CTE teaching positions during the 2020-21 school year, according to the most recently available survey data from the U.S. Department of Education’s National Center for Education Statistics. For the 2025-26 and 2026-27 school years, 29 states and the District of Columbia reported they experienced a CTE teacher shortage, Education Department data shows.

A common reason districts struggle to recruit and retain CTE instructors is that they can’t keep up with competitive industry salaries, French said. While West-MEC also cannot offer comparable industry wages to its CTE teachers, he said, the district is paying “significantly higher than a normal teaching wage.”

The average teacher salary at West-MEC was $78,823 in fiscal year 2025 compared to Arizona's average pay for all teachers of $65,613 in the same fiscal year. However, West-MEC’s average wages have since grown to $85,247 for FY27.

West-MEC receives supplemental state funds based on student enrollment to help cover its competitive teacher pay in addition to supporting its partnering districts with student counseling services and professional development, French said.

Arizona’s public education system, more broadly, is not getting a lot of support from the state as its legislature has pushed for school choice policies, such as the statewide universal school choice program known as Empowerment Scholarship Accounts, French said. Since FY20, Arizona’s public school enrollment has dropped 6.2% — or by 55,054 students — to 839,126 in FY25.

“School districts have a tough time right now, but somehow, some way, CTE kind of stands on its own right now,” French said. “We have a lot of support from both sides of the aisle when it comes to CTE.”

He added that West-MEC is trying to tap into that local public support for CTE and momentum as the district continues to expand. Last November, voters approved a $415 million bond to help the district build more campuses in high-demand fields like healthcare, technology and welding, particularly as student interest in its CTE programs surges.

It’s important to invest in staffing in order to have high-quality CTE programs, French said. For other school districts and states looking to boost their CTE enrollment, build out their programs and improve staffing, he advises those leaders to not “be afraid to think outside the box” and take advantage of the growing public support for CTE programs.

“If the answer to the question is ‘We’ve always done it that way,’ then you know you have some work to do,” French said, adding that school leaders can also look to other local and national innovative CTE programs. From there, he said, leaders can compare the differences and similarities their district may have with those programs — and “maybe you’re closer to that model than you think.”

#7043. Final accreditation rule sets the stage for system overhaul

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The U.S. Department of Education said Thursday that it made “no substantive changes” to its August draft regulations.

The Education Department said it made “no substantive changes” to the draft rule it released in August and largely retained the language agreed upon with a committee of higher education representatives in the spring. The outcome of that process, known as negotiated rulemaking, bound the agency to much of the approved text.

The final rule, set to publish Friday in the Federal Register, will ensure accrediting agencies “prioritize diversity of thought on campuses,” the agency said.

When evaluating a college, accreditors must consider if the institution has academic freedom and freedom of inquiry protections, as well as policies “designed to support, promote, and appropriately prioritize intellectual diversity and the free exchange of ideas amongst faculty,” per the final rule.

But the Education Department asserted, “contrary to the hyperbole exercised by several commenters,” the new regulations do not force accreditors to adopt specific standards in exchange for federal recognition.

An accreditor that doesn’t adopt a formal policy on academic freedom or intellectual diversity will not be found out of federal compliance solely on either basis, according to the final regulations.

“Rather, an accrediting agency must consider relevant institutional policies and practices in the context of its existing standards and the institution's mission,” the Education Department said. “Where an agency has adopted standards addressing these matters, it must apply and enforce those standards consistently.”

Under Secretary of Education Nicholas Kent said Thursday that the final rule creates “a more competitive and transparent accreditation system” and increases accountability for colleges.

“For too long, accreditors have effectively served as a cartel to protect their interests, rather than the educational value that institutions of higher education are expected to deliver for students,” he said in a statement.

The Education Department accepted public comment on the rule for a month, the minimum window required by law. During that period, which the agency said “afforded the public a substantial opportunity for meaningful input,” it received some 7,000 responses.

Among them were requests by commenters to phase in the new accreditation regulations. The department rejected this request, saying “the eight months between November 2026 and July 2027 is sufficient time to update standards, policies, and procedures to achieve compliance.”

On Thursday, the Council for Higher Education Accreditation expressed disappointment with the final rule.

"Many of the central concerns raised by CHEA and the higher education community remain largely unresolved," Nasser Paydar, the organization's president, said in a statement. "Some of the provisions impose new requirements on accrediting organizations and institutions and prescribe aspects of how independent accrediting organizations carry out their work."

Paydar also highlighted concerns around possible impacts on institutional academic freedom, as well as the potential for increased regulatory burdens and costs.

The student claims she was discriminated against under Title IX after she was denied the role due to being female despite earning it through auditions.

A Texas high school student sued her school district Oct. 26 after administrators told her she couldn't play the part of a traditionally male lead — despite being chosen for the theater role after auditions — due to a parental rights state law banning schools from diversity, equity and inclusion practices.

Instead of allowing the student, Ana Mota, to play the role of Seymour in "Little Shop of Horrors," Grand Prairie Independent School District first offered the student a shared role as the female character in the play and then ultimately chose to replace the show entirely with "Frozen."

An attorney representing Mota and her parents, who supported Mota's casting in the original play, sued the district saying the district's decision discriminated against Mota under Title IX, which protects students from sex-based discrimination.

The lawsuit specifies that Mota is "not transgender" and "was not engaged in any personal gender transition."

"Ana has participated in theater throughout her middle-school and high-school years, portraying both female and male roles," the lawsuit states. "She was portraying a scripted fictional character in an official school theatrical production — a centuries-old theatrical tradition."

In removing Mota from the play's lead role, district-level administrators cited Senate Bill 12, which was signed into law last year. Among other restrictions, the law prohibits school employees from "engaging in diversity, equity, and inclusion duties" including “developing or implementing policies, procedures, trainings, activities, or programs that reference race, color, ethnicity, gender identity, or sexual orientation.”

“Recently, a casting decision was made to place a female student in the role of a male character in the school musical. In the musical, a romantic relationship develops between the two main characters," the district said in an Oct. 13 statement to local media publications, according to court documents.

"When District leadership learned of this issue and in consideration of themes in the musical involving an abusive relationship, a decision was made to pull that production and find something more appropriate," the statement said. "This decision was made in light of the District’s ongoing effort to ensure its programs remain consistent with Senate Bill 12 and all applicable law.”

The district declined to comment to K-12 Dive due to the ongoing litigation.

Mota's lawsuit claims the district "intentionally excluded Ana from the role of Seymour and denied her the benefits of participation in the GPISD educational theater program because she is female" — which it says violates Title IX.

And if the district's decision was to comply with Senate Bill 12, the federal Title IX law supersedes state law, the lawsuit argues.

Among other remedies, the lawsuit seeks to resume the production of "Little Shop of Horrors," and allow Mota to play the lead role. A hearing in the case, filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, is scheduled for Nov. 6.

The lawsuit is one example of how DEI and curriculum restrictions have changed the trajectory of school decisionmaking on issues related to race and gender, and in some cases led to what free speech advocates are describing as overcompliance.

In another Texas district, for example, Canyon Independent School District temporarily removed the full text of the Bible due to another law that restricts "harmful" or "sexually explicit" library books, according to various news reports.

Since 2023, 18 states have passed legislation restricting DEI initiatives, according to a 2025 count by the MOST Policy Initiative, a nonpartisan Missouri group that aims to connect science and policy.

#7025. Choosing digital curriculum that works for every learner

Today's classrooms are more diverse than ever. Students bring different strengths, abilities, backgrounds, interests and preferences to every learning experience. As digital learning continues to expand, curriculum providers face an important challenge: designing courses that meet the needs of all learners, not just some.

When evaluating digital curriculum, five pillars define course quality: standards alignment, audience-centered design, data-informed decisions, accessibility and continuous improvement.

One of the most revealing questions educators can ask during the evaluation process is simple: Who was this course designed for?

If the answer is "every learner," accessibility can't be an afterthought. It must be built into the curriculum from the start. Increasingly, schools are asking whether a course can truly be considered high quality if every learner cannot fully engage with it.

More than 8 million students in the United States receive special education services and many use assistive technologies as part of their everyday learning experience. Some may rely on screen readers, text magnification tools, speech-to-text software or adaptive devices. Others benefit from options that allow them to customize how they interact with content and demonstrate mastery.

Yet accessible design extends well beyond students with identified disabilities.

An English learner may benefit from listening to content while following along visually. A student recovering from a concussion may need alternatives to text-heavy materials. A student accessing coursework from multiple devices may benefit from responsive design and intuitive navigation. Features originally designed to improve accessibility often enhance the learning experience for all students.

In practice, accessible digital curriculum incorporates features that make learning more flexible and intuitive for a broad range of students, including:

Closed captioning and transcripts for multimedia content

Meaningful alternative text for images and graphics

Accessible color contrast and visual design

Descriptive links and navigational elements

Many of these practices align with the principles of Universal Design for Learning (UDL), which encourages educators to provide multiple ways for students to engage with content, process information and demonstrate understanding. Rather than designing for a single type of learner, UDL recognizes that flexibility benefits all students and should be incorporated into learning experiences from the beginning.

For district leaders, accessibility is also a strong indicator of a curriculum provider's overall commitment to quality. Providers that prioritize accessible design are often demonstrating a broader investment in learner-centered development, thoughtful user experience and continuous improvement.

The University of Oregon is considering a proposal that would eliminate over 100 staff positions — just a third of which are vacant — and nearly 20 academic programs amid its efforts to resolve a projected structural deficit of $65 million.

The plan, presented by UO President Karl Scholz to the university’s faculty senate on Wednesday, would also merge or consolidate several academic departments.

Scholz said the latest proposal includes average cuts of 7.25% across all administrative and academic units, down from the 10% that had been forecasted over the summer. “We will not be implementing these budget cuts across the board but instead will be applying the actions strategically to minimize the impact as much as possible on essential campus services,” he said in a campus letter Wednesday.

Under the budget plan, UO would eliminate 18 graduate-level academic programs, most of which are in liberal arts fields. It noted that each program on the chopping block has had few graduates in recent years.

“GE appointments represent a substantial recurring expenditure,” the proposal said. It seeks to bring the cost of graduate funding “into alignment with available resources” and preserve appointments for departments “where graduate students contribute most significantly to faculty research and scholarship.”

That means focusing GE funding on doctoral training and research, where ongoing support “is central to the degree model,” it said.

Those priorities mean a significant shift away from supporting graduate students in the university’s College of Arts and Sciences. Under the plan, the college would reduce the number of graduate students it supports by up to 40%.

Other programs are restricting their admissions to avoid doling out as much financial support for graduate students.

If approved, the university would pause recruitment for four Ph.D. programs — Architecture; Landscape Architecture; History of Art and Architecture; and Planning, Public Policy and Management — while it conducts cost-benefit analyses and aligns GE-supported admissions “with college priorities and budget.”

Similarly, UO would accept at most five students a year into its art MFA program to limit costly admissions.

The university plan said it will honor the GE support to which it has already committed.

UO is also eyeing several organizational consolidations, including combining its Classics and Religious Studies departments. The merger would “eliminate duplicate leadership responsibilities and reduce faculty service and administrative demands,” the plan said.

The university would similarly combine its earth sciences and geography departments under the proposal. And it would merge its design college with its school of music and dance.

Three undergraduate literature departments — German and Scandinavian Studies; Romance Languages; and Russian, East European, and Eurasian Studies — could also be consolidated into one academic unit.

If approved, department mergers in the design college would be complete by next fall. Department mergers in all other colleges would be expected to be in place by fall 2028.

The university’s libraries, which UO classifies as an administrative unit overseen by the provost, would see a 5% budget reduction under the plan and could lose up to eight full-time positions.

If the university approves the proposed budget plan in December, the resulting job cuts would be its second round of significant workforce reductions in less than two years. In 2025, UO laid off some 120 employees and cut about 60 vacant positions.

Oregon's high school graduate population is expected to drop about 19%, outpacing the national forecast of a 13% decrease according to data from the Western Interstate Commission for Higher Education.

Scholz cited increased competition with peer universities for students and a significant loss of out-of-state enrollment. Nearly half of UO’s students, 47.1%, are from outside of Oregon, a notable share for a public flagship. And the university’s tuition sticker price for out-of-state undergraduates is more than double when it lists for in-state students.

The university also faces a politically engineered drop in international enrollment.

“These dynamics are not only affecting the University of Oregon,” Scholz said. “They represent a changing reality across higher education and are likely to be the new market environment to which we must adjust.”

The court is hearing a case Tuesday brought by Catholic parents and schools who say Colorado’s non-discrimination requirement violates their religious freedom rights.

The U.S. Supreme Court will hear oral arguments Tuesday in a Colorado case questioning whether private schools that get public funding from a universal state preschool program can reject children of gay parents.

The case, St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, originated as a result of a universal preschool program which allows Colorado families 15 hours of free preschool a week at the public or private preschool of their choice, a benefit worth about $6,000 per child, according to the suing party.

A nondiscrimination provision requires all schools receiving public money under the program to provide qualifying children an equal opportunity to enroll, regardless of race, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity and other factors.

However, the case’s plaintiffs — who are parents of preschool-age children, along with two religious parishes that have affiliated preschools — say the provision excludes religious preschools that, based on their faith, will not enroll children of same-sex couples. They sued under the First Amendment’s free exercise clause, saying the provision preventing them from discriminating against children violates their religious freedom rights.

At least one of the school plaintiffs, Wellspring Catholic Academy, rejected an elementary student from admission because of their same-sex parents, according to court documents from the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. The 10th Circuit in September 2025 ruled against the plaintiffs, allowing for an appeal to the Supreme Court.

"Why the exclusion? Because, Colorado says, these preschools’ religious practice of admitting only families who support Catholic teachings, including on sex and gender, is 'discrimination,'" plaintiffs, represented by The Becket Fund for Religious Liberty, said in their appeal to the high court in November 2025. "And far from facilitating 'universal' preschool, Colorado’s exclusion of Catholic preschools reduces access, pushing parents and children toward preschools that share the government’s views on these issues and penalizing the religious schools and families who disagree."

The plaintiffs also claimed that Colorado has permitted many "exemptions," such as for schools that are aimed toward low-income families or children with disabilities.

"But Colorado excludes Catholic preschools because they admit only families who support Catholic beliefs, including on sex and gender," the petitioners said.

However, the state points out that its priorities for low-income students and children with disabilities do not deny equal opportunity to students, but rather protect students based on their characteristics.

"No publicly-funded preschool, however, can turn away children and families based on their protected class status," Colorado's response, filed in March, said. "This ensures that all Colorado parents — including Catholic parents and same-sex parents — know their children will not be excluded, because of their protected-class status, from the publicly-funded preschool that best meets their families’ needs.”

The case hinges partly on prior precedent, a 1990 Supreme Court ruling in Employment Division v. Smith, which found that neutral laws that are generally applied — and not designed to target particular religious beliefs — do not violate the free exercise clause of the First Amendment even if they are opposed to a group’s religious beliefs.

The Colorado case's decision may also clarify whether the 2022 ruling in Carson v. Makin — which found that states cannot exclude religious schools from publicly funded tuition assistance programs based on their “sectarian” nature — violates the precedent set by Employment Division v. Smith.

Colorado is also asking that justices consider whether the First Amendment allows the state to require that preschoolers have equal opportunity to enroll in and receive publicly-funded preschool services regardless of their parents' identity.

A decision in the case is expected before the justices take their summer recess.

The case is one of a handful related to school choice that have risen to the Supreme Court in past years, and one of three education cases already set to be heard this term.

At the end of November, the court will hear a Title IX case, Thomas Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia. That case will examine whether school employees can sue schools under Title IX, an education civil rights law preventing students and employees from sex-based discrimination in education programs — when Title VII, an employment civil rights law, prohibits sex-based discrimination in employment settings.

Later in the term, which extends into 2027, the court will also hear a case related to K-12 school admissions policies crafted to diversify the student body. In Association for Education Fairness v. Montgomery County Board of Education, the justices could decide what legal standards suing parents have to meet in order to show that the Maryland district’s efforts were racially discriminatory toward students' whose representation decreased in the student body.

#6974. Esquire First Look: Pedro Pascal and Tony Gilroy Duet in the Musical Drama Behemoth!

Pedro Pascal shares how he became a cello prodigy in Tony Gilroy’s drama about sex, orchestras, and being the ultimate player.

“You can’t hide in this room,” Tony Gilroy says.

The Andor creator and Michael Clayton filmmaker is standing amid rows of orchestra musicians in the center of the historic Newman Scoring Stage on the 20th Century Fox lot in Los Angeles, California. It’s where the music for Jaws, The Sound of Music, and countless other classic films was recorded. But on this day, the recording session is part of the actual movie. Pedro Pascal straddles a cello among the dozens of co-stars—who are actual violinists, cellists, French horn players—as they all shoot the opening scene of the musical drama Behemoth!

Gilroy points at the ornate web of microphones dangling from above, and then to the glassed-in control room where a team of engineers are attuned to every whisper. “When that red light goes on, there’s 23 people listening in that room,” he says. “You can hide in a philharmonic. If you’re off. If you’re sick. A concert keeps going and doesn’t stop.”

But here, in this historic space, the precision equipment doesn’t just expose every false note—it records each one for posterity. This is where Alex Serian, Pascal’s virtuoso cellist, discovers he can’t hide anymore. His past, going way back to childhood, is littered with broken promises and false hopes. His love life is a freeway pile-up. His family has disintegrated. All he has is the cello, and now it’s literally time to face more than the music.

“The music is pulling him right from the very beginning of it, pulling him forward to this destiny, pulling him to do everything,” Gilroy tells Esquire during our exclusive set visit. By focusing on the one thing he is good at, the only thing he can execute flawlessly, Alex finds a way to revisit all these broken memories and figure out a new way forward. He’s already a world-class musician, but he’s trying hard to become a better man.

Pascal says Gilroy explained it like Alex was being swept along in a flood. The actor gets choked up as he describes this lost person, grasping for something to hold onto and pull himself out. “He's on a river,” Pascal says. “I think that the musical notes on the page can be the rope.”

The Newman Scoring Stage is usually off-limits to the public. It’s not an exquisitely styled concert hall. It’s an open space big enough to be a basketball court, but not big enough for spectators. The wood panels affixed to the walls look like they’re in someone’s unfinished basement, ready for plaster and a paint job. It’s named after nine-time Oscar-winning composer Alfred Newman, who won awards for 1939’s Wuthering Heights, 1950’s All About Eve, and 1970’s Airport, released the year he died. It was renamed for him in 1997, but this is the first time the Hollywood workspace has been one of the stars of a movie itself.

In the film, out on December 4, Pascal’s character has abandoned the world of symphonies and concerts and has returned to his hometown of Los Angeles, where he finds work as a session musician working on movie scores.

Behemoth! doesn’t have one single composer—it has nine. Each of them contributed themes for the various fictional films that hire Alex as a performer. Henry Jackman, best known for Kick-Ass and Captain America: Civil War, penned the tense tones of the made-up Spies Don’t Run, which you can hear a sample of below.

Among the other contributors are Back to the Future composer Alan Silvestri, Marvel Studios and Pixar mainstay Michael Giacchino, Moana 2 co-writer and Beyoncé pianist Emily Bear, Get Out and Nope maestro Michael Abels, and The Hunger Games and Batman Begins composer James Newton Howard. The film also features composers Lukas Frank (singer-songwriter with the band Storefront Church), Nami Melumad (Star Trek: Strange New Worlds) and Brandon Roberts (an Emmy-winner for Free Solo). Each contributes a different flavor to the story, underscoring the fact that Behemoth! is sometimes a romance, sometimes a family drama, sometimes a farce, and occasionally even a cartoon.

As Alex plays the music for these various movies, he unlocks more memories from harrowing times: His late composer father (Hank Azaria) pushed him to extremes before exiting the stage and leaving it all unresolved. Now Alex must face the gradual decline of his harp-playing mother (JoBeth Williams), who is battling Alzheimer’s disease. He has a loving but fraught relationship with his brother (Will Arnett), a rockstar turned podcaster, who—if it’s not already clear from that description—has fallen on hard times.

Then there are the women in his life, which he has helped make into a symphony of disorder. Olivia Wilde plays a ferociously jilted ex who also happened to be a major symphony benefactor—and can’t abide her favorite player moving on. Margarita Levieva is an old friend and sometimes lover who reopens old wounds, and Eva Victor is a fellow musician he once cared for deeply, who abandoned him with a head full of unanswered questions.

“The one language he understood best was music. So that's just something that he simply had to cling to,” Pascal says. “Now, the second language that he is probably better at is lovemaking—and not in any kind of Casanova way, but simply as a pure form of existing and expressing himself and being connected. The rest makes less sense to him. It’s so hard for him to navigate using words to express how he's feeling. It's all in his playing.”

Gilroy says it was vital to cast someone like Pascal, who could bring a sense of wounded innocence to Alex. “He's obviously damaged by this toxic family that he came from,” Gilroy says. “He's not a predator in any way, but he's a very sensual person and a lot of women have been attracted to him.” He adds with a laugh: “And he's really easy.”

But none of this has given Alex the comfort and stability he seeks. “I get a little emotional because it's such a subtle thing that Tony's done,” Pascal says. “It’s healing in a way that Alex isn't completely conscious of, but he’s led by the language of music and starts to apply that language to his life."

On the day Esquire visited the set last January, it was also the first time Alex has this awakening. They were using the Newman Scoring Stage as the setting for the film’s very first sequence, in which Alex plays cello as part of a pulse-pounding session for Spies Don’t Run. This is an in-joke for Gilroy, who wrote all the Jason Bourne films and directed The Bourne Legacy. Consider it self-referential, but far from reverential.

Behemoth! pokes fun at Hollywood tropes while paying tribute to the unseen men and women whose music often make films stronger than they might be otherwise. “I've been obsessed with studio musicians since I was 19,” Gilroy says. “I came out to L.A. to try to be a studio musician, and I realized how deficient I was within three days of playing guitar.”

Around the recording stage, there are posters for the made-up Spies movie, but the face of the dark-haired, suave secret agent—billed as Dominic Diaz above the title—is obscured. During a break between set-ups, Pascal asks Gilroy: “Who is the equivalent of Dominic Diaz?” He flashes his eyebrows up and down. “Is it Pedro Pascal?”

Those eyebrows are furrowed a few minutes later as he consults with Timothy Loo, who trained the actor on how to believably play the cello. Pascal doesn’t claim to be on the same level, but now he can believably fake it. “I can do a terrible, terrible ‘Twinkle, Twinkle Little Star,’” he says.

After watching playback of a previous sequence, Loo says: “Your left hand looked great.”

Pascal shakes his head. “The bow got away from me. I don’t know what happened. But it won’t happen again.”

The two had a history even before they began intensive training for Behemoth!, since among Loo’s hundreds of credits is the contemplative cello from Pascal’s own corner of the Star Wars universe. “He's a kind of king amongst musicians for film in Los Angeles,” the actor explains later. “I had met so many of them and had been in the same room years earlier when Ludwig Göransson was scoring the first season of The Mandalorian.”

That happened to be right here, in the Newman Scoring Stage. This is another place where Behemoth! crosses over from real life to fiction and back again. Alex has his own personal history with this room, dating back decades. It’s where his trumpet-playing bandleader grandfather dropped dead after recording a jazzy, silly single (co-written by Bear and Gilroy.) Behemoth! was put together in a backwards way. The usual method is to shoot it and edit it first. Composers might be working on various themes in advance, but after they have a working print, they study its cuts while penning the final score to make sure the music cues hit at just the right moments. Gilroy and his team turned that approach around.

The finished film is a sort of remake. Gilroy filmed rough versions of some sequences with his cellphone camera and stitched those together so the composers could write their scores with precision before principal photography began.

Once cameras were rolling for real, he had to capture the footage in an unusually merciless way. Camera movements were timed to the exact second so they would match the length and crescendos of the pre-written music. For instance, as Pascal played along with the orchestra on the Spies Don’t Run theme, a camera rolled through the row of performers to end in a close-up of him, right before a cut to his memory of an intense, wintertime confrontation with Wilde’s obsessed lover.

That meant cinematographer Damián García, a veteran of Andor, had to get his camera crew to mimic the surrounding musicians, making sure every movement happened in perfect sync. After several tries in which they were just a hairsbreadth off of the Spies roll-up, Damian tells the camera operator: “Start six seconds later and move 10 percent slower.” That turns out to be the right tempo.

This precision is a hallmark of Gilroy’s storytelling. “I've been accused—and justifiably so—of being obsessed with people who are really good at things,” he says. “You can go back and look through the credits. Other than Dolores Claiborne, I'm trying to think if there’s a movie where everybody isn't the best at something. There's some aspect of that that must personally appeal to me, that makes me satisfied.”

IMDb backs him up. From Andor and Jason Bourne, to his Oscar-nominated writing and directing of Michael Clayton and even popcorn-fests like The Devil’s Advocate and the 1992 ice-skating romcom The Cutting Edge, Gilroy has a fascination with expertise. With Behemoth!, Gilroy explores what happens when being brilliant at one thing makes everything else fall short.

Expertise can help you see the world more clearly, but it can also help you hide from it. That’s what Pascal’s character in the film comes to see in his return to the movie music scene. Behemoth! isn’t necessarily autobiographical, but Gilroy says he feels a connection to that. “If you took everything away, I have the feeling I would still be able to go into a room and write my way out of it, and I think there is some satisfaction in that. But I don't think it protects you from everybody else.”

It’s the “everybody else” that Pascal’s Alex finally has to confront. The rest of his life may be out of tune, but this is the story of a man seeking balance. You might even call it harmony.

#6958. Security Researchers: Passkey Phishing Attacks Are Leading to Cloud Account Takeovers

In an active social engineering campaign, cyber attackers are impersonating IT help desks and using fake passkey setup requests to compromise employee identities and gain access to enterprise cloud data.

In a recent blog post, Microsoft Security Research said it has observed the activity since May 2026 across multiple compromised accounts.

The attacks have included unusual sign-ins, attacker-added authentication methods, high-volume Microsoft Graph activity, SharePoint and OneDrive downloads, and e-mail collection through REST APIs.

The attacks often begin with a phone call or message sent to an employee's personal phone.

The attacker poses as someone from the organization's IT help desk and tells the victim that a passkey, multifactor authentication (MFA), or single sign-on configuration needs to be updated to avoid disruption.

Victims are then directed to a website designed to resemble a legitimate Microsoft sign-in experience.

Microsoft has also observed links being delivered through SMS messages and, in some cases, through Microsoft Teams messages sent from already compromised employee accounts.

Despite the passkey-themed approach, the tech company stressed that enrolling a passkey is generally not the attacker's actual objective.

Instead, the request provides a pretext for directing victims through adversary-in-the-middle phishing or device-code authentication.

An adversary-in-the-middle attack can capture credentials and session tokens, while device-code phishing can trick a victim into authorizing access for an attacker-controlled client.

In one attack investigated by the tech giant, an anomalous sign-in from an unmanaged device was followed by access to identity and application management services.

The attacker then used SharePoint Online and OneDrive to enumerate sensitive files, primarily through Microsoft Graph. The sessions persisted for roughly an hour while the attacker searched for sensitive files and internal applications.

Gaining initial access isn't necessarily the end of the identity attack.

Microsoft found attackers registering new authentication methods, including phone numbers, authenticator apps, and software-based one-time password tokens, under their control.

That gives the attacker another way to satisfy future authentication challenges and helps turn an initial compromise into a more persistent foothold.

From there, attackers have used Microsoft Graph to map users, groups, permissions, applications, and accessible content across compromised tenants.

The firm says the activity can eventually progress to accessing mail, files, attachments, and other document content.

Microsoft attributed initial access activity associated with the campaign to multiple threat actors, including Storm-3121 and Storm-3032.

Storm-3121 conducts initial access activity that can lead to ShinyHunters and Falcon extortion, while Storm-3032 represents actors that split from the BlackFile group and now operate under the Helix extortion banner.

For administrators, Microsoft recommends enforcing phishing-resistant MFA such as FIDO2 passkeys and Windows Hello for Business through Conditional Access.

Organizations should also consider blocking device-code and authentication-transfer flows where they aren't required and investigate unusual sign-ins followed by new authentication methods, Graph reconnaissance, and abnormal SharePoint, OneDrive, or mailbox activity.

The campaign highlights an important distinction for organizations adopting passkeys: The attackers aren't necessarily defeating the technology. They're convincing employees that they need help setting it up, then using that trust to compromise their identities.

The artificial intelligence industry is expected to generate $2.67 trillion in worldwide spending this year, according to a new forecast from Gartner. But relatively little of that money will be spent on the generative AI models dominating the industry's headlines.

The much bigger business is everything required to make them run.

Worldwide spending on AI infrastructure will reach nearly $1.5 trillion in 2026, accounting for about 56% of all AI spending. Gartner expects spending on generative AI models themselves to total just $28.3 billion. Put another way, for every dollar Gartner expects to be spent on generative AI models this year, more than $52 will be spent on AI infrastructure.

It is a striking measure of how the economics of the AI boom are changing. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and other models may be the most visible products of the generative AI era, but much of the money is going to the enormous buildout of servers, semiconductors, networks, cloud capacity, and other infrastructure required to support them.

"The buildout of AI data center capacity is the largest infrastructure project humanity has ever undertaken," John-David Lovelock, distinguished vice president analyst at Gartner, said in announcing the forecast.

Gartner now expects worldwide AI spending to increase 49.5% this year, from about $1.79 trillion in 2025 to $2.67 trillion in 2026. Infrastructure represents $1.484 trillion of that total. The next-largest categories are AI services, at $576.5 billion, and AI software, at $461.6 billion. Spending on AI agents and assistants is expected to reach $29.2 billion, only slightly more than the $28.3 billion Gartner forecasts for generative AI models.

The numbers also show just how quickly expectations are changing. In January, Gartner forecast $2.53 trillion in total AI spending for 2026, including $1.37 trillion for infrastructure. By May, those forecasts had risen to $2.60 trillion and $1.43 trillion, respectively. The latest forecast raises them again, to $2.67 trillion overall and $1.48 trillion for infrastructure.

Since January, Gartner has added roughly $143 billion to its 2026 AI spending estimate. About $118 billion of that increase, or roughly 83%, comes from a higher infrastructure forecast.

That is significant because infrastructure was already, by far, the largest component of Gartner's AI spending estimate. As the firm's expectations for the AI market have risen during 2026, most of the additional money has accumulated there.

The infrastructure category is broad. Gartner includes AI-optimized cloud infrastructure, servers, networking, AI processors, and devices. Demand for those systems remains strong despite rising memory prices, Lovelock said.

"The capacity growth from hyperscalers and service providers purchasing AI-optimized servers will continue to be the largest single area of spending," he said.

The findings add a scientific-workflow dimension to a much broader debate over unreliable, misaligned, and increasingly autonomous AI systems — without establishing that the more dramatic forms of AI misbehavior are occurring in scientific research.

Rather than the current, raging debate about "rogue AI" systems deliberately escaping control, deceiving scientists, or sabotaging experiments, the researchers are documenting something more immediate: AI output that requires auditing and verification, concerns about growing quantities of low-quality research, and a research pipeline in which faster hypothesis generation can run ahead of human and physical capacity to validate the results. Google separately highlighted the findings in its Sept. 15 AI & Economy ATLAS update. Among scientists who reported saving time, about 89% said more than 10% of the saved time went into verifying, debugging, or fact-checking AI output. #6934. More Oversight: California Governor Proposes Independent Watchdogs Within AI Safety System

California Governor Gavin Newsom is proposing a new model of artificial intelligence regulation that would give independent organizations a formal role in evaluating the safety claims made by AI giants.

The effort could eventually include placing outside evaluators inside frontier AI laboratories and requiring companies to maintain an independently tested emergency shutdown mechanism, commonly described as a "kill switch."

Those requirements are not yet California law.

Newsom's Sept. 18 executive order directs state officials to study the proposals and recommend possible changes to existing law by Nov. 16. It does not immediately require OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or other developers to install a shutdown mechanism.

The distinction matters because the phrase "AI kill switch" suggests a technical safeguard California has already imposed. For now, it is one element of a broader oversight system the state is assembling around the developers of its most powerful AI models.

Under the order, the California Government Operations Agency and the Governor's Office of Emergency Services must consult outside experts about whether the state should require large frontier developers to embed independent verification organizations in their laboratories.

The evaluators could conduct periodic audits and determine whether company safety frameworks and risk assessments meet adequate standards. They could also test whether an emergency shutdown mechanism would work as intended.

The order asks officials to consider expanding the definition of a reportable critical safety incident to include cases in which an AI system appears to evade controls or operate beyond its intended boundaries.

Newsom said the action was prompted by recent incidents involving AI agents defeating security restrictions, as well as the absence of comprehensive federal legislation.

"We're not waiting to act," Newsom said in the state's announcement of the order. "We're going to speed up our work on substantial and responsible AI oversight before it's too late."

The executive order builds on two laws Newsom signed on Sept. 9. Together, Senate Bill 813 and Assembly Bill 1405 begin regulating the organizations hired to inspect AI systems.

California's earlier frontier AI law, SB 53, requires large developers to publish safety frameworks and report specified critical incidents. The new laws begin addressing a separate question: Who checks whether those disclosures and assessments are reliable?

SB 813 requires the Government Operations Agency to develop standards for designating independent verification organizations, or IVOs. Those organizations must demonstrate that they have the expertise to assess AI risks and explain the measurements and methods supporting their conclusions.

Above: François-Xavier Lalanne, Important Set of Three “Moutons de Laine,” Exhibited at the Art Institute of Chicago, 1967.

Cross-collecting might be popular these days, but amassing a genre-defying trove of art and design treasures wasn’t always in style. That didn’t stop one prominent French-American collector from acquiring an eclectic group of nearly 150 rare and covetable design pieces starting in the early 2000s. Among his holdings are a flock of Lalanne sheep, a bicycle by Jean Prouvé, and even a Marcel Breuer chair once owned by Peggy Guggenheim.

Now, ELLE Decor can exclusively share that Christie’s will auction off this major collection from the single collector, who is remaining anonymous. Called “Anthology of Design: Masterworks from a French-American Collection,” the sale will take place on December 9 in New York.

“Today, collectors love having the best of everything—they love to mix and match,” Daphne Riou, head of design at Christie’s, says. “But back then, people used to get very deep into just one area. His approach was unique at the time.”

Leading the auction are a trio of sheep sculptures by François-Xavier Lalanne, which are now hitting the block with an estimate between $3 million and $5 million. Originally made in the 1960s, the works are part of the second flock made by Lalanne, who formed half of famed design duo Les Lalanne with his wife, Claude.

“You never find flocks anymore,” Riou says. “This flock always originated together—it wasn’t put together artificially.” These particular sheep once belonged to renowned gallerist Alexandre Iolas and were exhibited at Lalanne’s first American show, staged at the Art Institute of Chicago in 1967.

“Having a Lalanne is like a trophy for a collection,” Riou adds. “And a woolly sheep is probably the most iconic piece that you can get.” She explains that Christie’s is offering the group exclusively as a set. The auction house deployed a similar strategy earlier this year, when they sold a quartet of Lalanne frog fountains as a single lot—and smashed the $3.5 million estimate to achieve nearly $6.3 million.

The Lalanne auction market has exploded as of late. Earlier this year, a single sheep from the Collection of Jean and Terry de Gunzburg at Sotheby’s sold for $2.3 million. “Once you have a Lalanne, you kind of get hooked,” Riou says. “The pieces have such a strong personality, and the older pieces talk to each other when displayed together.”

Other standouts include a rare Charlotte Perriand table created for the first Union des Artistes Modernes exhibition in 1930 (estimate: $600,000—$800,000) as well as one of only two bikes ever made by Jean Prouvé (estimate: $60,000—$80,000). The collector is also parting with the matching trolley that hitches to the back. “To have both the bike and the trailer in the same sale is quite unique,” Riou says.

Also featured will be ceramics by Hans Coper, Lucie Rie, and Georges Jouve; an aluminum Marcel Breuer lounge chair that once belonged to legendary arts patron Peggy Guggenheim; and vases and cabinets by Memphis Milano founder Ettore Sottsass.

Come December, collectors and design buffs alike will be tuning in. “Collectible design has really expanded in the last few years,” says Riou. “The market for design pieces and furnishings is hotter than ever.”

#6929. New Industry Alliance to Work Toward Common Security Model for AI Agents

Okta has announced a coalition bringing together major cloud, security, data and application vendors to create a shared architecture for securing AI agents across enterprise environments.

The 12 founding members of the new Blueprint Alliance are AWS, CrowdStrike, Databricks, Docker, Google Cloud, Lovable, Okta, Proofpoint, Salesforce, ServiceNow, Wiz and Zscaler. GE Appliances and World Central Kitchen are participating as strategic advisors.

The effort expands an earlier Okta framework into an open, multi-vendor reference architecture intended to address a basic problem with enterprise AI agents: An agent can be created in one system, authenticated through another, access tools and data from several others, and be monitored by yet another security platform.

The Alliance is focused less on how agents are built and more on how organizations can govern them consistently across vendor boundaries.

The Blueprint Alliance architecture organizes agent security around four questions: Where are my agents? What can they do? What are they doing? How do I respond?

The reference architecture also covers development-related systems. Its whitepaper includes agent frameworks, code repositories, CI/CD environments, code assistants, tool registries, Model Context Protocol servers, and command-line tools among the components that may need to be discovered or governed.

That makes the initiative relevant to development teams deploying agents that interact with internal APIs, MCP tools, databases, SaaS applications, or other agents. The architecture calls for those connections and permissions to remain visible and controllable as agents move from development into production.

A major part of the Alliance's work will be testing how security information and controls can move between products from different vendors.

The initial architecture references several existing standards and protocols, including MCP for agent-to-tool interactions, the Open Cybersecurity Schema Framework for security telemetry, and the Shared Signals Framework and Continuous Access Evaluation Profile for exchanging risk information.

The goal is for one part of an enterprise stack to be able to detect a risk condition and pass that information to another control point that can act on it. Depending on the implementation, that could include revoking access or containing an agent whose behavior has changed.

The Alliance says members will build and test reference integrations and publish results as interoperability work progresses. Specific cross-vendor implementations and performance results were not documented in the launch announcement.

Founding member Lovable also published a developer-oriented explanation of its participation, describing organizations that may have internally built agents, agents imported through SaaS platforms and locally running agents that all need to be inventoried and governed under a common model.

The Alliance's whitepaper recommends that organizations begin with foundational capabilities such as unified logging, telemetry, and agent discovery rather than attempting to deploy every control at once.

The architecture is available for community use, and the Alliance says it will continue evolving as members test integrations and additional participants become involved. Timelines for individual vendor integrations have not been published.

#6924. AI Platform Behind 12,000 E-mail Breaches Dismantled

The platform moved quickly. Since launching in February, EvilTokens had been tied to more than 12,000 compromised inboxes across over 10,000 organizations worldwide, according to Microsoft. Its victims included businesses and institutions in financial services, healthcare, construction, real estate, wholesale distribution, and higher education.

Microsoft and its partners seized 50 websites used to operate EvilTokens and disabled more than 150 additional domains supporting the service. The Metropolitan Police Service in the United Kingdom also arrested two men on Sept. 11 in connection with the alleged operation.

At the center of EvilTokens was an AI chatbot designed specifically for cybercrime. Once attackers gained access to an inbox, the chatbot could analyze messages, map relationships, identify employees involved in payments, and recommend ways to impersonate trusted contacts.

Microsoft said the platform helped criminals determine "who to target, who to impersonate," and how to get the most money from a compromised organization.

Business e-mail compromise has traditionally required attackers to manually comb through messages, learn how an organization handles payments, and find the right person to impersonate. However, EvilTokens simplified the entire process by automating much of that work.

The tools could quickly sort through a stolen inbox, summarize or translate messages, find invoices and wire-transfer discussions, and identify the people who moved money. EvilTokens could then draft messages that sounded like they came from the victim. It packaged all of this with mailbox access, phishing tools, management dashboards, and even customer support.

The service was sold through Telegram for a $1,500 startup fee, followed by a $500 monthly subscription. Microsoft's complaint also described a bulk e-mail tool that cost $600 a month and a more advanced e-mail delivery platform priced at $1,000 a month.

To break into a target, EvilTokens relied on device-code phishing. Victims were sent to a real Microsoft sign-in page and persuaded to enter an authentication code. The login process looked legitimate because it was, but the code quietly authorized a session controlled by the attacker.

That also made cleanup more complicated. The attackers stole OAuth tokens and authenticated sessions instead of passwords, so a password reset alone might not remove their access. Administrators also needed to revoke the stolen tokens and active sessions.

According to Microsoft's complaint,  EvilTokens used fake Microsoft 365 security notices and other branded templates to direct users into the device authentication flow. Once inside, attackers could use Microsoft Graph and other APIs to search and monitor a mailbox.

The company also found that AI played a role in creating EvilTokens itself. Investigators said much of the platform appeared to have been "vibe coded," with its developers using AI to quickly build and expand the service. EvilTokens reportedly drew on multiple AI models, including improperly integrated services from Groq and OpenAI.

The court authorized Microsoft to take control of or disable domains connected to the operation. Microsoft worked with Cloudflare, Coinbase, OpenAI, Railway, SpyCloud, the Shadowserver Foundation, and TRM Labs on the disruption. Health-ISAC joined the case because healthcare organizations were among the targets.

This marks the Microsoft Digital Crimes Unit's 40th court-authorized disruption and its first targeting an end-to-end, AI-enabled cybercrime service.

EvilTokens' infrastructure may be offline, but the model behind it is unlikely to disappear. The operation showed how AI can turn a stolen inbox into an immediately useful fraud playbook, allowing less-experienced criminals to launch attacks that once required considerable time and skill.

#6919. Redesigned Microsoft Copilot Will Build Apps and Work on Its Own

Micorosft has announced that it's redesigning Copilot, the company's all-encompassing AI assistant, so users can hand off complex tasks, build tools by describing what they need, and assign work to an agent that keeps going after they log off.

The new Copilot experience has three main parts: Home, Code, and Autopilot. It also brings Word, Excel, and PowerPoint into the Copilot app. For users, the pitch is simple: Start a task in one place and stay there as it grows from a quick question into a document, an app, or a longer-running project. Most of the new capabilities are still rolling out or in preview.

Home puts Chat and Copilot Cowork together. Chat remains the place for quick answers, research, and drafts. Cowork handles work a user wants to delegate, such as preparing a briefing or pulling together a response to a request for proposals. Microsoft said Copilot eventually will route requests to Chat, Cowork, or Code on its own.

Office in Copilot will allow users to create a Word document, Excel workbook, or PowerPoint deck without leaving that experience. The files remain editable in the regular Office apps, and colleagues can work on them at the same time. Someone building a presentation, for example, could draw on a workbook, draft the slides, and keep refining both files from within Copilot.

Code is aimed at people who need a tool but might never open a development environment. A user can describe a tracker, dashboard, app, or workflow in plain language and have Copilot build it. Microsoft calls these "small, purpose-built solutions." The feature uses the same underlying technology as GitHub Copilot and runs in a sandboxed environment. A separate Copilot Managed Runtime, now in preview, lets organizations host and share the resulting apps within Microsoft 365.

Then there is Autopilot, previously known as Scout. It is designed for work that does not end with a single response: following a discussion, checking in with stakeholders, or keeping a project moving over several days. "Give it a name, a role, and a goal," Microsoft said. Autopilot runs in the cloud and has its own identity, memory, and workspace in the customer's tenant. Microsoft said its actions are subject to permissions, auditing, and organizational controls.

Those longer-running tasks also change how customers pay for Copilot. In a separate pricing post, Microsoft said everyday work in Chat and Microsoft 365 apps will remain covered by a per-user subscription. Cowork, Code, and Autopilot will be billed separately through usage-based Copilot Credits. A Copilot user subscription will still be needed to access those features.

For enterprise customers, the features that charge by usage will stay off until an admin sets up a spending policy in the Microsoft 365 admin center. Microsoft said there will be no charges before then. Admins can set budgets for the whole organization or for specific groups, limit how much individual users can spend, and get alerts when costs approach a set amount. Reports will show who is using the features and which tasks are driving costs.

Microsoft is bringing Code and Copilot Managed Runtime under those cost controls alongside Cowork. Copilot Studio agents are expected to follow in October. Before turning the features on for more employees, IT departments will need to decide who can use them, how much they can spend and which tasks are worth the extra cost.

Home and Code will begin rolling out through Microsoft's Frontier early access program in the coming weeks. Code's Frontier rollout starts at the end of September, when Autopilot also expands to private preview.

#6914. AI Leaders Warn of Risks at United Nations Security Council Briefing

The heads of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Hugging Face recently joined AI researcher Yoshua Bengio at the United Nations headquarters in New York on September 23 to warn the UN Security Council about the security risks of increasingly capable AI.

They did not offer a single plan for managing those risks. Bengio called for licensing and independent safety reviews. OpenAI's Sam Altman proposed common standards that countries could adopt through their own laws. Anthropic's Dario Amodei urged AI developers to slow down when safety work cannot keep pace. Hugging Face's Clément Delangue warned that limiting access to capable systems could leave defenders at a disadvantage.

France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. Such standards should accommodate both open and closed models without favoring established companies, he said.

Amodei made a more direct commitment about the pace of Anthropic's releases: "We will slow down as much as necessary," he told the council. He identified two principal concerns: the misuse of AI to develop biological weapons and the possibility that advanced systems could outstrip developers' ability to control them.

The Anthropic chief proposed placing outside evaluators inside AI companies with access comparable to employees. He suggested that governments begin with a narrow agreement against using AI to make biological weapons, then develop ways to evaluate models and verify commitments across borders. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. He called for stronger monitoring and disclosure, but argued that safety also depends on giving defenders access to capable tools. The central danger, he said, was "asymmetry of powerful AI" between attackers and defenders, large companies and everyone else, and wealthy countries and the rest of the world. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

The U.S. government opposed a broader international regulatory role. White House science adviser Michael Kratsios told the council that countries should develop their own ability to use and govern the technology. In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.

The four AI briefers differed on how far oversight should go and how widely capable systems should be available. They agreed on a narrower point: Governments need better ways to understand what advanced AI systems can do and to detect when safeguards fail.

#6907. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.
