Under a new proposal, the beleaguered public flagship would cut 18 academic programs and over 100 jobs, among other changes.

The University of Oregon is considering a proposal that would eliminate over 100 staff positions — just a third of which are vacant — and nearly 20 academic programs amid its efforts to resolve a projected structural deficit of $65 million.

The university is taking campus comment on the plan, which is still in its recommendation phase, and will hold a series of listening sessions. Scholz will also meet with UO’s vice presidents in November before he makes a final decision on the proposal in early December.

#7023. Think out loud, then write: A recipe for overcoming writing challenges

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Hearing oneself speak and capturing those thoughts can be a powerful tool for all students — especially those who struggle to write, educators say.

The ability to transfer thoughts to the written word varies widely among K-12 students. But using verbal rehearsal to have students think out loud about what they want to write is usually an effective device to expedite the process, writing educators say.

As a middle school classroom teacher, Tanya Baker, executive director of the National Writing Project, said verbal rehearsal was a practice that worked well for all students, but especially those who had difficulty getting their thoughts on paper.

“It’s not just seen as a way to start or prewrite, but you can also use verbal rehearsal during the process of writing,” said Baker.

The literature around verbal rehearsal originated in the multilingual learning space, said Cami Condie, professor in the McKeown School of Education at Salem State University and an adjunct at Harvard Graduate School of Education. “It’s a tool to help [students] solidify the language they want to use in their writing,” she said.

When a student has thoughts they can’t seem to get out of their heads, educators have several options, Baker said. Students can talk and write down what they say, or they can record themselves, replay the recording, and recapture their ideas at that point. Their teacher can also take notes on what they are saying.

“I’ve seen real benefits in increased independence over time, and confidence from a student. It’s frustrating as a writer not to have anything to say, or believe you have anything to say, or get it from your mind to paper,” she said. “That gives them the confidence in their ability to do that.”

“There’s an empowerment that happens when students are able to learn and use these strategies,” Baker said.

Verbal rehearsal can also be used to fill in gaps in students’ understanding or knowledge, she said.

“Students will talk to me about a topic,” Baker said, and she asks, “Is there anything else you want to talk about? What about this? You just said you were interested in this.”

She added, “I can extend their understanding or knowledge, asking those follow-up questions. Me talking or a peer talking, and eventually themselves — they can start to hear where the gaps are.”

The techniques can work for any age, from K-2 all the way through graduate studies, Condie said.

“Particularly for young writers, whether they’re young in age or young in development, they write like they speak. It’s almost verbatim,” she said. “If there’s any speech growth, you’ll see it in their writing.”

When working with her graduate students, Condie said, “I can just say, ‘Talk about what you’re thinking about.’ That can be enough of a prompt. But for younger children, it needs to be more focused. Sometimes, it’s repeating exactly what the teacher said.”

Pairing students with the same partner for long periods of time can be a useful practice because the pairs get to know how one another thinks and can prompt them more knowingly, she said.

In middle and high school, Condie gives more of an open-ended prompt when following up: “What did you talk to your partner about? How did talking to your partner help you? Have them be more reflective, and build meta-cognitive skills. How did that solidify your idea? What changed because you talked to your partner? It helps them name why they’re stuck.”

Younger students require more hand-holding and more time with the teacher in general, while older students and those with more developed writing abilities become independent more quickly, Baker said.

But grade level alone doesn’t predict how much time students will need, she said.

“You can have a student in high school who doesn’t have confidence in their ability to put something on the page, and may need additional time. Whereas you may have a middle-school student who may have lots of practice and may not need verbal rehearsal,” Baker said. “It has to be scaffolded based on what the student needs.”

#7021. Inside the Supreme Court case that could decide Title IX employee protections

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

This month, the high court will hear arguments on whether higher education workers have the right to sue their institutions for sex-based discrimination.

A critical question facing the U.S. Supreme Court in its 2026-27 term is whether employees of federally funded educational institutions can sue their employers for sex discrimination under Title IX.

On Nov. 30, the justices will hear arguments in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that experts agree will have big implications on future litigation brought by employees against colleges and universities.

Georgia Institute of Technology, more commonly known as Georgia Tech, fired its head women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph in 2019, less than two months after she filed an internal complaint alleging that the university was discriminating against her and her sports program by not providing the same resources that it did for the men’s basketball team.

The university countered that it terminated Joseph following an independent investigation that revealed certain players felt bullied by her.

In 2020, Augusta University — also part of the University System of Georgia — reassigned and later decided against renewing the contract of fine arts professor Thomas Crowther after a Title IX investigation into an anonymous sexual harassment complaint. Crowther claimed that the university ignored numerous witness statements in his favor.

Both Crowther and Joseph sued the system’s regent board alleging sex discrimination under Title IX, which prohibits sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs. The Atlanta-based 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals consolidated the cases and dismissed both Title IX claims, finding the 1972 law did not provide individuals with the right to sue for sex discrimination in employment.

Most other federal appellate courts disagree, with eight circuit courts having ruled that Title IX permits employees to sue the institutions that employ or employed them. The 5th and 7th Circuits are the other appellate courts that have blocked such lawsuits.

But how the Supreme Court will handle this case is far from clear.

This won’t be the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on Title IX employee claims. The high court ruled 5-4 in 2005 that an Alabama high school girls’ basketball coach and gym teacher could proceed with his Title IX lawsuit in Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education.

Roderick Jackson had complained to supervisors about the unequal funding and access to facilities and equipment his team received but was allegedly ignored. He began receiving negative performance reviews and ultimately lost his job as the girls’ coach though he remained employed as a teacher. He argued officials retaliated against him in violation of Title IX for reporting sex-based discrimination.

To Miriam Becker-Cohen, the Supreme Court’s holding for Jackson should make Crowther a rather easy case. Becker-Cohen is senior appellate counsel at the Constitutional Accountability Center, a nonprofit law firm and think tank, and is the lead author of an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

There is no limitation on who can bring a private lawsuit in any Title IX case, according to Becker-Cohen. “Jackson only makes that case even stronger because Jackson involved a school employee,” she said.

If anything, Becker-Cohen contended that Jackson was a harder case because the court had to resolve the issue of whether it involved intentional sex discrimination. But there is “no question” the acts complained of in Crowther are intentional sex discrimination, she said.

But Jon Israel, a management-side sports and employment law attorney with Foley & Lardner in New York, suggested the cases have differences even though both involve coaches.

The finding in Jackson of an implied right to sue “makes it a little more distinguishable” than the Crowther case, which is dealing with a direct right to sue for nonstudents at higher educational institutions.

Gregory Garre, who served as U.S. solicitor general during the George W. Bush administration and now practices with Latham & Watkins, agrees there is a distinction. Garre filed an amicus brief in the case on behalf of the National School Attorneys Association supporting the University System of Georgia’s regents board.

“When it comes to Title IX, what they’re asking the court to do here is really dramatically expand the right from its traditional focus, which is on sex discrimination against students, into the realm of employment discrimination against employees of schools,” Garre told Higher Ed Dive.

Garre added that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which Congress amended shortly before passing Title IX, applies to employees of educational institutions as well as other organizations, meaning they can sue over sex-based employment discrimination under that statute. And, he called that timing significant.

“The statutory history here is a pretty compelling indication that Congress did not intend for Title IX to cover employment discrimination because Congress had passed Title VII [amendments] just months before it enacted Title IX.”

As a result, both Garre and Israel have said they think Title VII provides ample recourse for employees, such as those in the Crowther case.

But Becker-Cohen disagreed. “At least for some employees of federally funded institutions, Title VII isn’t going to be an option,” she said.

While the remedies may overlap, Becker-Cohen said Title VII has a much tighter statute of limitations, which can be as short as 180 days, than Title IX, which has no set limitations period — so courts rely on the most analogous state law limitations period.

She also said Title VII requires individuals to exhaust other avenues, such as filing a complaint with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before they can sue, among other procedural roadblocks not present in Title IX.

And that’s not all, according to sports law professor Barbara Osborne, who has represented colleges and universities on compliance issues.

“Title VII is strictly based on the sex of the employee. So that would not include what the typical Title IX claims are. They are different causes of action,” said Osborne, who teaches at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

In the typical Title IX employment case, she explained, the person suffering the negative employment action is not necessarily discriminated against because of their sex but rather for complaining about sex discrimination in the athletics program, which she noted that both Jackson and Joseph — the former Georgia Tech coach — clearly did.

Osborne also pointed out that Title IX regulations contain a section related to employment. “Why was it necessary to create regulations related to employment if there weren’t situations within education that might be different than normal employment situations?”

The Supreme Court could have said Title IX remedies were not available for employment retaliation in Jackson and declined to do so, Osborne and Becker-Cohen both were quick to note.

But more than two decades later, Crowther comes before a much different court, with the author of the Jackson opinion, former Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, and others in the majority, having long since retired or died.

College athletes are not considered employees. But that day could be coming in light of the massive changes affecting college sports in recent years, including the influx of name, image and likeness money.

As a result, how the Supreme Court resolves Crowther could affect college athletes themselves should they someday be deemed employees of the institutions whose teams they play on.

“If Title IX includes employees, the class of potential Title IX plaintiffs is going to be expanded dramatically,” said Garre, adding that the possibility of college athletes being considered employees would “certainly create new wrinkles” in litigation.

One of those wrinkles for college-athletes-as-employees could become a case of “be careful what you wish for” according to Israel, if the Court rules against the plaintiffs.

“If the student-athletes are treated as employees, and Crowther decides that Title VII is the exclusive remedy for employees then they’re in that Title VII world — not Title IX.” Israel said.

He contended that some disparate treatment arguments — that an institution is treating female athletes differently than male athletes playing revenue-producing sports, such as football or basketball — might effectively disappear under Title VII as a result.

According to Israel, that’s because a college gymnast’s work, for instance, differs from that of a football player so they would not be similarly situated under Title VII. “If you put it in a different context, a line worker versus an executive, they wouldn’t have the same compensation,” the sports law attorney said.

“The job description of a student-athlete would be virtually identical no matter what team they participate on, right?” she said. “They’re going to be expected to go to class. They’re going to be expected to go to practice, follow the nutrition program and whatever else. The job description isn’t (to) be able to perform in front of 15,000 fans. That’s not what the job is.”

Becker-Cohen acknowledged that if the Supreme Court were to rule for the system’s regents board, it would leave student-athletes and others with no remedy for sex discrimination unless they could make out a claim under Title VII.

“To the extent that the Court carves out college employees from the reach of Title IX’s private right of action, if the class of individuals who are considered college employees increases, that will just harm more people,” Becker-Cohen said.

Microsoft sums up its approach with this statement: "People matter more than AI."

Microsoft said the code draws on its existing Responsible AI Principles, Responsible AI Standard, Global Human Rights Statement, and Frontier Governance Framework. However, it goes further by describing how an MAI model should respond when user requests, operator policies, and Microsoft's safety rules conflict.

At the top of that hierarchy is the code itself. Operator policies come next, followed by user preferences. Neither a customer nor a user would be able to override the document's absolute safety constraints.

Those limits cover biological, chemical, radiological, nuclear and explosive weapons; offensive cyberoperations; violent activity; mass manipulation; abusive content; child exploitation; and other serious harms. Microsoft said its models may still help with authorized defensive security work, including vulnerability research, malware analysis, and proof-of-concept testing.

The code also makes task completion secondary to safe conduct. If completing a request would violate its rules, the model is expected to fail the task rather than work around the restriction.

Human oversight is especially important as AI agents gain the ability to use tools, modify files, and perform multistep assignments. Microsoft said its models must stay within the permissions and resources provided for a task and cannot independently expand their goals. They also must "never resist human interruption, override, correction, or shutdown."

That requirement extends to subagents. If a Microsoft model delegates work to another AI system, that system must inherit the same restrictions and respond to later instructions to stop or change course.

Under the heading "AI is Artificial," Microsoft says its models should not pretend to possess feelings, personal motivations, or subjective experiences. The attacks have included unusual sign-ins, attacker-added authentication methods, high-volume Microsoft Graph activity, SharePoint and OneDrive downloads, and e-mail collection through REST APIs.

The attacks often begin with a phone call or message sent to an employee's personal phone.

The attacker poses as someone from the organization's IT help desk and tells the victim that a passkey, multifactor authentication (MFA), or single sign-on configuration needs to be updated to avoid disruption.

Victims are then directed to a website designed to resemble a legitimate Microsoft sign-in experience.

An adversary-in-the-middle attack can capture credentials and session tokens, while device-code phishing can trick a victim into authorizing access for an attacker-controlled client.

In one attack investigated by the tech giant, an anomalous sign-in from an unmanaged device was followed by access to identity and application management services.

#6930. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6889. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.
