#7117. Stopped-out student population reaches 37.8M, data finds

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

About 1.9 million people in this group left higher education recently, according to the National Student Clearinghouse Research Center.

The stopped-out population, which represents nearly 1 in 5 working-age adults nationwide, has become an increasingly important pool of potential recruits as the sector confronts shrinking numbers of traditional-age students.

The majority of stopped-out students who return to higher education select new institutions to attend. Just over a third of returning students in the 2024-25 academic year, 34.4%, reenrolled at a college they previously attended, according to researchers.

One type of higher ed institution is particularly well positioned to benefit from returning students.

In 2023-24, public two-year colleges were the most popular type of institution for reenrolling stopped-out students who did not return to their original college. For example, 45.4% of students who previously attended a public four-year college enrolled at a community college in 2023-24, while just 18% returned to their original institution type.

Over half of students returning to higher ed, 53.2%, pursued an associate degree.

Stopped-out students were more likely to return to higher ed and earn a credential if they had completed a significant amount of coursework prior to disenrolling, according to the clearinghouse.

Among those who completed at least two years of full-time enrollment, 7.5% returned to college in the following decade, compared to 2.7% of the overall stopped-out population. And 8.5% earned a credential within their first year back, compared to 4.7% more broadly.

Most returning associate degree students enrolled in liberal arts and general studies programs, while bachelor’s students were most likely to pursue degrees in fields like business and management. Healthcare and the clinical sciences were the most popular fields for reenrolled students seeking an undergraduate certificate.

#7080. How Richmond, Virginia, schools are tackling a $680M facilities funding shortfall

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Voters approved a 1% sales tax to help with funding. In an assessment, each school’s needs are weighed against what replacement would cost.

As it became clear in early November that Richmond voters would pass a 1% sales tax measure for school construction and renovation, Richmond Public Schools Superintendent Jason Kamras was grateful that residents would approve a tax increase even while households around the country are experiencing an affordability crisis.

“We are dealing with decades of deferred maintenance [and] disinvestment,” Kamras told Facilities Dive. “Our challenges are legion, which is why I think the voters so overwhelmingly supported the 1%.”

The district faces a roughly $680 million, 20-year backlog in maintenance, repair and upgrades to a portfolio of more than 40 schools, according to an independent assessment the agency conducted in 2024. Almost a quarter of the schools are more than 100 years old. About 70% are more than 50 years old. Only a handful are less than 15 years old.

Earlier this month, voters in Richmond were asked to decide whether to increase the sales tax by 1% to create a fund exclusively to build new public schools and renovate existing facilities. When the final votes were counted, 68% of the electorate voted in favor of the assessment, according to an Axios Richmond report.

Kamras says the actual need is even more than what the assessment shows — “well north of $1 billion,” he said.

With passage of the 1% assessment, the district could raise an estimated $850 million over the next 20 years. But that’s still not enough to meet all of the needs, Kamras said.

“I wish the sales tax would solve everything,” he said. “It's going to take us a lot farther, a lot faster, but it by itself does not solve all of our facilities' needs.”

The district currently gets about $2.5 million a year for facilities work, according to the school.

Adding to its challenges, the district fired its director of facilities earlier this year for improper use of funds and as of mid-November was operating with an interim director, according to the district’s website. The former facilities chief is alleged to have made personal purchases using his school district credit card, according to news reports.

“The investigation found direct evidence of asset misappropriation and unprofessional conduct” by the official, a school district investigation found, ABC 8 News reported in October.

Among the needs the district faces is a $938,000 roof repair at Woodville Elementary School, $2.5 million for brick wall repairs at John Marshall High School and $1.7 million for updated windows at Thomas Jefferson High School.

Mold, flood damage and HVAC issues are widespread, according to the assessment, which used a facility condition index to determine what each of the school’s needs are weighed against what it would cost to replace the school.

“The Facility Condition Index … is defined as the ratio of the cost of current needs divided by the current replacement value (CRV) of the facility,” the assessment says.

Schools that score between 0 and 5 are considered new or well-maintained, with little or no visual evidence of wear or deficiencies. Those with a score of between 5 and 10 show wear but are still in a serviceable and functioning condition. Those with a score of between 10 and 30 show signs of hard or long-term wear and are nearing the end of their useful or serviceable life. Those with a score of 30 and above have reached the end of their useful or serviceable life, making “renewal” necessary.

The Richmond City Council will need to pass an ordinance to enshrine the sales tax increase into law. The district has been using funds from a local meals tax to patch together funds for school construction dating back to 2018.

Kamras said the city will determine which projects to prioritize based on feedback from nine stakeholder meetings, as well as building conditions, enrollment trends, and concentration of student need.

Richmond is not alone in its need for school renovations. School districts on average only receive about 17% of their construction and equipment funding from states, according to 21st Century School Fund’s State of our Schools report. The federal government doesn’t help much, either, adding just 3.2% of the funds schools need, the report says. Local communities tend to be left to foot the bill.

The result is that U.S. public schools face an $85 billion shortfall on facilities, the report found.

Mary Filardo, executive director of the 21st Century School Fund, would like to see the states and federal government do more to support school facilities across the country, she told Facilities Dive. She said the country should view its ailing school infrastructure similar to debt.

“You can not replace that leaky roof, but it's not going away. You still owe that building a roof,” she said.

Filardo said that schools’ HVAC systems are complicated. They’re not just for temperature control, but also air filtration and dehumidification. Retrofitting older schools also presents unique challenges.

“In the 1950s, there would be one outlet in a classroom, maybe,” she said. “If you were lucky, one on each wall.”

From the states’ perspectives, Filardo sees a wide range of approaches to start addressing needs. Wyoming, for example, has used coal lease bonuses — mining company payments for extraction permits — and Massachusetts has implemented a statewide sales tax.

Meanwhile, in Virginia, Richmond was one of many seeking to boost facilities budgets. The same measure failed to pass in 32 of the 47 localities statewide.

Kristen Nye, executive director of Virginians for Modern Schools, an organization supporting the measure, said voter education proved to be very important in Richmond’s case. She added that the measure opened up opportunities that may have previously seemed out of reach.

“Let's say those plans haven't been touched in well over a decade,” Nye said. “They're sort of blowing the dust off of them and taking them back out in the community again.”

Richmond City Councilor Katherine Jordan led the city’s legislation that put the sales tax measure on the ballot.

“Very often education is a popular talking point on the campaign trail, but when it comes to budget time, somehow it just doesn't make it through,” she said.

Richmond School Board Member Katie Ricard said that if the measure had not passed, it might have been put back on the ballot for a future year. RPS might also have looked to the state for more funding.

Although the measure passed, the school district will need additional funding, said Ricard, noting that she expects the district to continue applying for grants.

“We have 42 school buildings across the district,” Ricard said. “It just gets really costly to maintain all of them. They are old, and their systems are pretty much hanging on by a paperclip, sometimes until they fail.”

At trial, a jury held for the plaintiff’s advisor. She appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which reversed and ordered a new trial.

The plaintiff again appealed to the 6th Circuit, alleging that the district court incorrectly instructed the jury on what facts were relevant to its determination of whether she was an employee. She also claimed that the court abused its discretion in excluding certain evidence and that it improperly prohibited her from requesting economic damages in her closing argument.

Each of these claims failed, the 6th Circuit held.

The plaintiff forfeited her challenge to the court’s jury instructions because she failed to raise it, despite the district court specifically asking whether the parties had objections or edits to its instructions. To grant a third trial on this point “would be unfair to the court and the defendants,” the 6th Circuit said.

In her evidentiary argument, the plaintiff claimed that the district court improperly prevented her from submitting portions of a slideshow containing the results of an Ohio State campus climate survey on sexual assault. She argued that the presentation would have made her allegations more plausible.

However, the plaintiff failed to include the relevant slides in her appeal, leaving the 6th Circuit unable to conclude whether they had sufficient weight to affect the jury’s verdict. The appeals court also could not determine whether the slides offered anything “above and beyond” other testimony in the case.

In all, the plaintiff failed to show that any of her allegations amounted to unfair prejudice warranting a new trial.

#7027. Title IX sex bias employment case about to get its day in Supreme Court

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

A lower court said institutions may not know when accepting federal funds that they are accepting liability under Title IX, when Title VII typically covers employment claims.

School districts could get a hint of how the U.S. Supreme Court will rule on a case that could change how teachers and other school employees bring sex discrimination claims when the justices hear oral arguments next week.

In Thomas Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, the 11th U.S. Court of Appeals ruled in 2024 that Title IX’s right to sue doesn’t apply to employees, diverging from at least eight other appeals courts. At odds is whether school employees have the right to sue privately under Title IX outside of protections under Title VII.

Title IX protects anyone who interacts with a school — including students, employees and parents — from sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs and is often invoked by students who are harassed, assaulted or bullied due to their sex. Title IX protections for school employees include, for instance, prohibiting discrimination against teachers due to pregnancy or pregnancy-related conditions, among other things.

Meanwhile, Title VII prohibits employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex and national origin.

In Crowther, two University System of Georgia employees — a basketball coach at Georgia Tech and an art professor at Augusta University — said the university system discriminated against them on the basis of sex when it fired them.

Georgia Tech women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph was fired in 2019 shortly after she filed an internal complaint that alleged the university was discriminating against her and her program by not providing the same resources as the men’s program.

At Augusta University, art professor Thomas Crowther was told his contract would not be renewed in 2021 after several students reported allegedly inappropriate conduct in his class — including sexual harassment — which triggered a Title IX investigation. That investigation process and ensuing negative performance reviews, he said, discriminated against him on the basis of him being male.

Joseph and Crowther both reported the alleged discrimination to the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, but also sued in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Georgia under Title IX.

The two cases were combined before the 11th Circuit, which said in its 2024 decision that schools may not understand when accepting federal funds that they accept potential liability for damages of Title IX claims by employees, when those types of claims are typically filed under Title VII.

The Association of Title IX Administrators called the Supreme Court case "one of the most consequential decisions impacting the intersection of Title IX and employment law in decades."

Multiple major education and legal organizations have filed briefs in the case, including the National Education Association and the National Women's Law Center.

"The American education system faces a severe and worsening crisis in educator recruitment and retention," said NEA's brief that was filed earlier this year in favor of the former University System of Georgia employees. "Anti-discrimination protections affect whether talented professionals enter, remain in, or depart from careers."

The NEA said that a court decision implicates "a nationwide educator workforce crisis, the professional environment in which recruitment and retention decisions are made, and ultimately the quality of education available to students across the country."

"When sex discrimination drives talented educators from the profession, the consequences extend beyond individual career harm and cause systemic degradation of educational quality," the teachers union said.

Granting teachers the right to sue privately under Title IX would help improve an environment where sex discrimination "has remained pervasive," the NWLC said in its own brief. The center cited teachers' high rates of sexual harassment from their colleagues and students, unequal pay for female teachers despite their overrepresentation in the profession, a lack of leadership opportunities, and pregnancy discrimination, such as a lack of accommodations and supportive school policies.

Such a decision would also help address lower investments in girls' school sports and barriers to STEM in education programs, NWLC said.

Title IX "can only be meaningful for women who work in education if they can enforce their right to be free from sex discrimination, including in court," the organization argued.

However, the Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia maintained in its opposing brief filed in October 2025 that Title IX does not include a private right to sue and that petitioners "had every chance to prove their claims for employment-based sex discrimination under Title VII."

"Had Congress desired that employees of educational institutions have a bonus remedy for employment discrimination under Title IX, unburdened by Title VII’s exhaustion requirements and damage caps, it could have provided as much," the respondents said. "It has not."

The case invokes another — Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education, a 2005 Title IX case that was decided 5-4 in favor of the plaintiff.

That case was brought by Roderick Jackson, a high school girls’ basketball coach who spoke out to his supervisors after he noticed that his team was not receiving equal funding and equal access to athletic equipment and facilities. Following his complaints, he received negative work reviews and was ultimately removed from his position.

His lawsuit reached the high court, which decided that Title IX's private right to sue included retaliation claims for complaining about sex discrimination.

"Reporting incidents of discrimination is integral to Title IX enforcement and would be discouraged if retaliation against those who report went unpunished," the Supreme Court said in its decision. "Indeed, if retaliation were not prohibited, Title IX’s enforcement scheme would unravel.”

However, more than two decades later, Crowther will be heard by a much different bench, given the justices in the majority on the Jackson opinion have long since retired or died.

#6969. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

Enterprise ambitions around AI are running into a fundamental problem: Orgnanizations aren't always confident in the networks those projects depend on, according to a report from telecommunications provider Arelion.

The research found that concerns about network providers have caused organizations to rethink strategic initiatives over the past two years. Among respondents whose initiatives were affected, AI and data-driven projects were the most cited, at 49%. That put AI ahead of security and compliance programs at 44%, digital transformation at 42%, and cloud migration at 38%.

The findings come from Arelion's report, "The Trust Illusion: Why AI and Digital Transformation Need More Than a Reliable Network," based on a survey of 518 senior enterprise network decision-makers in the U.S., U.K., Germany, and France conducted by Savanta.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

Another tension runs through Arelion's findings. Enterprises need networks they can trust to support AI projects, but many of the same decision-makers are concerned about AI's growing role inside those networks. A total of 54% of respondents identified AI and automation in network management and security as the factors most likely to negatively affect network trust over the next three years. That ranked ahead of data sovereignty expectations and geopolitical instability, both at 39%, and regulatory requirements and quantum computing threats to current encryption standards, both at 38%.

At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. Proactive AI-driven fault prediction was among the capabilities they said would matter most to future trust, alongside stronger built-in security, greater accountability, and responsive support. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. "As businesses become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, enterprises are looking for network providers that can demonstrate transparency, accountability, and operational excellence, not just availability."

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

"The idea that a security admin is going to review and approve access for a group of autonomous agents making 10,000 tool calls a second isn't just outdated — it's a fantasy," SailPoint CTO Chandra Gnanasambandam said.

Another part of SailPoint's approach focuses on visibility.

New endpoint and browser sensors, along with SIEM and XDR telemetry and non-human identity discovery capabilities, are designed to uncover agents, MCP servers, credentials, tools, and data stores that haven't been brought under an organization's identity program.

The company is also introducing Autonomous Identity Security Posture Management, or A-ISPM, which uses identity information from its Atlas platform to identify dormant accounts, orphaned access, shadow administrators, and other potentially risky access paths. Organizations can then revoke access, disable an identity or AI agent, assign an owner or automate enforcement.

Just-in-Time provisioning is another part of the strategy. Rather than giving people, service accounts, and AI agents permanent access, permissions can be granted when needed and subjected to conditions such as reauthentication or a business justification.

Capabilities across SailPoint's Agentic Fabric, Human Fabric, and Atlas platforms are beginning to roll out globally. A-ISPM and several other announced features are expected in the company's fourth quarter of fiscal 2027.

"Autonomous agents are no longer just experimental copilots; they are becoming the primary execution layer of modern enterprises," SailPoint President Matt Mills said.
