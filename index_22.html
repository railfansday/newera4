Mansfield’s closure would leave Commonwealth with two campuses — its main location in Bloomsburg and one in Lock Haven. The university was created in 2022 out of the merger of Bloomsburg, Lock Haven and Mansfield universities.

#7363. Court blocks DHS policy limiting international student training opportunities

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The higher education groups suing to strike down new limits on the Curricular Practical Training program are likely to succeed, a federal judge ruled.

The lawsuit was brought by prominent higher education groups, including the Association of American Universities. They argued that DHS unlawfully changed decades of “settled agency interpretation” when it issued its new two memos.

DHS pushed back in court documents, arguing that the two memos didn’t alter “the legal regime” because they were simply reminders about the CPT program’s regulatory requirements. However, Kobick wrote that they “represent a marked departure” from the agency’s prior interpretation.

Since 1991, the federal government has allowed international students to qualify for training opportunities — even if they weren’t mandated by their degree program — so long as they resulted in academic credit, Kobick wrote.

Yet in the August memos, DHS put new restrictions on the CPT program without acknowledging that it was shifting long-standing federal policy. Under its interpretation, an international student could only participate in a training opportunity like an internship if it was required of all students in their degree program.

“In practice, for example, this meant that a law school could not authorize CPT for a law student on an F-1 visa to extern for a federal judge for academic credit unless such an externship was required of all students seeking to earn a juris doctor degree,” Kobick wrote.

DHS also threatened college officials with civil and criminal liability if they didn’t comply with the memos.

Many colleges responded to the two memos by rejecting or revoking CPT authorization for some of their international students or by adjusting their program requirements.

At Columbia University, in New York, officials denied CPT authorization to over 120 international students this fall, according to court documents. Similarly, over a dozen international students at Grinnell College, in Iowa, lost out on internships due to the change.

Some colleges — including Cornell and Michigan State universities — reported that they’ve lost international students due to the two directives. And college officials have reported reluctance to sign documents related to the program over fear of being prosecuted.

A spokesperson for U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, which operates under DHS, said in an email Wednesday that it does not comment on pending litigation.

#7361. How can districts keep CTE programs relevant as tech evolves?

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The rapid pace of change can make it difficult for even employers to have clear answers for workplace readiness, an industry expert says.

How can leaders of high school career and technical education programs ensure those offerings remain relevant in preparing students to adapt in a rapidly changing workforce?

“That question is one everyone is trying to answer right now. I’m not sure there is one clear answer,” said Alisha Hyslop, chief policy, research and content officer at the Association for Career and Technical Education.

Hyslop said the main piece of advice for educators is to work closely with businesses and industries to ensure programs are preparing students with the skills they’ll need to be successful — and that’s probably the same, at least on the surface, as it would have been 10 or 20 years ago.

But there are important distinctions in 2026, Hyslop said.

Because technology is changing so rapidly, employers don’t always have a clear answer for workplace readiness approaches, she said.

Hyslop said ACTE is also hearing that students need to understand basic, foundational artificial intelligence skills and how to use them. Additionally, students should continue to "build up durable skills," which is "really critical," she said.

Despite employers’ own lack of clarity at times, regular conversations should definitely continue, Hyslop said.

“There should be a give-and-take between the CTE educators, and folks working on curriculum and instruction in CTE, and the employer community,” she said. And although technology will continue to rapidly evolve, “students are still going to need critical thinking and communications skills, and the ability to work in teams, and evaluate information — and all of those foundational skills that apply across technologies.”

CTE programs focused on information technology are changing the most rapidly, although Hyslop said she has seen plenty of research to show that across the spectrum of career fields, new AI tools are being implemented and deployed. One example is the use of the technology in the agriculture field to monitor soil health and predict crop growth, she said.

“We had some examples from a school in Bentonville [Ark.] that was using AI in health science programs. They had created a meal planning app for mothers with gestational diabetes, or for pregnant women who could get answers to specific questions; those were joint projects between healthcare and technology students," Hyslop said. "There were lots of applications in digital media, and lots of ethical considerations that students are exploring as they go through that process.”

While some industries are more impacted by the advent of AI and other changes in the workplace than others, at first glance, people might think it’s mainly an issue in technology classes, Hyslop said. “And that is not the case. Because we’re seeing applications across career areas, once students get into the workplace.”

During a panel discussion at a recent ACTE conference focused on the use of AI in CTE programs, Hyslop said, students emphasized how important it was that they learned foundational skills first.

One student pointed out the approach of using a calculator to solve math problems, she recalled. “But that’s not how we teach students in kindergarten. We teach them basic mathematical functions first, and then introduce the calculator to do more complicated things.”

By learning the foundational level skills in CTE programs, they could evaluate the answers given by AI more competently, the students said. For instance, a student studying automotive mechanics could examine AI-generated instructions on how to fix cars.

“If you had no real understanding of those instructions, you wouldn’t know if it made a mistake,” she said. “That was a really important takeaway from that panel — that even students realized that AI, or whatever the next technology is, is still going to need people to instruct, evaluate and modify all of these technologies.”

#7199. This Common Drug Was Just Linked to a 24% Lower Risk of Dementia

For all of the havoc dementia causes to a person's life, scientists are still trying to figure out exactly how to prevent it. While keeping your brain and body active and following a healthy diet seems to help, there doesn't seem to be a pill you can pop to lower your risk of developing the devastating disease. But new research suggests that a common osteoporosis drug might be worth investigating further.

It's a class of medications called nitrogen-containing bisphosphonates (or NBPs). While these are typically used to strengthen bones in people with osteoporosis, NBPs could have the added benefit of supporting brain health as you age, too.

It's important to stress this now: The researchers found a link between people who used NBPs and a lower risk of dementia, but they didn't prove that the medications themselves actually dropped the risk. Still, doctors say there could be something to this. "The association is intriguing," says Anna Hohler, MD, director of neurology for Northwell Health's Westchester Region, and chair of neurology at Phelps Hospital and Northern Westchester Hospital.

Here's what experts want you to take away from the findings.

Meet the experts: Ching-Lung Cheung, PhD, study co-author and associate professor in the Department of Pharmacology and Pharmacy at the University of Hong Kong; Clifford Segil, DO, a neurologist at Providence Saint John's Health Center in Santa Monica, California; and Anna Hohler, MD, director of neurology for Northwell Health's Westchester Region, and chair of neurology at Phelps Hospital and Northern Westchester Hospital.

The study, which was published in the journal Alzheimer's & Dementia, analyzed the electronic medical records of more than 120,000 patients aged 60 and older in Hong Kong. All of the participants had osteoporosis or fractures due to fragility (meaning, their bones had weakened).

Researchers discovered that people who took NBPs had a 16 percent lower risk of developing Alzheimer's disease and related dementias compared to those who didn't take medication for osteoporosis. But when they compared those who used NBPs to those taking other types of osteoporosis medications, the dementia risk was 24 percent lower.

The link seemed to be especially strong in women and patients who had hip fractures.

The researchers were inspired to look into NBPs and Alzheimer’s because the drug had been shown to improve cognitive function in previous animal models of neurotoxicity and Alzheimer’s disease, says Ching-Lung Cheung, PhD, study co-author and associate professor in the Department of Pharmacology and Pharmacy at the University of Hong Kong. “These findings led us to investigate whether their use might also be associated with a lower risk of dementia in humans."

While the study didn't prove that NBPs were responsible for the lowered risk, doctors have a few theories on what could be driving this.

For one, it’s possible that the link is more indirect, according to Clifford Segil, DO, a neurologist at Providence Saint John's Health Center in Santa Monica, California. NBPs improve bone density over time, which allows people to be more active, Dr. Segil says. "Broken bones do not cause dementia but do decrease activity," he says. (Being sedentary is a risk factor for dementia.)

There may also be something about NBPs themselves that could lower the risk of dementia, though. "Some researchers speculate that these medications could influence inflammation, immune pathways, or other biological processes involved in neurodegeneration," Dr. Hoehler says. But, she points out, more research is needed.

For now, doctors don't plan to prescribe NBPs in an effort to lower dementia risk. But Dr. Cheung says it should make people feel confident about using this class of medication for bone health. "Given the low uptake of osteoporosis treatment, people with osteoporosis should consider appropriate anti-osteoporosis medication, which may offer an additional benefit for brain health," he says. Even though osteoporosis drugs are commonly available, uptake has been historically low—studies have previously estimated that around seven percent of women with osteoporosis actually take medication for it.

Ultimately, doctors say that a clinical trial is needed to see if NBPs actually lower the risk of dementia. "If future studies confirm these results, they could open an exciting new avenue for Alzheimer's prevention or treatment," Dr. Hoehler says.

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.
