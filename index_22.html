#7171. 29 states will require finance course in high school by 2031, report predicts

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Ten states received an “A” for requiring standalone personal finance courses in the National Endowment for Financial Education’s latest report.

“We’re excited about the progress this year’s report shows,” said Taylor Stanek, managing director of policy and advocacy for NEFE. “There is momentum and movement behind the effort to create a national expectation that students receive financial education in high school.”

The center's research involved reviews of each states' approach to personal finance education in high schools, state academic standards on the topic, and related laws, guidelines and regulations.

Broadly speaking, states with an “A” grade have shown they are implementing financial education with fidelity, while those receiving a “B” or “C” offer some level of financial education but short of a full semester course. These states also may have some degree of requirements in statewide academic standards.

States receiving a “D” or “F” have little or nothing to offer at the moment, Stanek said, expressing optimism that all 50 states will receive an “A” eventually.

“There are a few things we are keeping a close eye on as states move forward,” he said. “Implementation is key; passing one of these requirements is just a start."

The states also need to make sure schools are offering the courses as expected, as a standalone commitment, with teachers who have been trained, and have curricular materials available to meet the needs of specific students, Stanek said.

"This is an opportunity for every state … to make sure they’re evaluating the effectiveness of these programs and maximizing their benefit and impact,” Stanek said. Each state takes a somewhat different approach based on their unique range of needs, he added.

Utah implemented the first semester-long financial education requirement in 2008 and conducts statewide audits on programs, while Missouri was the second state in 2010, Stanek said. Even states with “C” grades or lower have seen progress in certain localities, with Denver Public Schools adopting this ahead of the state of Colorado, for example, he said.

“In the majority of other states, there’s at least something in place,” he added. “It’s ensuring that it’s offered across the board — requiring it to make sure every student receives it. When it’s an elective, students of color, low-income students and female students are less likely to take it.”

The figures from the 2025 national opinion poll underscore why the momentum has grown, especially at a time of economic uncertainty, Stanek said. “There’s a recognition that people need extra support to be [financially] successful,” he said.

“This is no longer a niche side topic. Folks are saying this is something worthy of being a core academic discipline in our K-12 system.”

The previous five editions of the report were published by the Center for Financial Literacy at Champlain College in Burlington, Vt.

The U.S. Department of Justice recently threatened an antitrust probe over the requirement, arguing that it drives up legal education costs.

The ABA’s accreditation council voted to eliminate the standard while under pressure from the Justice Department. In an October letter, Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward said the group could face an antitrust investigation if it didn’t eliminate the full-time faculty requirement, according to The Wall Street Journal.

Woodward argued that adjunct faculty are less expensive to hire and often have more firsthand experience, and that limiting their role in first-year law school education drives up costs and hurts students, the Journal reported.

The accreditation council also plans to seek public comment on further potential changes to its policies regarding full-time instructors that are meant to provide more flexibility to law schools.

The council is in communication with the Justice Department, a spokesperson for the council said Tuesday. The department did not immediately respond to a request for comment Tuesday.

Woodward’s letter came after the ABA’s accreditation council began seeking public comment in September on proposed changes to its full-time faculty requirements.

The accreditation council’s proposals drew mixed reactions from law schools and professors.

In a public comment, the faculty leadership of the University of Colorado Boulder’s law school opposed nixing the requirement regarding the first year of curriculum.

They argued that the first-year of law school “is a key period for educating students on core legal principles, doctrines, and analytical methods” and “a critically important time for creating community among law school students.”

“Full-time faculty are simply more available for answering student questions outside of class, as it is part of their full-time job to do so,” the faculty leaders wrote. “And perhaps most importantly, full-time faculty build pedagogical expertise in designing courses, sequencing learning, assessing progress, and giving constructive feedback.”

Yet others voiced support for the change. Terence Lau, dean of Syracuse University’s law school, argued that the changes would reduce “undue burden” while still preserving full-time faculty members’ “core role” in legal education.

Lau argued the current standard limits the ability for law schools to tap sitting judges or emeritus professors for first-year instruction. “Law schools should be able to draw on these teachers where they best serve students,” he wrote.

Last week’s vote comes as the accreditation council is under the microscope by the Trump administration.

In August, a report from U.S. Department of Education staffers recommended that the agency strip the ABA’s accreditation council of federal recognition. The staffers concluded that the council wasn’t compliant with over two dozen federal criteria and alleged that it wasn’t sufficiently independent from the ABA’s professional organization.

Losing federal recognition would mean the ABA’s accreditation council could no longer serve as a gatekeeper to Title IV financial aid. Although the group accredits some 200 law schools, only five rely on it for access to federal aid, a council representative said in September to a federal advisory panel reviewing the department’s findings.

That panel, which advises the Education Department on accreditation matters, deadlocked on a vote on whether to recommend that the council lose recognition. Education Under Secretary Nicholas Kent is soon expected to make a final call on the matter.

The ABA’s accreditation council has also caught flak from the Trump administration over its prior diversity standard. The council formally repealed the standard in September after suspending it in February 2025.

#7090. Inside the Lavish Estates Where The Gilded Age Was Filmed

These locations are straight out of 1880s high society.

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE Decor editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Why should the Brits have all the fun? The writer of Downton Abbey, Julian Fellowes, went stateside with the HBO series The Gilded Age, which tells the story of New York City high society in the 1880s amid the halcyon days of the industrial boom. The series explores the tension between old-money elites and their escalating feud with the nouveau riche neighbors who threaten the established social order. Meanwhile in real life, powerhouse families like the Vanderbilts and the Carnegies left their opulent footprints on Newport, Rhode Island, in the form of their famously ostentatious and absolutely gargantuan “summer cottages.” Circumstances which lend themselves to set-design magic.

In honor of season four of The Gilded Age, which premiered November 1, we’re revisiting the real-life settings that have helped bring the series to life. The social alliances may shift, but the architecture remains a dependable scene-stealer. Below, tour 21 properties that rank among the East Coast’s architectural crown jewels, from historic house museums to lavish mansions.

Features as: Bertha Russell’s ballroom and George Russell’s bedroom, as well as the billiard room.

These so-called dollar princesses and their families lived in Manhattan and spent their summers in Newport, Rhode Island. Of course, these industry magnates didn’t consider their infrequent visits (some only came a few weeks in a year) as a reason to exercise restraint. The mammoth Breakers mansion is one of four so-called summer “cottages” (read: castles) that were completed just blocks from each other in a building boom between 1892 and 1902. The mansion was designed by Richard Morris Hunt in the Renaissance Revival style, as a less-than-subtle symbol of the Vanderbilt family’s social and financial preeminence in the Gilded Age. It was architectural one-upmanship that is considered the grandest of all the manses, featuring ornate ballrooms, extensive art collections, manicured gardens, and the latest (then) in modern technology, including electric lights. It has 70 rooms, an interior using marble imported from Italy and Africa, and a library mantel purchased from a château in France.

“When you walk into the dining room of The Breakers, with its painting of the goddess Aurora on the ceiling 50 feet above you or see 500,000 cubic feet of marble in a variety of different hues, you can’t help but be awestruck by the incredible wealth of these Gilded Age tycoons,” says Trudy Coxe, the chief executive officer and executive director of The Preservation Society of Newport County, which has under its stewardship six of the grand mansions featured in the series.

The preservation society worked closely with the filming crew to ensure the safety of the objects and spaces. “We had long discussions about the billiard table at The Breakers,” she explains. “It could not be removed, but it was important to the scene being filmed. Rather than moving it, we worked with their props department to put an alternative covering on the table to simulate an actual game of pool.”

In the series, the Breakers’ neo–Italian Renaissance great hall and music room are featured as Bertha Russell’s (played by Carrie Coon) ballroom, which we see in the final ball scene. George Russell (Morgan Spector) also discusses business while playing billiards in the mansion’s massive billiard room.

Features as: Bertha Russell’s bedroom in the Russell residence

Marble House was built from 1888 to 1892 as a summer cottage for Alva and William Kissam Vanderbilt. It was also designed by Richard Morris Hunt, but in the Beaux-Arts style. For an American house, it was unparalleled in opulence, with a temple-front portico resembling that of the White House, which had been completed 92 years earlier in 1800 (lofty, no?).

Mrs. Russell’s bedroom, built on a soundstage in Bethpage, New York, was inspired by real-life socialite Alva Vanderbilt’s bedroom in the Marble House. The French Louis XV bed was painted and reupholstered in salmon-pink velvet, and the custom bedding used 300 yards of fabric that have been draped, cinched, and swagged.

And that’s just the textiles. Most of the room’s furniture is refurbished antique and everything in the room has been painted to match. Sometimes, Shaw notes, it was difficult to paint a lovely wooden antique piece. “The scenic artists would look at us and say, ‘You really want me to paint over the chestnut? Are you sure?’” says Shaw. “Yes.”

Consuelo Vanderbilt’s (Alva and William’s child) monochrome red bedroom in the mansion also serves as George Russell’s bedroom in the show.

Features as: The exterior of Sylvia Chamberlain’s house

Rosecliff was built for Nevada silver heiress Theresa Fair Oelrichs, whose father, James Graham Fair, was one of the four partners in the Comstock Lode. The famed architectural firm McKim, Mead & White was commissioned to design the mansion, which was modeled after the Grand Trianon, the garden retreat of French kings at Versailles. The French Baroque Revival gem was designed for entertaining at a grand scale—and it certainly fulfilled its purpose. Fair Oelrichs would become known as one of the three great hostesses of Newport.

The white front facade of this mansion, which recently got a $7.4 million renovation, is a stand-in for Sylvia Chamberlain’s (played by Jeanne Tripplehorn) house. You’ll see it in Episode 7, when Tom Raikes (Thomas Cocquerel) pays a visit.

Features as: The servant kitchen and the real-life Sarah Berwind’s bedroom as parts of the Russell residence

The Elms is a Classical Revival dwelling that was completed in 1901. Architect Horace Trumbauer designed it for the coal baron Edward Julius Berwind, taking inspiration from the 18th-century d’Asnières French château. It also houses an impressive collection of paintings and tapestries, as well as the latest technology of its age.

In The Gilded Age Seasons 1 and 2, The Elms stands in as part of the Russells’ lavish residence, including scenes in the kitchen and Bertha Russell’s bedroom. But it truly steps into the spotlight in Season 3, when it hosts a dazzling ball. And as a long-awaited confession of love looms (we won’t spoil from whom), the opulent Newport mansion proves to be the perfect setting for high drama and high society.

Each guest is granted a cinematic entrance, illuminated by a golden glow that bathes the grand façade—a new addition this season, as electricity is now becoming available. “The house’s interiors would not have been electrified yet, but people were beginning to use it for parties,” Shaw says. “That would mean they would have to have their own generator installed somewhere on the property to light up those lights.” To accomplish this, the crew went to specialty shops that make reproduction bulbs. “The original bulbs were much bigger than what we have in a household today,” Shaw adds. “Some of the lightbulbs we used were 10 inches in diameter, which is how big they were then.”

Features as: The exterior is used for Caroline Astor’s Beechwood house; the boardinghouse is used for a scene with Oscar van Rhijn; and another room serves as Agnes van Rhijn’s bedroom

Completed in 1852, Château-sur-Mer is one of the first grand Bellevue Avenue mansions of the Gilded Age in Newport, built by architect Seth C. Bradford for William Shepard Wetmore. Its interiors were designed by Ogden Codman Jr., who co-authored Edith Wharton’s Decoration of Houses. The mansion was host to many major parties, including the Fête Champêtre, an elaborate country picnic for more than 2,000 guests in 1857, as well as a debutante ball for Edith Wetmore in 1889. Wetmore’s son, George Peabody Wetmore, went on to have a distinguished political career as the governor of Rhode Island and, later, as a U.S. senator.

In The Gilded Age, the home’s exterior was used for Caroline Astor’s (Donna Murphy) Beechwood House. The hallway, dining room, and ballroom were used during Mamie Fish’s (Ashlie Atkinson) doll tea party. Several bedrooms serve as Agnes van Rhijn’s (Christine Baranski) bedroom, Oscar van Rhijn’s (Blake Ritson) bedroom, Anne Morris’s (Katie Finneran) bedroom, and Patrick Morris’s (Michel Gill) office. There’s also a scene filmed at the boardinghouse with Oscar van Rhijn, according to Patch.

Features as: The office of Brook family lawyer Tom Raikes

Hunter House was constructed in 1748 at the harborfront near the north end of the Newport Historic District at a time when the city was a bustling seaport in the British empire. The house was owned by wealthy merchants and overlooked the wharves that serviced their trading ships. It’s one of the finest examples of Colonial architecture in Newport, according to the Preservation Society. Inside, its resident families furnished the house with decorative arts produced by expert craftsmen, much of which has been preserved today. Such a luxurious lifestyle, however, depended upon the labor of enslaved people of African descent; recent research by the Preservation Society has identified at least 13 enslaved individuals who lived and worked at Hunter House. Today’s Hunter House honors their perseverance and incalculable contributions to Newport’s past.

One of the sitting rooms in Hunter House functions as the office of Brook family lawyer Tom Raikes, and is set fictitiously in Doylestown, Pennsylvania.

Features as: The Astor family summer home

Belcourt was completed in 1894 for Oliver Hazard Perry Belmont, a prominent American socialite and coachman who served in the U.S. House of Representatives. Like many of the Newport mansions, the Château-esque structure was completed by architect Richard Morris Hunt; it was designed to mimic the hunting lodge of Versailles, built for Louis XIII of France, but with a special ask to accommodate Oliver’s collection of horses and automobiles. In 1896, Oliver gave the house to his second wife, the former Alva Vanderbilt, who designed the neighboring Newport mansion Marble House. Alva used Belcourt as a gathering place for the elite of society, hosting lavish parties and balls and attracting notable figures in politics, business, and the arts.

In The Gilded Age Season 1, Belcourt shows up in Episode 8 as Mrs. Astor’s lavish new Newport home, which Mrs. Russell sneaks inside and is forced to flee to avoid a less-than-pleasant meetup.

Features as: The social gathering scene where Caroline Astor and George Russell meet

Built in 1867 by prolific Boston tea and china merchant Robert M. Cushing, the Ledges was originally the summer home of the Cushing family. This majestic oceanfront pile is perched on one of Newport’s most desirable peninsulas, overlooking Bailey’s Beach. It was built by architect John H. Sturgis, who designed a large, Stick-style cottage that was equal parts comfort and elegance. Today it features original furnishings, papered walls, and unique artwork, including a studio that was added to the side of the house for Robert’s artistic children.

Today, the estate remains in the hands of the Cushing descendants, who occasionally open the house up to film crews. It was featured a few years ago in the film Evening, starring Glenn Close and Meryl Streep. “Working at the Ledges was pretty extraordinary,” Gilded Age location manager Lauri Pitkus told The Providence Journal, “because I don't think I’ve ever been in a home with spectacular views out of every window.”

This historic house was built in 1864 for John Noble Alsop Griswold, an Old China Trade merchant and diplomat. The house is the work of Richard Morris Hunt; it was one of the earliest buildings in the American Stick style of architecture. The house has a complex roofline with a main mansarded section that is pierced by numerous gable and dormer sections. It boasts a number of balconies sheltered by deep eaves, with gable ends decorated with woodwork. Today, the house is a gallery location for the Newport Art Museum. In the series, the house features in Season 1 Episode 1, and again in Episode 5.

Features as: Mingling with the other elite scene

The International Tennis Hall of Fame and its perfectly preserved grass courts are in the Newport Casino, a National Historic Landmark property. The historic seven-acre estate was commissioned by New York Herald publisher James Gordon Bennett as a social club for the elite summer residents of Newport. The Newport News boasted three days after it opened in July 1880: “It is doubtful if a livelier place can be found.” Today, the space is recognized as the official hall of fame for the sport of tennis globally. Since 1955, 262 distinguished inductees representing 27 countries have been enshrined in Newport.

In the eighth episode of The Gilded Age Season 1, the characters are seen promenading around the piazza that overlooks what is known today as Horseshoe Court. A few characters are even playing tennis. “It was fitting to have that history depicted here, given The Gilded Age’s look into that time period,” the hall of fame’s Megan Erbes tells ELLE DECOR. “Several of our staff members got to work closely with the production team and actors, teaching everything from the history of our property to how to hit a proper forehand.” RI Monthly reported that crews returned to the courts last summer to film Season 2 of the show, so it’s possible that Newport Casino will make another appearance.

Features as: The parlor, library, entryway, and staircase of the Morris house, located on Fifth Avenue in the show; Mr. and Mrs. Morris’s bedrooms were filmed in the Newport mansions

William Smith Slater hired General William Walker to build a home as a wedding gift for his daughter. The result was this historic home, which was completed in 1877. The Victorian house is filled with treasures—the fireplace surrounds, for example, were carved by Charles Dowler, who came to the United States to produce arms for the Civil War. Today, the house is open to visitors and is known as the Clouds Hill Museum.

The Clouds Hill Museum is the only Rhode Island site outside of Newport that was used in the series. The crew scheduled filming for Season 1 in late February of 2021, when a few hundred people were on site during two days of filming, museum director Wayne Cabral told Warwick Online, noting that the taping finished around midnight on the second day of filming (after five days of work to prep the set). Almost all the parlor’s furniture was removed, and the head set designer brought in items sourced from local antiques stores in East Greenwich and North Kingstown, museum president Anne Holst added. The producers were adamant about removing the existing curtains, which were disintegrating, Holst added, so she negotiated to leave the replacements (which cost $10,000) with the museum after filming.

Features as: Visit to the home of Aurora Fane scene

The Gothic Revival–style Lyndhurst Mansion was designed in 1838 by Alexander Jackson Davis. Lyndhurst’s third owner, Jay Gould, is the real-life inspiration for the “new money” character George Russell. “He was fantastically ruthless in business, to an extent that Mrs. Astor would never let him in, he was never allowed into society, and she didn’t let his children in until he was dead,” creator, writer, and producer Julian Fellowes said in a podcast. “But he was an incredibly affectionate father and a very caring and faithful husband…and his children all had these nostalgic memories of Lyndhurst, their house on the Hudson.”

In Season 1 of The Gilded Age, Lyndhurst’s interiors are the home of characters Charles and Aurora Fane (Ward Horton and Kelli O’Hara), who are related to the van Rhijns and part of the “old money” side of society. The Lyndhurst lawns and grounds also doubled as Central Park and the Sheep’s Meadow in the opening sequence. The home boasts a bowling alley that dates back to 1894, which was re-created to be the ferry terminal that Marian Brook (Louisa Jacobson) and Peggy Scott (Denée Benton) take to Manhattan for the first time in Episode 1. And finally, the house’s carriage house was transformed into The New York Globe newspaper offices.

The Belvedere Estate, located between Lyndhurst Mansion and writer Washington Irving’s Sunnyside home, is a century-old mansion on 25 acres in historic Tarrytown. The estate was originally part of Philipsburg Manor, a 90,000-acre property built for Florence and Casper Whitney. “While the mansion dates from the 1920s, it has a much older European feel that could represent something from decades earlier,” says Belvedere Estate sales director Linda Fiorentino, who recalls the massive legwork required to film at the mansion.

“I will never forget the background actors descending from a yellow school bus, fully dressed in period costumes and felt hats,” she says. The Season 1 scene was filmed at the pond at the back of Belvedere Estate, acting as a stand-in for Central Park in the scene when Marian meets Mr. Raikes at Bethesda Fountain in Episode 1. The pond is a man-made feature, and the water level sometimes sinks to a mere foot or two, Fiorentino explains. “Before filming, they filled the pond with roughly 600,000 gallons of water to fill to capacity, giving the illusion of a much larger body of water and to allow them to have pleasure boats filled with passengers on the water,” she says. They also re-created Central Park’s Shakespeare Bridge from rough-hewn logs and brought in trees, shrubs, and foliage to cover the existing link fence because it was too modern.

Features as: The drawing room of wealthy widow Sylvia Chamberlain

Toward the end of the 19th century, Colonel Elliott Fitch Shepard, a lawyer who founded the New York State Bar Association in 1876, purchased more than 500 acres on a hill high above the Hudson River north of Tarrytown. He engaged the renowned architecture firm McKim, Mead & White to design his family’s manor house here. The result was a 75-room Victorian structure that mimicked 18th-century Italian villa architecture. Think: hand-carved mahogany paneling, a grand stairway near the front entrance, high patterned ceilings, and elaborate cornice work. Unfortunately, Shepard died before it was completed in 1893, and it passed into the hands of William Rockefeller and Frank Vanderlip. These men were part of a group (including John Jacob Astor, who died on the Titanic in 1912) who went on to convert the building to the Sleepy Hollow Country Club, which remains a private members-only club today.

Scenes in the drawing room of wealthy widow Sylvia Chamberlain were shot on location here. “The entire room was redressed by our team in the Rococo style,” set decorator Regina Graves told 1st Dibs.

Features as: Part of Mrs. Astor’s residence

Glenview was built in 1876 by John Bond Trevor, a highly successful stockbroker, who engaged New York City architect Charles W. Clinton to build his estate. The mansion was a paragon of luxury and modern comfort, featuring indoor plumbing, gas illumination, and a coal-burning furnace to circulate heat throughout its 37 rooms. The exterior had elements of Victorian Gothic and French Second Empire design, complete with a mansard roof, a gray stone facade constructed of blocks hewn from a Hastings-on-Hudson quarry, and decorative sandstone elements brought from Indiana.

The mansion makes an appearance as Caroline Astor’s residence in Season 1. “They were all so complimentary about Glenview, and of course we did a lot of polishing and waxing,” Laura Vookles, chair of the museum’s curatorial department, told The Rivertown Enterprise. “They focused on our exquisite interiors and filmed in six rooms, including the dining, library, and sitting rooms, and used all but one painting that is normally on view.”

The John Paine Mansion, also known as the Castle, was built in 1896 and was deemed the grandest house in Troy, New York. It has since served as a private residence, a college administration building, a movie set (see: Martin Scorsese’s 1993 drama The Age of Innocence), and, currently, a fraternity house. In the show, one of the bedrooms in the Castle features as Tom Raikes’s office.

This residence, the home of Troy locals Bill and Sue Comiskey, stands in for the Brooklyn residence of the well-off Scott family. At one point early in the series, Arthur Scott (John Douglas Thompson) and his daughter, Peggy (Denée Benton), sit on carved-oak chairs around a damask-covered dining table. The home’s elaborate details really shine in this historic home, which dates back to the turn of the 19th century. Stained-glass windows and an elegant china cabinet are memorable architectural features that are original to the location and transport the viewers back in time.

While filming what would be the Scott family residence, Bill, the homeowner, told News10 that a few prized family possessions actually made the cut. “My desk was in it, front and center…you [also] saw the whole front and back parlor into the dining room where they were having a meal,” he explained. “[There were] great shots of this room catching the stained glass, the fireplace, [and] this bookcase my grandfather made.”

Features as: The interior is used as George Russell’s workspace

Hempstead House was built for Howard Gould by architects Hunt & Hunt in 1912 on the north shore of Long Island. The mansion was eventually sold to art mogul Daniel Guggenheim (yes, that Guggenheim). Hempstead House is one of four mansions on the grounds of the Guggenheim Estate—which includes Castle Gould, Falaise, and Mille Fleur. The Tudor-style castle was the embodiment of Gold Coast opulence, a place where the esteemed family hosted grand parties, performances, and exhibitions by gatherings of the elite of the time. To say it is massive would be an understatement: 50,000 square feet, with 40 rooms and a 60-foot-tall entry foyer. In The Gilded Age, the mansion’s interior was used as George Russell’s workspace.

Features as: The late General Henry Brook’s house

Rock Hall Museum was initially an 18th-century plantation. The two-and-a-half-story Georgian-style home was built in approximately 1767 and later restored to its original appearance in the 1950s. In Season 1 Episode 1, the house’s white facade is featured within the first 15 minutes as a backdrop for the late General Henry Brook’s house. Marian, his daughter, is seen walking out of the home on her way to the train station.

The Hart Cluett Museum is a work of Federal architecture designed by Martin E. Thompson in 1827. He was hired by William Howard. Thompson brought the design of the Hart Cluett House back to New York City, where many examples of the 1827 Federal-style townhouse were copied throughout Manhattan. This house, a museum open to the public today, was featured in The Gilded Age in Episode 2 as Anne Morris’s home.

#7037. It’s Time to Talk About “Duty Sex”

There are certain things that belong on your chore list: Doing laundry, washing dishes, taking the trash out, scrubbing the toilet. Sex, as you can see, is not one of those things—and it shouldn’t be. And yet, “duty sex” has become a pretty recognizable term in today’s relationships.

Duty sex typically stems from good intentions, Marisa Derr, LMFT explains. This happens when one partner wants to keep the peace or protect the relationship. It’s when you have sex because for some reason or another, you feel like you have to—not because you’re worried for your safety, but you believe that the success of your relationship lies on “fulfilling” some sexual duty.

This isn’t just an unnecessary pressure that can be demoralizing, it’s also just super unsexy.

“The problem is that sex out of obligation teaches the body that intimacy is another task, and desire fades even more,” she says. “Over time, the partner initiating feels rejected, and the partner saying yes feels used, even though neither one wants that.”

There’s an important distinction between duty sex and what we call “willing consent.” Willing consent is when you’re not necessarily feeling desire for your partner in the moment, but you consent to a sexual interaction with them because you’re open to desire showing up. It’s like when you’re not feeling particularly hungry, but you know that you really enjoy a certain meal so you go out to eat at the spot anyway. Duty sex is born from obligation, rather than curiosity—like eating that weird casserole your aunt brings on Thanksgiving when you really don’t want to, but do anyway because you prefer to not ruffle any feathers.

When did sex with our partners become that weird casserole or equivalent to scrubbing the toilet?

According to a recent study in Evolutionary Psychology, duty sex can be rooted in fear: Fear that a partner will cheat, fear that one could be less attractive than their partners and need to somehow “make up for it,” and fear that a partner will lose interest in them.

And while we typically assign duty sex as something that women mostly do—particularly because of the pressures to perform that disproportionately affect women—the study showed that men were having duty sex at similar rates as women. Though, more research is needed to find out if there is a difference between why men have duty sex and why women do it.

The thing that most people having duty sex had in common was that they were in long-term relationships.

“Couples can start worrying about how often they're having sex, rather than whether they're actually enjoying it,” sex educator Anna Richards tells Cosmopolitan. “But intimacy shouldn't be about meeting a target or keeping score. It should be something you both genuinely want.”

Sex should be about feeling pleasure and connecting with your partner in a fun, sexy, and intimate way. But for some couples, somewhere in the midst of all of the responsibilities of everyday life, sexual desire was put on the back burner. And instead of working to get it back, people are essentially forcing themselves to have duty sex to maintain their relationships. We need a serious overhaul of partnered sex!

“You can't demand desire, but you can create an environment where it has room to grow,” Richard says. Think about what the study said about why people have duty sex: they’re afraid of losing their partner. And one of the ways to bridge this gap of insecurity is to foster a relationship that comes with reassurance. If you’re the one finding yourself having duty sex, communicate with your partner about the pressure you feel. Talking about your needs—whether they come in the form of emotional security, verbal affirmations, or quality time—can help your partner learn how to help you foster desire. And if you’re the partner that’s noticing your S.O. initiate sex just to check it off a list, maybe it’s time to think about whether you’re reassuring them enough in the relationship. Do you create an environment of trust and comfort? Are you caring? Do you help them with their daily tasks?

One of the best ways to remove sex off your to-do list and onto your want-to-do list is to take it off your list completely. That may sound counterintuitive, but Richards says it helps alleviate the pressure.

“Intimacy doesn't always have to lead to the bedroom,” she says. Flirting, spending quality time together, being affectionate, making each other feel appreciated, talking about what you enjoy—these can all help rebuild a connection between you and your partner.

“Sometimes removing the expectation is exactly what allows desire to return naturally,” she says. You also need to make sure that your relationship is a place where saying “no” feels safe.

“A yes only means something when a no is fully okay,” Derr explains. “Pressure, sulking, or keeping score kills desire.”

It’s also about the little things. Saying “thank you” when they do something nice, touching each other throughout the day, complimenting each other, and just being a good partner can all lend themselves into reigniting desire.

Life is way too short to be having sex because you feel like you have to. Plus, any healthy and happy relationship would not foster an environment where sex feels like a duty in the first place. Yes, making a relationship work longterm does take work and effort—but that doesn’t mean that every aspect of your relationship should feel like work. The parts that are meant to be fueled by fun and curiosity (like sex) should remain fun. And the second sex starts feeling like a chore, it may be time to clear your checklist and make reconnecting your first priority.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

#7016. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#6989. Is This the Ultimate Aspen Anti-Chalet?

Designer Rafael de Cárdenas, known for creating arresting spaces for Skims, Nike, and a host of New York City restaurants, worked with longtime client Rachel Dorrell on a mountain retreat with otherworldly glam.

Above: The primary bathroom of a home in Aspen, Colorado, designed by Rafael de Cárdenas with architecture by SAOTA. Custom tub with Dornbracht fittings; ceramic vase by artist Kazunori Hamana; side table by Rafael Prieto.

“We’re having a crisis of taste,” architect and designer Rafael de Cárdenas is in the midst of saying. “There’s a sameness. It’s all a historical mishmash.” He’s at breakfast in Manhattan drinking a cinnamon latte (the restaurant did not have the turmeric he requested). He has already completed his run in Central Park and his gratitude list in his Day One journal app, and he’s on day 735 following a visit to the Hoffman Institute—the famously mysterious weeklong “personal growth” retreat. “I feel like we’re in The Handmaid’s Tale,” he says. “AI is happening, but we’re going to pretend we’re designing manor houses in the English countryside in the 19th century?” Later he adds, “I have never been interested in recreating the past.”

So when longtime client Rachel Dorrell asked him to create a mountain house in Aspen for her growing family, no one expected a typical ski chalet. From her first visit to the plot of land, she realized the views of the town and the slopes beyond would be unmatched. Today those “crazy views” are framed by windows so large it’s hard to tell where rooms end and the outside begins. De Cárdenas, who collaborated with SAOTA architects to create the modernist structure, set about realizing Dorrell’s vision of “Colorado, but refined,” as she puts it. “Glamorous ’80s apartment” is how he describes the results.

“The mood board was Angelo Donghia,” that great of American design who worked with the likes of Liza Minnelli. “And Beetlejuice, always Beetlejuice! And Mickey Rourke’s apartment in 91/2 Weeks.” A cheetah-print rug sits in the library, while sculpted white custom sofas anchor the immense yet minimalist living room. “So many people have asked, ‘Shouldn’t there be art above the fireplace?’ ” de Cárdenas says. “I’m like, ‘Why?’ ”

Following the rules has never been his style. It has been 20 years since he launched his design firm, after studying at UCLA and assisting architect Greg Lynn at Vienna’s Angewandte institute and MAK museum, then returning to his native New York. The son of an erstwhile fashion PR executive and an actuary, de Cárdenas aspired to work for David Rockwell or for Alex de Betak’s fashion production company, where he once applied for a job. In the end he did a stint at Calvin Klein and worked as a production designer for films and commercials. Then a friend asked for help redoing a kitchen, something he did “kind of at night,” de Cárdenas remembers. Next came his first real client: actress Parker Posey. It was a revelation. “She was the first person I ever had to make window treatments for. I was like, ‘What the f***?’ I remember looking at a window and being like, ‘What is this supposed to look like?’ I didn’t understand that residential interior design is, essentially, fabric.”

Around the same time, he landed his first major corporate client, Nike, and did the design of aNYthing, a Lower East Side boutique that became a nexus for skaters, artists, and musicians in the late 2000s. “No one was doing stores like that at the time,” says creative director Jen Brill, who worked with de Cárdenas on several Nike projects. “There was nothing minimal about it. It was loud, it was fun. It was the first store I saw that was like retail theater.”

Another friend, Felix Burrichter, a curator and the founder of Pin-Up architecture magazine, recalls de Cárdenas’s early fascination with the Memphis Group architects (“when it was still considered bad taste,” Burrichter says) as well as his willingness to experiment with materials, such as applying duct tape patterns to the walls of a Jeffrey Deitch exhibition in Rome, or wrapping an Ikea chair with fluorescent gaffer tape. “For the aNYthing store, I physically built a lot of those things, because there was no one to do it and no budget,” de Cárdenas says. He sometimes laments not being so hands-on anymore, now that he has a team of 18 based in New York and Paris. “There was a lot of energy in those projects.”

“His work is like Limelight–meets–Park Avenue,” says Burrichter, who calls de Cárdenas the heir to ELLE Decor A-List designer Peter Marino, a maestro who works with significant private clients alongside luxury behemoths such as Dior and Chanel. De Cárdenas may have moved from duct tape to gold leaf applied by European artisans, “but there is always that tension in Raf’s work,” Burrichter says, “the binary between decorating and being an architect, between uptown and the club.”

Dorrell, for her part, became aware of de Cárdenas a decade ago, when she and her husband purchased a home in the West Village that he had staged. “We were like, ‘Who did the interiors and who did the art? It’s perfect.’ ” She says they were drawn to the ’70s vibe and the peachy hues, an unusual shade at the time. “We didn’t change a thing.” Since then they have worked together on homes in Austin, New York, and currently their largest yet, in Palm Beach. Dorrell sends “rapid-fire” texts of inspiration ranging from tiled walls at Lake Como’s Villa d’Este to barstools in Paris. “Raf cracks me up—we are brutally honest with each other,” she says, explaining that her taste skews more traditional, while his is often edgier.

“Some of my projects have moments that are ugly, and they excite me,” he says. “Because something truly excellent, something that is pushing boundaries, is not going to land pleasurably at first.” He points to Madonna, a frequent touchstone and source of inspiration for him. “The first time you saw Blond Ambition, the first time you saw Madonna with a high ponytail and with the headset, it was a little jarring. The thick eyebrow and the cone bra.” He often finds himself thinking, Where is that moment now?

Despite his concerns about the flattening effect of the algorithm, he’s still happy he’s in the business of taste. “I can’t believe I was lucky enough to land here, because I didn’t know what interior design even was,” he says. “I like my job. I’m grateful for my job. I want people to say, ‘He’s good at his job.’ ”

The Dorrells might concur: “ ‘We’re not doing another project,’ ” Rachel recalls her husband saying about Aspen. “Now he’s like, ‘Thank god,’ because it’s so awesome.”

#6979. Microsoft Expands Protections and Controls for Sovereign Cloud

Microsoft is introducing new tools within its sovereign cloud offerings designed to give organizations tighter control over where their data resides, who can access it, and how critical systems operate, especially in light of AI becoming a bigger part of enterprise infrastructure.

In a recent announcement, Douglas Phillips, president and chief technology officer of Microsoft Specialized Clouds, outlined updates spanning public cloud, private cloud, and partner environments. The changes target governments, regulated industries, and organizations that need tighter control over sensitive workloads without abandoning cloud-based AI capabilities.

"Digital sovereignty should give organizations greater control and security over the technology they depend on, while continuing to innovate," Phillips wrote.

One of the biggest additions is Microsoft's Sovereign Control Panel, which will enter public preview at Microsoft Ignite. Available through the Azure portal, the tool will give IT administrators one place to manage data sovereignty settings, spot compliance issues, and identify potential risks that could disrupt operations.

Microsoft is also tightening controls over who can access sensitive systems. Its Data Guardian feature will require oversight by personnel based in Europe when Microsoft engineers access certain production systems in the European Union and European Free Trade Association. Another tool, Production Touch Ledger, will keep tamper-resistant records of that access, giving organizations a clearer picture of who accessed their systems and what they did.

Microsoft is also giving customers more control over encryption. External key management with Azure Managed HSM is now generally available, letting organizations keep their main encryption keys on hardware they own or manage outside Microsoft's cloud.

The company is also expanding its Sovereign Private Cloud offerings, giving organizations more options to run systems with or without an internet connection. New capabilities include GitHub Enterprise Local, GitHub Copilot with locally hosted AI models, and additional data services running on Azure Local.

The push for greater operational control comes as Microsoft is also reconsidering how enterprises should secure increasingly autonomous AI systems.

As reported by Redmond magazine, Microsoft is expanding Project Perception to address risks posed by what the company calls "agentic insiders," AI agents capable of accessing sensitive information and performing actions without constant human supervision.

That initiative introduces plans for Guardian Agents, specialized security agents designed to monitor other AI systems, evaluate their behavior and intervene when their actions become risky.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," said Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security.

Project Perception is about limiting what AI agents can do. Microsoft's sovereign cloud expansion tackles a different set of questions: where those systems run, who gets to administer them, and how the data stays protected.

Taken together, the two efforts point to a shift in Microsoft's enterprise AI strategy. The company is putting more weight on oversight, not just automation.

Phillips said AI is making these questions more pressing as organizations roll out intelligent systems across sensitive business processes and critical infrastructure.

#6977. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. Cisco says customers can extend the same access model to other infrastructure, including servers, Kubernetes clusters, and cloud resources.

#6976. This London Townhouse Was Designed for the Holidays

Every item on this page was chosen by an ELLE Decor editor. We may earn commission on some of the items you choose to buy.

Miles Redd and David Kaihoi created the ultimate English Christmas retreat for longtime clients—cozy, festive, and swathed in reds and greens.

Above: In the living room, the seating is by Luther Quintana with Samuel & Sons fringe. The wallcovering is by Iksel, and the cocktail table has a Fornasetti top.

Some homes are for summers by the water, others for weekends on the slopes. When Krystyna Houser set about redecorating her family’s London townhouse, she was thinking of Christmas. “We spend every Christmas there,” she says. At this house, that meant more than just putting up a tree.

Located near Richmond Park, in southwestern London, the 19th-century townhouse has been in Houser’s family for more than 20 years. It was always a place to return to while Houser, founder of the nutritional startup mBIOTA Elemental, and her husband Dan, a co-founder of Rockstar Games, were raising their three children in New York City. Dan had grown up nearby, and the ample greenspace that gives the area its name made it an appealing place to visit with a young family. The interiors, however, had never quite received their due.

Houser turned to Miles Redd and David Kaihoi, the partners behind the New York–based firm Redd Kaihoi. She had known Redd since 2012, when she hired him to decorate a Brooklyn Heights townhouse, and the two became friends. They have since worked on multiple houses together. For London she trusted the designers to craft the warm and celebratory atmosphere she craved. “I wanted it to be equally elegant, cozy, and family-oriented,” she says.

Red and green were a natural starting point, as green already had the family’s allegiance. “It’s one of their favorite colors,” Redd says. “So we leaned into that a lot.” Verdant hues appear in velvet, felt, and marble throughout, while the entrance hall is dressed in crimson. These were holiday colors with no intention of coming down in January, and Redd was going for sophistication rather than holiday kitsch. “I wanted romantic 19th-century elegance, the feeling that they’re stepping into a Merchant Ivory movie.”

This approach suits a firm known for exuberant color and layered furnishings. Redd brings a sense of theater to traditional decorating, drawing on the work of such legendary designers as Billy Baldwin, Albert Hadley, and John Fowler and mixing lacquer, chintz, and bold geometric patterns. Before opening his practice in 1998, he trained with antiques dealer John Rosselli and decorator Bunny Williams. Kaihoi, a sculptor, joined the ELLE Decor A-List firm in 2008 and became a partner in 2019. Together the duo give grand rooms an ease and wit that keep formality in check.

Houser considers “interior designer” an insufficient description for Redd and Kaihoi. “I feel like they are set curators,” she says. She gave them considerable freedom, occasionally inviting them to surprise her with potential purchases. “I don’t think it’s my place to hinder creative expression, and, as a result, the rooms are just magical.”

The house’s existing architecture gave that freedom some useful structure. “It had great English bones,” Redd says. He retained the original living room mantelpiece, library doors, and front door, while upstairs he and Kaihoi worked to restore character lost to earlier alterations that had stripped away 19th-century details. An excavation created additional basement space for a screening room and a ping-pong area, giving the family more places to gather and play.

From first visit to completion, the project took the better part of an unhurried decade, with pauses for permits, the Covid pandemic, and other commitments along the way. All the while Redd and Kaihoi kept shopping at auctions, antiques stores, and fairs, which ensured that the rooms would have an accumulated character. The designers looked to the guards at Buckingham Palace for the entrance hall and main staircase, borrowing the red and gold of their uniforms for its matka silk walls trimmed with gold braiding. Beyond the hall, the green velvet–lined library opens into a living room papered to resemble a coromandel screen.

Upstairs, the primary bedroom and a sitting area occupy a long, narrow space. Green felt screen dividers give the bed its own area in one corner without a wall breaking up the suite. “It’s all sort of one big bedsit, which is a very English notion,” Redd says. A small painting of a parrot on one of the screens keeps Houser company at her bedside.

The couple wanted their suite to feel like a luxurious hotel—inviting places to read and work, a comfortable bed, and a generously scaled bathroom—without sacrificing a sense of home. “You’ve ruined hotels for us,” Redd recalls Houser telling him of their previous work.

Step Inside the Ultimate Christmas Retreat

But Houser’s favorite rooms are the ones that bring everyone together: the kitchen and the adjoining striped family room, which open onto the garden. It’s here where everyone gathers for meals and to relax. The connected spaces provide separate places to eat, play games, and watch television, allowing the family to spend time together even when everyone is doing something different.

“Their design helps bring us together,” Houser says. “And that’s what it is. It’s a family home.”

#6972. Survey Finds 84% of Organizations Reported at Least One AI-Related Incident in the Past Year

Companies are running into problems as they give artificial intelligence more control over critical business applications, with 84% reporting at least one AI-related incident in the past year, according to a new survey from identity security firm Delinea.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Those gaps can make it difficult to identify segregation-of-duties conflicts, in which a person or agent has permissions across systems that should be kept separate.

Despite those issues, 95% of respondents said they were confident their access controls would withstand scrutiny in an external audit.

However, 26% reported access-related audit findings that had recurred in consecutive years, while 25% said some findings had remained unresolved for three or more years.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

Meanwhile, 60% reported an increase over the past two years in the number of critical applications operating outside the scope of access governance or Sarbanes-Oxley Act compliance.

The findings highlight the oversight challenges associated with giving AI agents authority to act within business systems, particularly when organizations cannot consistently identify who owns an agent or trace its actions.

Frank Vukovits, chief security scientist at Delinea, said organizations remain responsible for the actions AI agents take within their systems.

"When an agent can approve a transaction or change permissions, the business owns the outcome. You need to know what every agent can do, who owns it, and who approved it, before an auditor asks or your financials are misstated," Vukovits said.
