At trial, a jury held for the plaintiff’s advisor. She appealed to the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which reversed and ordered a new trial.

The plaintiff again appealed to the 6th Circuit, alleging that the district court incorrectly instructed the jury on what facts were relevant to its determination of whether she was an employee. She also claimed that the court abused its discretion in excluding certain evidence and that it improperly prohibited her from requesting economic damages in her closing argument.

Each of these claims failed, the 6th Circuit held.

The plaintiff forfeited her challenge to the court’s jury instructions because she failed to raise it, despite the district court specifically asking whether the parties had objections or edits to its instructions. To grant a third trial on this point “would be unfair to the court and the defendants,” the 6th Circuit said.

In her evidentiary argument, the plaintiff claimed that the district court improperly prevented her from submitting portions of a slideshow containing the results of an Ohio State campus climate survey on sexual assault. She argued that the presentation would have made her allegations more plausible.

However, the plaintiff failed to include the relevant slides in her appeal, leaving the 6th Circuit unable to conclude whether they had sufficient weight to affect the jury’s verdict. The appeals court also could not determine whether the slides offered anything “above and beyond” other testimony in the case.

In all, the plaintiff failed to show that any of her allegations amounted to unfair prejudice warranting a new trial.

Voters approved a 1% sales tax to help with funding. In an assessment, each school’s needs are weighed against what replacement would cost.

As it became clear in early November that Richmond voters would pass a 1% sales tax measure for school construction and renovation, Richmond Public Schools Superintendent Jason Kamras was grateful that residents would approve a tax increase even while households around the country are experiencing an affordability crisis.

“We are dealing with decades of deferred maintenance [and] disinvestment,” Kamras told Facilities Dive. “Our challenges are legion, which is why I think the voters so overwhelmingly supported the 1%.”

The district faces a roughly $680 million, 20-year backlog in maintenance, repair and upgrades to a portfolio of more than 40 schools, according to an independent assessment the agency conducted in 2024. Almost a quarter of the schools are more than 100 years old. About 70% are more than 50 years old. Only a handful are less than 15 years old.

Earlier this month, voters in Richmond were asked to decide whether to increase the sales tax by 1% to create a fund exclusively to build new public schools and renovate existing facilities. When the final votes were counted, 68% of the electorate voted in favor of the assessment, according to an Axios Richmond report.

Kamras says the actual need is even more than what the assessment shows — “well north of $1 billion,” he said.

With passage of the 1% assessment, the district could raise an estimated $850 million over the next 20 years. But that’s still not enough to meet all of the needs, Kamras said.

“I wish the sales tax would solve everything,” he said. “It's going to take us a lot farther, a lot faster, but it by itself does not solve all of our facilities' needs.”

The district currently gets about $2.5 million a year for facilities work, according to the school.

Adding to its challenges, the district fired its director of facilities earlier this year for improper use of funds and as of mid-November was operating with an interim director, according to the district’s website. The former facilities chief is alleged to have made personal purchases using his school district credit card, according to news reports.

“The investigation found direct evidence of asset misappropriation and unprofessional conduct” by the official, a school district investigation found, ABC 8 News reported in October.

Among the needs the district faces is a $938,000 roof repair at Woodville Elementary School, $2.5 million for brick wall repairs at John Marshall High School and $1.7 million for updated windows at Thomas Jefferson High School.

Mold, flood damage and HVAC issues are widespread, according to the assessment, which used a facility condition index to determine what each of the school’s needs are weighed against what it would cost to replace the school.

“The Facility Condition Index … is defined as the ratio of the cost of current needs divided by the current replacement value (CRV) of the facility,” the assessment says.

Schools that score between 0 and 5 are considered new or well-maintained, with little or no visual evidence of wear or deficiencies. Those with a score of between 5 and 10 show wear but are still in a serviceable and functioning condition. Those with a score of between 10 and 30 show signs of hard or long-term wear and are nearing the end of their useful or serviceable life. Those with a score of 30 and above have reached the end of their useful or serviceable life, making “renewal” necessary.

The Richmond City Council will need to pass an ordinance to enshrine the sales tax increase into law. The district has been using funds from a local meals tax to patch together funds for school construction dating back to 2018.

The result is that U.S. public schools face an $85 billion shortfall on facilities, the report found.

Mary Filardo, executive director of the 21st Century School Fund, would like to see the states and federal government do more to support school facilities across the country, she told Facilities Dive. She said the country should view its ailing school infrastructure similar to debt.

“You can not replace that leaky roof, but it's not going away. You still owe that building a roof,” she said.

Although the measure passed, the school district will need additional funding, said Ricard, noting that she expects the district to continue applying for grants.

#6921. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

#6887. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

#6822. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.

For customers using Cisco Identity Services Engine (ISE) and Terminal Access Controller Access-Control System Plus (TACACS+), the offering can draw on existing policy and device context to govern access to Cisco routers and switches. Cisco says customers can extend the same access model to other infrastructure, including servers, Kubernetes clusters, and cloud resources.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

The shift suggests that attackers are finding more value in carefully crafted scams that exploit existing business relationships and workplace routines.

Business e-mail compromise (BEC) remains particularly common, accounting for 51.2% of phishing e-mails analyzed in the report. Human resources, finance departments, and executive leadership are among the most frequently targeted groups.

Attackers also appear to be timing their campaigns around normal business activity. KnowBe4 found that 90.4% of phishing messages arrive Monday through Friday, with Monday accounting for the largest share at 20.6%.

One of the report's more troubling findings involves the growing use of legitimate technology platforms to deliver malicious content.

KnowBe4 found that phishing attacks were twice as likely to originate from legitimate infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter.

That includes AI services, where attackers are taking advantage of trusted domains to host fake developer consoles, login prompts, and credential-stealing pages. The report also identified a 228% increase in AI-brand impersonation attacks between March and August.

Because these malicious pages can operate on legitimate domains with valid security certificates, users may not see the usual warning signs associated with suspicious websites.

"For years, security advice has focused on a predictable checklist: Check the sender address, hover over the link, and look for the padlock," said Jack Chapman, senior vice president of threat intelligence at KnowBe4.

"While still foundational, that guidance is becoming less useful by the day."

The findings echo a recent report about management software and AI e-mail assistants. Research from Fortra and Barracuda showed how criminals are using legitimate administrative tools and hidden AI instructions to manipulate users and compromise systems.

Avantis Education, the creator of ClassVR, has introduced two new immersive learning libraries for its Eduverse+ platform: EscapeRooms, a math suite for K-8 students, and LingoLab, a Spanish and English language learning suite for students in grades 6-12.
