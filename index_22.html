#7156. The US ranks last in teacher pay worldwide

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

When comparing the teacher pay gap with over 30 countries, Brookings found that U.S. teachers only make two-thirds of what other college graduates earn.

Public school teacher pay in the U.S. lags significantly when compared to other industrialized countries, according to a late October analysis by the Brown Center on Education Policy at the Brookings Institution.

Examining average pay for teachers at the primary school level versus that of other college graduates across more than 30 countries, Brookings found the U.S. ranked last — and that its primary school teachers earn about two-thirds of what other college graduates are paid annually.

That pay gap is wider than what the Economic Policy Institute has found in its ongoing teacher pay penalty studies. According to a late August EPI report, U.S. teachers in 2025 earned about 25% less than college graduates in other professions.

In its report, Brookings described the U.S. teacher pay gap as “striking” and said that one possible explanation for the disparity may be widening income gaps in the U.S.

The teacher pay gap seems to widen particularly for teachers who fall in the middle-to-high range of the salary scale, according to Brookings.

Teacher salaries and benefits typically account as the largest expense in school district budgets.

But in examining federal data from the National Center for Education Statistics, Brookings found that the share of public school expenditures on teacher salaries has dropped over the last 100 years. For instance, the report said, teacher salaries made up 57% of public school expenditures in 1919, compared to 26% in 2019.

Brookings added that one explanation for this trend is that school districts have chosen to increase their staffing capacity instead of increasing teacher salaries.

“If you go back 100 years, more than half of school spending went to teacher salaries. Where the data ends — just before the start of the COVID-19 pandemic — the fraction going to teacher salaries had fallen to a quarter,” Brookings said. “Basically, schools are spending more money, just not on teacher salaries.”

The latest findings from Brookings come as some education policy experts have suggested schools should lean into strategic teacher pay approaches — especially as districts have to tighten their budgets due to challenges from widespread enrollment declines. Strategic teacher pay policies can include increasing teacher salaries in hard-to-staff schools and subjects, as well as boosting pay for high-performing teachers.

The National Education Association reported in April that the average teacher salary was $74,495 during the 2024-25 school year. Despite that being a 3.5% increase from the previous school year, the nation’s largest teacher’s union said, the latest teacher pay figures reflect a 5% decline in national averages compared to a decade ago when adjusting for inflation.

The overall student population shrank between the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years, but the number of students restrained and secluded rose.

The U.S. pre-K-12 public education system shrunk slightly, with fewer students, schools and school districts recorded in the 2023-24 school year compared to the 2021-22 school year, according to figures compiled by the U.S. Department of Education's Civil Rights Data Collection.

These trends align with ongoing patterns across the country as districts ramp up student recruitment efforts and consider school closures and consolidations to adjust to falling enrollments fueled by lower birthrates, competition from school choice, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

The 2023-24 civil rights data collection summary, released by the Education Department this fall, also includes statistics about discipline, restraint and seclusion, academic access and student characteristics.

The mandatory data collection takes place every two years. For the 2023-24 school year, the CRDC included data from more than 17,000 school districts and 97,000 schools nationwide. The Education Department may change data collection prompts between collection years or make other data collection or release changes. In releasing the 2023-24 civil right data this fall, the Education Department redacted data collected about nonbinary students’ experiences, such as enrollment, discipline, bullying and harassment, and whether districts have policies prohibiting the harassment or bullying of students on the basis of their gender identity.

Here, we break down what you need to know about the latest release of civil rights data.

About 5% of K-12 students — or 2.3 million — in 2023-24 received one or more in-school suspensions, which was the same percentage in 2021-22. About 5% of students in both school years also received one or more out-of-school suspensions.

Black boys and girls, Hispanic or Latino boys, White boys, and boys of two or more races in public schools were overrepresented in suspensions and expulsions in both the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years.

During the 2023-24 school year, students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act were also disproportionately disciplined — a trend also noted in the 2021-22 school year. Although students with disabilities comprised 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, they accounted for 24% of students who received one or more in-school suspensions, 27% of those who had one or more out-of-school suspensions, and 23% of those who were expelled.

More K-12 students were physically restrained, mechanically restrained or placed in seclusion at schools during the 2023-24 school year (129,700) compared to the 2021-22 school year (105,700). About 84,300 students were physically restrained and 32,300 students were secluded in 2023-24.

Overrepresented student subgroups that were restrained or secluded during the 2023-24 school year included boys, Black students, White students, students of two or more races, and students with disabilities.

For instance, Black students accounted for 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, while making up 19% of students secluded, 29% of students physically restrained, and 46% of students mechanically restrained.

Of the country's 27,200 high schools, three-quarters offered chemistry, 59% offered physics, 51% offered computer science, and 45% offered calculus in 2023-24, which is relatively on par with data reported for the 2021-22 school year.

For the 2023-24 school year, a slightly higher rate of high school boys — compared to high school girls — were enrolled in Algebra I, calculus, and physics. Boys were almost twice as likely to enroll in computer science compared to girls — at 11% compared to 6%. Girls, on the other hand, had slightly higher enrollment rates in advanced mathematics, biology and chemistry compared to boys.

High School students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act had lower enrollment rates in all these courses — except Algebra I — compared to their nondisabled peers.

About 3.1 million, or 18%, of high school students are enrolled in Advanced Placement courses and about 2.1 million high schoolers participated in dual enrollment or dual credit programs in 2023-24.

Public school pre-K-12 enrollment dipped slightly from 50 million in 2021-22 to 49.7 million in 2023-24. Of those students in 2023-24, 44% were White, 30% were Hispanic or Latino of any race, 15% were Black, 6% were Asian and 5% were two or more races.

Of the 2023-24 pre-K-12 student population, 15% were students with disabilities served under IDEA and 4% were students with disabilities served only under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. That's a slight increase from 2021-22, when 14% of students were served by IDEA and 3% of students were served under Section 504.

Twelve percent of students were English learners in 2021-22, compared to 11% in 2023-24.

Behind the wicker transformation is Wētā Workshop, the New Zealand design and effects company whose credits include The Lord of the Rings and Avatar. Its artists make the costumes, props, and creatures that give imaginary worlds something tangible—and, in this case, marriageable.

For prosthetic makeup designer Joe Dunckley and lead makeup effects artist Sarah Rubano, the assignment was to turn Skarsgård into a convincing wicker husband without losing the expressions that make him recognizable. The team spent 18 months developing and manufacturing his suit and facial prosthetics, translating his features into a weave, right down to his furrowed brows.

Naturally, we had questions. How do you make wicker sexy? What happens to the eyelashes at lunch? And could anyone involved look at a household basket the same way again? Below, Dunckley and Rubano explain the work behind the wicker.

Before we get to the cheekbones, how did this assignment arrive?

Dunckley: We were approached by Eleanor Wilson and Alex Huston Fischer about 18 months out from filming. Initially, they were after a piece of concept art—a beautiful piece that they could take to potential cast members. This was part of their development process, so they had a tool for helping show their vision for the project.

Pretty quickly after that came the question: How are you going to make this wicker husband character? So much of the film’s success hinges on the success of that character. They always wanted to make the wicker husband a physical prosthetic character. That led us into a process of research and development. It was an 18-month period of R&D and manufacture to get to the final product.

A husband made to order. What, exactly, was in the order?

Dunckley: About 95% of the prosthetic and fabricated suit is made out of a 3D-printed material. Most prosthetic materials are manufactured to imitate human skin, but the wicker husband is built of wicker, not flesh and bones, so we knew we needed something different.

Sometimes we found the limits of existing programs and had to write our own code for weaving. Weaving is such complex geometry that it was very challenging for sculptors to weave in 3D.

The prosthetic appliances were then printed directly, without needing to go through the mold-making process.

Rubano: On [Skarsgård’s] body, we had about 50 individual pieces stitched and anchored onto thin spandex or mesh. The face, including the wig, was about 10 individual pieces. We also used foam latex for the lips, eye bags, and underlip and chin piece.

Did anyone get to weave an actual basket, or was this strictly a digital operation?

Dunckley: Absolutely. We had a team of weavers who had just come off Avatar, where they’d been weaving costumes for the Na’vi. Our design team would block out the shapes that we liked, and our lead weaver would do a physical weave sample using flax, giving us those shapes.

We’d print, do a makeup test, and learn what we learned: This one’s too loose. This one’s too tight and it binds. We’ll need more movement here. Slowly, we developed what you ended up seeing in the film.

I had a crush on the wicker husband. How do you make wicker handsome?

Dunckley: One of our main objectives was to make sure Alexander shines through the makeup. He’s a handsome man, and he has an expressive quality that’s really unique and recognizable.

We wanted to get those qualities into the weave. It was a matter of deconstructing what it is about those expressions that are endearing, that are heart-melting, and then reconstructing them in a weave pattern and enabling that movement for him through the prosthetic.

How long did it take for Alexander Skarsgård to become the wicker husband every day of filming?

Rubano: We got the whole morning process down to an hour and a half. That was the brief at the beginning, because he was working every day. It was an ambitious shooting schedule, so all the build techniques supported being able to expedite it.

He would come in, and we would put on a partial fabricated undersuit. He would come to the makeup chair, then go back and get more of the body suit on. Then we got into gluing the appliances to his face, the eyelashes, the wig, the styling, the makeup around the eyes—all the finishing touches.

And at lunch? Surely even a wicker husband gets a break.

Rubano: About halfway through, once we were really confident with our processes, we started giving him a bit of relief at lunchtime. I would take off the eyelashes because they’re quite robust, and he’s not used to wearing fake eyelashes. We would wrap up his beard and put a little bib over it so he could eat and be comfortable. We’d take off the gloves—just all the things we could do to give him some reprieve. After lunch, we’d build him up 100% again. The cleanup at the end of the day was about half an hour. The real challenge was keeping him cool. He runs hot, so we had fans and ice packs.

How do you feel about all the online chatter about “sexy wicker”?

Dunckley: We never would have expected this kind of level, to be honest. It’s been quite a ride to watch the enthusiasm, all the excitement, all the memes. We sat around watching memes for hours and laughing our heads off.

Rubano: We’ve built so many different types of characters for so many different types of films over 30-plus years. Sometimes they strike a chord as hoped, sometimes they don’t. Sometimes the chord has been struck louder than you could ever have expected. This is an example of that.

Has this changed how you look at the wicker in your own home?

Rubano: I have this tiny little wicker basket, and it’s not necessarily sexy, but I do love it. I look at it with love and adoration. It’s just this tiny little basket I put all my jewelry bits and pieces in. But, of course, the hottest basket of all is Alexander Skarsgård. Definitely.

#7094. DOL final rule revamps antidiscrimination requirements for apprenticeships

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The U.S. Department of Labor issued a final rule to rescind “affirmative action requirements” for federally registered apprenticeships, including in education.

Education is the top occupation in the U.S. for degree-based apprenticeships, according to an August report by New America. Beyond teachers, K-12-related registered apprenticeship programs also include principals and lead teachers.

Registered Apprenticeship programs are meant to bridge the gap between employer needs and entry-level professionals, per the official federal apprenticeship fact sheet. What sets them apart is that they are industry-vetted and paid opportunities, they offer structured on-the-job learning and supplemental classroom education, and give participants portable credentials.

The programs span several industries, including agriculture, construction, education, energy, financial services, health care, hospitality, manufacturing, technology and transportation.

In the final rule, DOL summarized public commenters’ concerns with the new regulation’s potential effect on educator apprenticeship programs.

Specifically, one commenter said that students need access to teachers who they can relate to, who can “affirm their identities,” and who can “teach with an understanding of the cultural context of their lives,” according to DOL. Otherwise, the commenter said, students “may feel invisible,” and their learning outcomes may suffer when the teaching workforce is "homogeneous."

DOL noted that another commenter said the rule would “restrict active and expansive recruitment efforts that address gaps in the educator workforce.”

In response to those comments, DOL said the final rule reduces “prescriptive Federal mandates” that will provide apprenticeship sponsors — including in the education sector — greater flexibility to design their programs, such as with outreach and recruitment strategies.

“The Department acknowledges the important role that educator apprenticeship programs can play in fortifying the teaching workforce,” DOL’s response said. “The Department’s final rule does not prohibit sponsors from engaging in lawful recruitment efforts.”

More broadly, DOL “expects that removing these obligations will significantly reduce administrative burdens for sponsors,” and encourage further adoption of Registered Apprenticeship programs, the final rule document said.

Since 2024, DOL has continued to deregulate apprenticeship programs. DOL originally proposed a rule in December 2023 seeking to “modernize” apprenticeship programs. At the time, then-Acting Secretary of Labor Julie Su said in a press release, “Equity and job quality have marked the most successful Registered Apprenticeship programs for workers and employers alike. This proposed rule codifies the Department of Labor’s strong commitment to these principles.”

#7045. Report: AI's Productivity Gains in Science Tempered by Time Spent Validating Outputs

Artificial intelligence is saving scientists significant amounts of time, but researchers are giving some of those gains back by checking the technology's work, according to a new study involving Google, Google DeepMind, and MIT. The findings add a scientific-workflow dimension to a much broader debate over unreliable, misaligned, and increasingly autonomous AI systems — without establishing that the more dramatic forms of AI misbehavior are occurring in scientific research.

Rather than the current, raging debate about "rogue AI" systems deliberately escaping control, deceiving scientists, or sabotaging experiments, the researchers are documenting something more immediate: AI output that requires auditing and verification, concerns about growing quantities of low-quality research, and a research pipeline in which faster hypothesis generation can run ahead of human and physical capacity to validate the results. The paper cites worries that AI-generated content, hallucinations, and what prior researchers have called illusions of understanding could strain peer review and validation.

The September paper, AI in Science: Early Insights, combines three main empirical sources: approximately 15 million anonymized Gemini interactions, an inventory of 2,690 specialized scientific AI models, and a survey of 637 active scientists in the United States and United Kingdom. Nearly half of the surveyed scientists reported using some form of AI every day, and researchers who reported net time savings averaged just under seven hours a week.

Google separately highlighted the findings in its Sept. 15 AI & Economy ATLAS update. The company emphasized both the productivity gains and the counterweight: Scientists were spending significant time validating AI output, accumulating hypotheses that still needed testing, and encountering bottlenecks in physical experimentation and clinical validation. Google said those constraints mean faster individual tasks may not immediately translate into more discoveries.

Productivity findings are substantial. Around three-quarters of surveyed researchers reported saving time through AI, and the average reported net saving was almost seven hours per week. Researchers said much of that time went back into research. The paper also reports that about 68% perceived greater access to insights from other disciplines, while roughly 65% said AI had increased the breadth of their research agendas.

But the authors found that checking AI output consumes a meaningful share of those gains. Among scientists who reported saving time, about 89% said more than 10% of the saved time went into verifying, debugging, or fact-checking AI output. About 46% said more than one-quarter of their saved time went to that work. #7015. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

Above: The kitchen in designer Phillip Thomas’s Bellport retreat.

Do the words “antique” and “trend” really belong in the same sentence? In many ways, a great antique shop feels like the antidote to trend fatigue: a space where the true must-haves are hidden in dusty corners, with part of the fun in the thrill of the hunt. Luckily for the amateur antiquer, not all trending vintage styles require a substantial space commitment or glamorous provenance—although an interesting backstory helps. “Antiques are always about a story: where you found them, who owned them, and how that story will continue on after us,” Cullum says.

Ahead, we asked top designers and experts to share the most inspiring antique trends of the moment. From neoclassic mirrors to hand-painted chests paired with modern art, their takes are sure to inform your next estate sale rummage, or inspire you to restyle an old heirloom.

We called mirrored surfaces a major trend to watch for fall 2026, and antique trends have followed suit. Unexpected (or better yet, artfully manipulated) reflections can add rich dimension and movement to any space, but in this category, antiques create a window to the past that’s tough to beat.

“One of the easiest ways to add a sense of history to a room is to include an antique mirror,” says Drake. “Whether you choose a sober and strict neo-classic piece or a more flamboyant baroque one, reflections of the past add depth and sparkle.”

“One trend I’m observing is an uptick in antique dowry chests,” antiques dealer Ali Mahon says. From Pennsylvania Dutch to Swedish Folk and Spanish carved, she’s noticed clients gravitating toward more delicately adorned storage over spare. With thoughtfully mixed furniture (from style to period) becoming more desirable than matching sets, Mahon sees more hand-painted antique surfaces in our future: “With their decorative tulips, leaves, birds, and hearts, dowry chests create a colorful splash in any room.”

ELLE Decor Rising Star Reagan Gail Struble agrees—although delicate designs are not a requirement of the trend, in her book: “Something about a playful wash of color on an awesome old piece of furniture is very alluring, especially when you can see the age and texture of the wood peeking through,” she says. “Hand-painted case pieces add a little punch of charm to any room.”

Luckily, these antiques can be just as functional as they are fashionable. Mahon recommends using a painted chest as a coffee table, at the foot of the bed, or in a space that could benefit from a bit more romance (and a few extra drawers). According to ELLE Decor A-List designer Nick Olsen, Art Deco pieces are setting the tone for 2027 antique trends—although, if you ask him, they were never out. “I'm particularly inspired by the [art deco influence in the] Gujarat palace in India,” he says. “I'm fascinated by how every element seems to have a wide border: marble floors, carpets, walls of veneer and mohair velvet upholstery.”

According to Olsen, using Art Deco design compositions can do as much for a room as actual pieces from the period. “I like the idea of contrasting borders: solid textures punctuated by smaller graphic prints on furniture and more delicate lighting,” he explains. And while borders are easy to create with paint, paneling, and carpet seaming, Olsen challenges clients to push the Art Deco agenda further by bordering with luxe materials.

Whether you’re new to antiquing or simply trying to add more character to a space, lighting is an excellent place to start. “Lighting is the jewelry of the home, so it’s one of my favorite places to bring in something vintage,” says A-Lister Corey Damen Jenkins. “An Art Deco sconce or an antique brass lantern has a warmth to it that’s hard to find in something brand new.”

Those are the pairings that make people stop and look twice.” Drake adds that seemingly out-of-place seating is an easy way to add intrigue. “I love including antique chairs as sculptural notes, contrasted against modern upholstery and tables,” he says.

But when it comes to antiquing, there’s really only so much one can plan for. Letting a great find lead the way often turns out to be a better strategy than arriving to an estate sale with a rigid checklist. The best way to keep up with top designers when it comes to antiquing this year? Find a piece that has something to say, place it where it seems to belong, and then try it where it doesn’t.

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#6969. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

#6963. Microsoft Expands AI Security Platform to Address Agentic AI Security Risks

Microsoft is expanding the scope of its AI-powered Project Perception security platform to address the growing concern that AI agents may have access to sensitive information and systems, yet don't always behave as expected.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.
