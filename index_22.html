President Donald Trump also fired every member of SBE’s governing board, who plaintiffs said had been kept in the dark about the agency’s future since he retook office almost two years ago.

Their lawsuit argued that NSF breached the APA by failing to consider the outcome of doing away with SBE — namely, that the agency would be unable to fulfill its mandate to “initiate and support basic scientific research and programs” in the social sciences.

Each year, SBE distributes some $247 million in grants for research into the social, behavioral and economic sciences — nearly two-thirds of all federal grants in these fields, according to NSF.

“Without that funding, hundreds of promising research projects directed to the pressing problems of our time — from the use and effects of artificial intelligence to child development in the age of social media — will never proceed,” the lawsuit said. The plaintiffs also said NSF has given no official rationale for why it decided to extinguish the directorate.

In response to questions from The Atlantic, a White House spokesperson said in a May statement that the Trump administration “is committed to cementing America’s dominance in cutting-edge technologies of the future — innovation that is being driven by advancements in hard sciences, not in ideologically-driven ‘social sciences.’”

But the administration never said why it is labeling entire fields such as psychology and economics as “ideologically driven,” the plaintiffs said in their lawsuit Monday. And even if it were to explain, they argued, the rationale would directly conflict with the statutes that created NSF, “which repeatedly direct the Foundation to support the social sciences.”

All of this has occurred with a significant power vacuum at the top levels of NSF. The agency has been without a permanent leader for more than a year and a half, after its last director, Sethuraman Panchanathan, resigned in April 2025. It is also without a deputy director.

Both positions require a presidential appointment and Senate confirmation. Trump’s pick for NSF director, former U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Deputy Secretary Jim O'Neill, has not been given a confirmation hearing.

NSF Chief of Staff Brian Stone, a defendant in Monday’s lawsuit, is currently performing the duties of the agency’s director. The vast majority of NSF's significant cuts and the corresponding tumult occurred during Stone’s tenure. He is set to retire at the end of the year.

“NSF cannot shut down the government’s largest source of funding for an entire scientific sector without a Director and without consulting the body Congress created to make such Decisions,” Monday’s lawsuit said. “And it certainly cannot do so with no explanation.”

Alongside two named college instructors, the organizations suing NSF are the Federation of Associations in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, the Society of Experimental Social Psychology, and the Social & Affective Neuroscience Society. The plaintiffs are represented by the advocacy nonprofit Democracy Forward.

#7202. Broward schools ‘no longer the only game in town’ as enrollment drops

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The Florida district is doubling down on student recruitment and retention by stepping up marketing and engagement efforts.

This is the second installment of a three-part series on the impact of private school choice on public school enrollment.

When Valerie Wanza started as a teacher in Florida's Broward County Public Schools in 1992, the district was at the beginning of what would be explosive growth in student population over the next decade.

Throughout the 1990s and early 2000s, Broward County Public Schools saw such an increase in enrollment that by 2004, more than 100 of its 315 schools were deemed "critically overcrowded."

Now, 34 years later as the district's chief strategy and innovation officer, Wanza is part of a team of administrators working to turn around an enrollment decline that began in the 2015-16 school year. The student population plummeted by 17.5% over the next decade and is forecast to continue decreasing through at least the 2030-31 school year, according to district data.

The district attributed the drop to various factors: lower birth rates leading to fewer children entering schools, rising housing costs that make it harder for families to live in the desirable area bordering the Atlantic Ocean, fewer immigrant students due to shifts in federal policies, and growing competition from private school choice options.

And with the area's school choice proliferation, Wanza said, Broward County Public Schools is "no longer the only game in town."

Now, to stay financially healthy, the district is taking a comprehensive approach that includes consolidating and closing some school campuses, cutting at least 1,000 staff positions, and implementing other measures to save money. It's also attempting to boost enrollment by vigorously recruiting new students through media campaigns, personalized phone calls, and hosting events to drum up interest in and excitement for the district's schools.

Broward is even turning to an outside vendor for help. Earlier this year, the district approved a one-year contract with student recruitment firm Caissa K12 to help connect with families who have left its public schools, determine their reasons for doing so, attempt to resolve those concerns and inform them about the district's benefits and schooling options.

The performance-based contract said Broward County Public Schools will pay Caissa K12 $850 per recruited student who enrolls in a district school, up to 176 students or a maximum payment of $150,000.

“If we aren’t real about, No. 1, recognizing what adjustments we need to make, and then be bold enough to make them, we’re going to become the Kmart … of the public schools,” Wanza said, referring to the once-dominant discount retailer that filed for bankruptcy in 2002 amid growing competition and other pressures.

The enrollment decline that Broward County Public Schools is experiencing mirrors the challenges faced by many other school districts across the country.

While falling birth rates and rising school choice are often to blame, the multitude of factors can be complex and specific to each region's ability to draw new residents with young families. Nationally, enrollment in traditional public schools is expected to drop from 54.7 million in 2021 to 49.4 million in 2035, according to a 2025 analysis from the Brookings Institution based on U.S. Census and other federal data.

On the other hand, some states are predicted to see increases in public school student populations between fall 2022 and fall 2031. Among those are Idaho (+11%), North Dakota (+7.4%) and Tennessee (+2%), according to data from the U.S. Department of Education.

Enrollment in Florida's public schools is projected to increase by 3% over the same time period.

From 2015-16 through 2025-26, Broward County Public Schools lost 17.5% of its student population, or 38,640 students. That translated to a loss of $352 million in per-pupil revenue over that 11-year span. In fiscal year 2026, which ended June 30, the district expected to show an $80 million shortfall because of continued enrollment loss.

The district has already closed seven schools since the 2025-26 school year and is exploring other closures, consolidations and grade configurations for the future. New data released this fall shows district enrollment dropped by another 10,272 for the 2026-27 school year.

Broward leaders are carefully analyzing data that can explain why families aren't enrolling their students in county schools. Joseph Beck, the district's director of demographics and enrollment planning, said his team is constantly coming up with ways to look at the data differently to better understand why students are coming or going.

For instance, the data shows that about 20,000 students, on average, withdraw from Broward County Public Schools annually. That number, which doesn't include graduating seniors, has been consistent over many years, Beck said.

The district also investigates how many new students enroll each year, a figure that can be affected by immigration policy changes. For instance, the district had 3,400 fewer newcomer students in 2025-26 than it did the previous year, Beck said.

The data also shows that 1,700 students enrolled in the 2024-25 school year transferred to a private school the following year using a Florida Family Empowerment Scholarship to help pay tuition.

The decision to close 129-year-old Perry Elementary School in Belvidere, Ill., earlier this year devastated many community members who didn't want to see their historic building shuttered.

Its aging structure needed a prohibitive $14 million to $16 million in repairs, according to Cassandra Schug, superintendent of Belvidere Community Unit School District 100.

The district's enrollment has in fact remained relatively stable, with only a modest decline in recent years, Schug says. Enrollment stands at 7,348 students in 2026, down from 7,470 in 2021.

Still, the decision to close Perry and the reaction it engendered from the community offer constructive guidance for administrators everywhere who are staring down school closures brought on by shrinking student populations.

When Perry's closure became inevitable after a 6-1 school board vote in April, the community rallied to usher its beloved school out in style, Schug says. Longtime teacher Susan Thomas spearheaded a committee to honor the school’s legacy with a citywide celebration ultimately attended by hundreds of people in May.

Thomas, who taught 5th grade at Perry for 19 years, says the neighborhood school was “so much more” than just a building. The closure decision, she says, was nothing short of traumatic.

Perry “has been a cornerstone for Belvidere. It needed to be celebrated. It needed us to be able to show our appreciation for what it had meant for so many years, and I just wanted it to be done right,” Thomas says.

The celebration allowed people across the community to visit and view old yearbooks, photos, letters, newspaper articles and student projects — all organized by decade and displayed on tables and walls throughout the gym.

“So you could walk through and just see all of that Perry history and recognize how education had changed over the years, how the building had changed over the years, and again, just really spend time sort of saying goodbye,” Schug says.

The celebration “helped us all to process what had been a really difficult time," honor the school's legacy, "create that closure, and give us a chance to celebrate those positive memories that Perry had brought to the community.”

Beginning this school year, Perry's staff members and students dispersed to other schools in the district, with students transferring through a lottery system.

Though Belvidere enrollment is expected to stay steady, district officials are carefully watching enrollment numbers and projections, especially as more public schools nationwide experience declines, Schug says.

And, she adds, she hopes she never has to close another school in her career again.

As some school communities grieve shuttered schools, districts are proactively working to boost enrollments and stave off closures. Here are three examples of how school leaders are striving to stay competitive.

The days of traditional public school systems having a monopoly on education and just worrying about teaching students are gone, says Brian Stephens, CEO of Caissa K12, a Memphis-based strategic communications firm.

In 2009, Stephens co-founded a public strategy firm that specialized in complex communication crisis management. But in 2017, as he saw more states adopt private school choice programs, the firm converted to focusing exclusively on student recruitment and retention for traditional public schools.

Currently, Caissa K12 — named for the mythical goddess of chess and strategy — works with nearly 100 districts in 26 states. The company says it has helped recruit about 55,000 students for its clients through research, communications, events and knocking on doors.

The firm is one of a growing number of players in this space.

Taking a page from the retail industry, one of Caissa K12's services for its district clients is performing "secret shopper" outreach. Caissa K12 employees or contractors act as families interested in enrolling their child and visit or connect with public and private schools to look for enrollment or communication barriers or other customer service problems.

The company works with school districts including Florida's Broward County Public Schools through performance contracts, meaning Caissa K12 only gets paid if the company meets an agreed-upon student recruitment goal.

When it comes to recruiting and retaining students, Stephens says, the greatest mistakes public schools make are not advocating for themselves and not being willing to improve flaws like hard-to-navigate websites.

Those steps may sound easy, but they are difficult for school systems with a lot on their plates, he says.

If the government and the people want public schools to "act like a business instead of a public good, then we have to act more like a business," Stephens says. "That means we need to advocate for ourselves."

Districts that are innovative and want to build trust with families "absolutely have to reach out and let people know that they exist and let people know how awesome they are," he says.

Stephens points to creative approaches public schools can take to help attract and keep students.

One district client, he notes, installed a washer and dryer at a school to support school attendance by making sure children could have clean clothes. Other schools have provided feminine hygiene products, which also boosted school attendance, Stephens says.

Eliminating outdated and abrasive policies can make a difference too. For instance, schools need to remove attendance policies that punish students for having overdue library books or unpaid fees for band uniforms, he says.

“Stepping up those things has really helped. You have to identify what the issue is that's stopping a child from attending.”

Elsewhere, public school advocates from Iowa to Denver are also making plans to direct some of these taxpayer contributions to public schools.

And at least one new national scholarship-granting organization has formed to funnel donations to services supporting public school students. The Future School Fund, founded by Deborah Gist, former Rhode Island commissioner of education and state education superintendent in the District of Columbia, intends to help schools identify programs where scholarship funds can be used.

The federal Education Freedom Tax Credit program, which launches on Jan. 1, will guide taxpayer donations to students attending either public or private schools, who can then use the scholarships to pay for allowable educational expenses.

This first-ever nationwide school choice program is projected to raise nearly $26 billion annually for about 2.2 million scholarships by 2030, according to the administering agencies, the U.S. Department of the Treasury and the IRS.

This could overshadow the nation's current largest education program, Title I, which has an $18.4 billion budget for fiscal year 2026 budget to aid children at low-income schools. Title I serves about 26 million students in 90% of the country's school districts, according to the Bipartisan Policy Center.

Only students living in states that opt into the Education Freedom Tax Credit program would be eligible to receive scholarships. As of Oct. 22, 30 states have indicated that they will opt into the program, according to the IRS.

Participating states will approve scholarship-granting organizations to operate in their states, which will then direct taxpayer donations to families to use for education expenses.

ACE Scholarships is one such SGO. The Denver-based nonprofit is working with public and private school systems in several states interested in soliciting donations for the federal tax credit scholarships that can then be used at their schools, according to CEO Norton Rainey.

ACE Scholarships already has several charter school clients and is in talks with public school districts to manage and distribute their donation accounts, he says. The organization, he adds, will focus on directing scholarships to students with the greatest financial needs.

But while some are urging public school systems and supporters to embrace the federal scholarship program, critics have many concerns. They worry about a lack of accountability, a financial drain on public schools and potential inequities.

"Simply permitting some public school students to benefit does not ensure that resources are equitably distributed or that schools and students most in need are served by the program," according to a coalition of 16 civil rights advocacy organizations in a Sept. 28 letter to state governors.

The rapid increase in school choice options is leading more public school leaders to consider creative ways to invest in marketing to keep their enrollment numbers up, says Mellissa Braham, associate director of the National School Public Relations Association.

But that doesn’t mean they necessarily have the budgets to do so, Braham says.

“Because districts nationwide are facing really tough times right now, a lot of them are having to reduce staff, and in that light, it's painful to put additional focus on marketing, even though ultimately it does help your budget,” she says.

One way districts are approaching enrollment marketing in a small yet impactful way is by having dedicated in-house staff solicit student and teacher testimonials and create videos to post on their communication channels.

Other low-cost marketing strategies include paying to boost social media posts or have their school or district website appear on search engines more frequently instead of relying on organic search, Braham says. Some districts are paying for targeted marketing emails, too.

She points to successful elements she's seen in school marketing campaigns, including firsthand testimonials from students and families, a focus on what makes a school and district unique, and targeted emails that feel specific to the person receiving it.

But districts in highly competitive areas may need to invest in more costly marketing efforts, she says. That could include buying billboard, television or streaming ads.

“If you're in a really competitive environment with a lot of different schools to choose from, it might be an investment you have to make with the ultimate return of enrollment, which brings in funds, which allows you to support staff programs and services,” Braham says.

In districts where school choice options are fairly new, Braham says, sometimes the biggest challenge for school leaders is to understand that families are now customers and that neighborhood public schools are not the only option.

Shifting to a marketing mindset is different from sending out regular school communications, which aim to share information and build relationships, she says. And that change is “new for a lot of public schools.”

#7161. Is private school choice a ‘tipping point’ for school closures?

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

As declining birth rates drive enrollment challenges, district leaders and public school advocates worry that school choice programs will further strain budgets.

This is the first installment of a three-part series on the impact of private school choice on public school enrollment.

That's the message families across the country are hearing from both public and private schools as competition for students heats up in the wake of declining birth rates and the rapid growth of taxpayer-supported private school choice. And while private schools are used to marketing their offerings, it's a new world for public schools that historically were the first — and most often only — choice for the great majority of families.

Now school districts large and small are scrambling for students as they watch their enrollments fall. They are boosting academic programs, giving personalized tours, and analyzing data on why students stay or go as they face greater rivalry with private schools. All of this is adding strain to already cash-strapped public schools as government funding is tied to enrollment. Throughout the U.S., public schools are closing or consolidating because they simply don't have enough students to fill their classrooms.

When Pflugerville Independent School District’s board of trustees in May approved closing four elementary schools in the 2027-28 school year, the district’s superintendent said a $20 million budget shortfall made the decision necessary.

The closures are expected to save the Texas district $15 million to $16 million a year as it grapples with declining enrollment alongside rising or fixed costs, said Pflugerville Superintendent Quintin Shepherd. Between the 2019-20 and 2025-26 school years, enrollment fell 5% from 26,400 students to 25,046, according to district data.

And Pflugerville ISD may face more difficult decisions about its budget and school consolidations in the future, district officials fear.

All of these things, we have to respond to simultaneously ... It sort of feels like an avalanche.

Superintendent, Pflugerville Independent School District

The district’s enrollment has dropped for a variety of reasons, Shepherd said, including a decline in birth rates, challenges with local housing affordability, changing demographics, charter school growth, virtual school options, migration patterns, open enrollment — and more recently a $1 billion universal statewide school choice program in Texas that’s rolling out this school year.

“All of these things, we have to respond to simultaneously,” Shepherd said. “It sort of feels like an avalanche.”

The state projected in April that Pflugerville ISD’s enrollment could drop as much as 1.6% — or by 389 students — in the 2026-27 school year directly because of the state's new Texas Education Freedom Accounts. That would amount to a loss of $4.3 million in state funds for the district.

“This is where the voucher conversation becomes so darn important,” Shepherd said.

While not all enrollment declines are driven by vouchers, he said, for those districts "already facing demographic challenges, the vouchers could become a tipping point. And that tipping point is what’s going to trigger the avalanche.”

For Pflugerville ISD specifically, Texas’ new school choice program “could become another tipping point,” Shepherd said.

“I think it puts everything back on the table for us,” he added. “Potential future closures, potential program cuts, and so on and so forth. It’s that our system has got to remain flexible and adaptive to the situation and the context as it continues to evolve.”

As enrollment challenges continue for public school districts nationwide, school closures have steadily increased in recent years.

While 755 schools closed in the 2021-22 school year, the number jumped to 1,011 in the 2024-25 school year, according to the National Center for Education Statistics. That seems to signal a return to school closure trends from before the aberration of COVID-19 pandemic times. For instance, NCES data shows 1,122 closures in 2018-19.

However, the national data on school closures is limiting, said Marguerite Roza, director of the Edunomics Lab and research professor at Georgetown University, as it does not yet capture the spike in school closures over the past two school years. During that time, she said, closures appear to have drastically picked up mostly due to sweeping birthrate declines.

Even where you see a lot of school choice, a lot of the vouchers are going to kids who are already in private schools.

School choice policies may be a variable that is impacting closures in certain communities, Roza said, but they are not the primary factor driving school closures. The key underlying issue is declining birth rates, she said.

“Even where you see a lot of school choice, a lot of the vouchers are going to kids who are already in private schools,” Roza said.

At the same time, some district leaders, public school advocates and researchers worry that large statewide school choice programs like those in Arizona, Florida and now Texas, could make matters worse and speed up public school closures.

Between 2022 and 2031, public school enrollment is expected to fall nationally by 5.5% — or 2.7 million students, according to an April analysis of NCES data by the Education Commission of the States. That enrollment loss would lead to a widespread decline in state revenue for public schools of $11.5 billion a year by the 2030-31 school year, projects a separate report by Bellwether and WestEd.

The ongoing enrollment drops are largely due to decreasing birth rates, the two education nonprofits said. But contributing factors include a “national slowdown in immigration,” families migrating out of state in some places, and growing interest in private school and homeschooling fueled by school choice initiatives.

Private schools, meanwhile, are not immune to the enrollment challenges and pressures from school choice competition. For instance, of 5,829 member schools of the National Catholic Educational Association, 62 closed or consolidated during the 2025-26 school year, and only 13 schools opened, for a net loss of less than 1% of the total number of NCEA member schools.

The competitive pressure public schools are feeling from private school choice programs will likely intensify as the first federal school choice program available nationwide launches next year. The massive and controversial Education Freedom Tax Credit program is expected to fund 2.2 million taxpayer-supported student scholarships totaling $26 billion each year by 2030, according to the IRS, which along with the U.S. Department of Treasury where it is housed, is administering the program.

Only students living in states that opt into the program will be eligible to receive funds through a scholarship-granting organization. According to the IRS, 30 states indicated they will participate as of Oct. 22. The Treasury Department issued temporary and proposed rules for the program Oct. 1.

Opponents consider the program a threat to public school funding that will only benefit wealthy families. Supporters, on the other hand, say that because scholarships can go toward both private school tuition and public school expenses, the program will expand educational opportunities and funding for all students. Covered public school expenses can include tutoring, ed tech and services for students with disabilities.

"Everyone is a winner in the education freedom tax credit, whether it's a private school or it's a public school," said Norton Rainey, CEO of ACE Scholarships, a Denver-based nonprofit scholarship-granting organization.

But Augustus Mays, vice president of partnerships and engagement at EdTrust, said taxpayer funds shouldn't be “funneled” to private schools, which often don't accept all applicants. Instead, federal tax credit dollars should go into public funding streams like Title I and the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act that “support all students,” he said during a forum hosted by Sen. Mazie Hirono, D-Hawaii, on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., on Sept. 22.

“This is not free money or a targeted investment in students with the greatest needs,” Mays said. “It is a[n] uncapped federal tax subsidy steering public resources toward private education with few safeguards.”

Meanwhile, public school advocates and researchers have said a majority of state private school choice program funds in Arizona, Florida and Texas have primarily benefited students already enrolled in private school or being homeschooled.

While that may mean there hasn’t been a “mass exodus” of public school students moving to private schools, even small groups transferring out can still “meaningfully impact” public schools — especially when enrollment is already slipping because of declining birth rates, said Joshua Cowen, a professor of education policy at Michigan State University.

When a state obligates more of its budget to expand voucher programs, he said, over time the state won’t be able to keep public school revenue above rising inflation costs.

“That leaves districts on their own at the local level, which leads to closure if you’re underenrolled or you’re just under financial distress," Cowen said. “So there’s like a downstream effect of all this.”

In the 2011-12 school year, Arizona implemented the nation’s oldest education savings account program. The state also became one of the first to expand its private school choice program to include all eligible students, beginning in the 2022-23 school year.

Education savings accounts, or ESAs, are funded from taxpayer dollars and can be used by parents for various educational purposes, including private school tuition, school transportation and tutoring. ESAs differ from vouchers, which give eligible families a portion of taxpayer-supported funding to pay for private school expenses.

Arizona’s school choice program’s participation boomed as more students gained eligibility, jumping from 12,000 students in 2021-22 to 88,000 by 2024-25, according to a Rand Corp. analysis published in November 2025. By the 2024-25 school year, about 7% of students in the state had an education savings account for private school tuition and other educational expenses.

“I do think some students likely experience ‘science anxiety’ for a variety of reasons,” said Christine Royce, who’s also an education professor at Shippensburg University and co-director of the master’s in STEM education program.

One proximate cause is the high-stakes assessment system in the U.S., which instills a belief that there’s always one right answer to any given question, Royce said.

“If you’re looking at strategies to try to reduce science anxiety, one of the best things teachers can do is start to normalize the idea that productive struggle is part of the process,” she said. “They can learn from mistakes and move forward. To strengthen that link, science teachers should help students recognize how what they’re learning about in the classroom is connected to the real world, Royce said.

“If they have a more grounded understanding of what’s going on because they can see it as part of their environment, they can see it as concrete, they will have a better ability to work through the connections,” she said. “If there’s no connection, students are, perhaps, guessing at answers."

On the other hand, "if there’s a connection, they’re using the information they already know to make predictions and draw conclusions,” Royce said.

Another method Royce recommends for helping students better understand science and reduce their anxiety around the topic is to encourage working with peers or in groups, to develop a wider notion of potential discoveries and answers they could gin up.

The dialogue between peers can help students make sense of what they're learning, she said. “That also helps them to realize that it’s OK to struggle. Phillip Thomas’s Bellport retreat is a laboratory for color, collecting, and the occasional improbable antique.

Phillip Thomas’s first shop had a captive clientele. He called the enterprise Phillip & Co. (“There was no Co.,” he says. It was just him.) His customers, he recalls, were more or less obligated to buy something.

The Phillip & Co. space is now a pantry for extra tableware, but Thomas is still rearranging the house around it. His family has owned the late-19th-century home for 40 years, and the New York–based interior designer returns almost every weekend. The four-bedroom house includes two porches, a sunny sitting room overlooking the bay, and a living room anchored by a large fireplace. Thomas still sleeps in his childhood bedroom when there, and his parents, siblings, nieces, and friends gather at the property regularly, too.

Thomas’s Manhattan apartment is white and contemporary, filled with Latin American art. Bellport called for something different: “an American cottage by the sea,” he says. “I really just want to feel comfortable and I want everyone around me to feel at ease.” Getting there has involved decades of experimentation, a few unlikely purchases, and regular consultation with a formidable client committee: his family. “It’s a democracy,” he says. “It’s not a dictatorship.”

His love of Bellport extends well beyond the borders of his family’s property. The South Shore village has long been a mecca for creatives. The South Bay Art Association held its first official meeting at the village’s community center in 1957. Thomas, who has been coming to Bellport since he was four, remembers spending time in that same building as a child. In 2019, he led the renovation of its basement bowling alley and recreation space through the Bellport Village Program Fund.

There, he took Long Island’s Great South Bay as a starting point, introducing blue floors punctuated with red circles that evoked buoys, which village workers helped paint. The project restored a childhood gathering place for another generation, with color doing much of the work on a limited budget. It is a public expression of the instincts that have shaped his family’s house: keep what matters, reconsider what no longer works, and make the result inviting enough that people will use it.

At home, those decisions are rarely final. The dining room has gone from lacquered hunter green to red to its current painted pattern, where undulating lines surround a large, exuberantly colored painting. Elsewhere, Thomas used stencils to give walls the irregularity he seeks out in handmade things. “Having something painted onto the walls gives the room soul,” he says. “There is perfection in imperfection.”

The collections have accumulated on similar terms. Antique tools, gathered with his father over decades, line a hallway in carefully ordered arrangements. Thomas admires them as objects made by hand to facilitate other handmade things; displayed together, their worn wooden forms are sculptural. In the powder room, a coal-mining cart found a second career as a basin, paired with a vintage mirror frame fitted with glass cut to accommodate the plumbing. “You’d be surprised what I’ll put in the back of my car,” he says.

Other pieces traveled even farther. A campy painting of dogs playing poker came with his mother’s family from Yugoslavia to northern Chile, then to Santiago. After his grandmother died, the family agreed it belonged in Bellport, where it hangs in Thomas’s studio. The family’s routines shift with the seasons, which in turn shape how they use the house. Winter draws everyone toward the living-room fireplace, where the sofa is covered in a Schumacher fabric reminiscent of an American rag rug and throw pillows are made from textiles Thomas found in Turkey. From May through October, life moves out to the porch, where varied flexible seating facilitates long dinners and several conversations at once.

For the porch fireplace, Thomas collaborated with local artist Jay Sylvester on a sculpture of foraged branches surrounding a bull’s-eye mirror. The reflection ensures that even someone seated with their back to the bay gets a view.

Even the garden has benefited from his instinct to collect. Thomas acquired Chilean sculptures, including a Corten-steel piece inspired by wind-gnarled trees. For the latest overhaul, he collaborated with John Beitel of Landspace Inc., who has tended the gardens for 35 years. Together, they shaped a landscape where formal beds give way to looser planting along paths that frame views of the water, with red dogwood branches providing color through winter.

For all its evolution, the house’s greater purpose has remained remarkably consistent. Thomas’s nieces now invite their own friends; bedrooms are invariably full on summer weekends; and everyone helps cook or set the table. His mother, Sava, spends time on the porch before the household wakes, with a cup of coffee and the breeze coming off the water. After 40 years, she is still photographing the view. “I take photos and photos and they’re all the same,” she says. “But it’s beautiful.”

The findings add a scientific-workflow dimension to a much broader debate over unreliable, misaligned, and increasingly autonomous AI systems — without establishing that the more dramatic forms of AI misbehavior are occurring in scientific research.

Rather than the current, raging debate about "rogue AI" systems deliberately escaping control, deceiving scientists, or sabotaging experiments, the researchers are documenting something more immediate: AI output that requires auditing and verification, concerns about growing quantities of low-quality research, and a research pipeline in which faster hypothesis generation can run ahead of human and physical capacity to validate the results. The paper cites worries that AI-generated content, hallucinations, and what prior researchers have called illusions of understanding could strain peer review and validation.

France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. Amodei made a more direct commitment about the pace of Anthropic's releases: "We will slow down as much as necessary," he told the council. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.
