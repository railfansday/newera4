On Nov. 30, the justices will hear arguments in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that experts agree will have big implications on future litigation brought by employees against colleges and universities.

Georgia Institute of Technology, more commonly known as Georgia Tech, fired its head women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph in 2019, less than two months after she filed an internal complaint alleging that the university was discriminating against her and her sports program by not providing the same resources that it did for the men’s basketball team.

The university countered that it terminated Joseph following an independent investigation that revealed certain players felt bullied by her.

In 2020, Augusta University — also part of the University System of Georgia — reassigned and later decided against renewing the contract of fine arts professor Thomas Crowther after a Title IX investigation into an anonymous sexual harassment complaint. Crowther claimed that the university ignored numerous witness statements in his favor.

Both Crowther and Joseph sued the system’s regent board alleging sex discrimination under Title IX, which prohibits sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs. The Atlanta-based 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals consolidated the cases and dismissed both Title IX claims, finding the 1972 law did not provide individuals with the right to sue for sex discrimination in employment.

Most other federal appellate courts disagree, with eight circuit courts having ruled that Title IX permits employees to sue the institutions that employ or employed them. The 5th and 7th Circuits are the other appellate courts that have blocked such lawsuits.

But how the Supreme Court will handle this case is far from clear.

This won’t be the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on Title IX employee claims. The high court ruled 5-4 in 2005 that an Alabama high school girls’ basketball coach and gym teacher could proceed with his Title IX lawsuit in Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education.

Roderick Jackson had complained to supervisors about the unequal funding and access to facilities and equipment his team received but was allegedly ignored. He began receiving negative performance reviews and ultimately lost his job as the girls’ coach though he remained employed as a teacher. He argued officials retaliated against him in violation of Title IX for reporting sex-based discrimination.

To Miriam Becker-Cohen, the Supreme Court’s holding for Jackson should make Crowther a rather easy case. Becker-Cohen is senior appellate counsel at the Constitutional Accountability Center, a nonprofit law firm and think tank, and is the lead author of an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

There is no limitation on who can bring a private lawsuit in any Title IX case, according to Becker-Cohen. “Jackson only makes that case even stronger because Jackson involved a school employee,” she said.

If anything, Becker-Cohen contended that Jackson was a harder case because the court had to resolve the issue of whether it involved intentional sex discrimination. But there is “no question” the acts complained of in Crowther are intentional sex discrimination, she said.

But Jon Israel, a management-side sports and employment law attorney with Foley & Lardner in New York, suggested the cases have differences even though both involve coaches.

The finding in Jackson of an implied right to sue “makes it a little more distinguishable” than the Crowther case, which is dealing with a direct right to sue for nonstudents at higher educational institutions.

Gregory Garre, who served as U.S. solicitor general during the George W. Bush administration and now practices with Latham & Watkins, agrees there is a distinction. Garre filed an amicus brief in the case on behalf of the National School Attorneys Association supporting the University System of Georgia’s regents board.

“When it comes to Title IX, what they’re asking the court to do here is really dramatically expand the right from its traditional focus, which is on sex discrimination against students, into the realm of employment discrimination against employees of schools,” Garre told Higher Ed Dive.

Garre added that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which Congress amended shortly before passing Title IX, applies to employees of educational institutions as well as other organizations, meaning they can sue over sex-based employment discrimination under that statute. And, he called that timing significant.

“The statutory history here is a pretty compelling indication that Congress did not intend for Title IX to cover employment discrimination because Congress had passed Title VII [amendments] just months before it enacted Title IX.”

As a result, both Garre and Israel have said they think Title VII provides ample recourse for employees, such as those in the Crowther case.

But Becker-Cohen disagreed. “At least for some employees of federally funded institutions, Title VII isn’t going to be an option,” she said.

While the remedies may overlap, Becker-Cohen said Title VII has a much tighter statute of limitations, which can be as short as 180 days, than Title IX, which has no set limitations period — so courts rely on the most analogous state law limitations period.

She also said Title VII requires individuals to exhaust other avenues, such as filing a complaint with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before they can sue, among other procedural roadblocks not present in Title IX.

And that’s not all, according to sports law professor Barbara Osborne, who has represented colleges and universities on compliance issues.

“Title VII is strictly based on the sex of the employee. So that would not include what the typical Title IX claims are. They are different causes of action,” said Osborne, who teaches at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

In the typical Title IX employment case, she explained, the person suffering the negative employment action is not necessarily discriminated against because of their sex but rather for complaining about sex discrimination in the athletics program, which she noted that both Jackson and Joseph — the former Georgia Tech coach — clearly did.

Osborne also pointed out that Title IX regulations contain a section related to employment. “Why was it necessary to create regulations related to employment if there weren’t situations within education that might be different than normal employment situations?”

The Supreme Court could have said Title IX remedies were not available for employment retaliation in Jackson and declined to do so, Osborne and Becker-Cohen both were quick to note.

But more than two decades later, Crowther comes before a much different court, with the author of the Jackson opinion, former Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, and others in the majority, having long since retired or died.

College athletes are not considered employees. But that day could be coming in light of the massive changes affecting college sports in recent years, including the influx of name, image and likeness money.

As a result, how the Supreme Court resolves Crowther could affect college athletes themselves should they someday be deemed employees of the institutions whose teams they play on.

“If Title IX includes employees, the class of potential Title IX plaintiffs is going to be expanded dramatically,” said Garre, adding that the possibility of college athletes being considered employees would “certainly create new wrinkles” in litigation.

One of those wrinkles for college-athletes-as-employees could become a case of “be careful what you wish for” according to Israel, if the Court rules against the plaintiffs.

“If the student-athletes are treated as employees, and Crowther decides that Title VII is the exclusive remedy for employees then they’re in that Title VII world — not Title IX.” Israel said.

He contended that some disparate treatment arguments — that an institution is treating female athletes differently than male athletes playing revenue-producing sports, such as football or basketball — might effectively disappear under Title VII as a result.

According to Israel, that’s because a college gymnast’s work, for instance, differs from that of a football player so they would not be similarly situated under Title VII. “If you put it in a different context, a line worker versus an executive, they wouldn’t have the same compensation,” the sports law attorney said.

“The job description of a student-athlete would be virtually identical no matter what team they participate on, right?” she said. “They’re going to be expected to go to class. They’re going to be expected to go to practice, follow the nutrition program and whatever else. The job description isn’t (to) be able to perform in front of 15,000 fans. That’s not what the job is.”

Becker-Cohen acknowledged that if the Supreme Court were to rule for the system’s regents board, it would leave student-athletes and others with no remedy for sex discrimination unless they could make out a claim under Title VII.

“To the extent that the Court carves out college employees from the reach of Title IX’s private right of action, if the class of individuals who are considered college employees increases, that will just harm more people,” Becker-Cohen said.

Project-based learning programs have the potential to more thoroughly engage students, according to educators who have embraced the model. And, they say, the projects warrant public showcases.

Under a project-based learning approach, students in science classes might explore how to conserve water to keep costs down and steward natural resources. Or in math, they could determine where to locate security cameras in a community to get the best bang for the buck, accounting for both the money to buy the cameras and which angles will best cover the area to be surveilled.

A decade ago, Virginia’s Loudoun County Public Schools worked with nonprofit PBLWorks to institute a “train the trainer” model in about 100 schools — with a separate training for school leaders — aligning the pedagogy and structures of project-based learning to district-wide instruction, said Ashley Ellis, chief academic officer for the district.

“One of the connections was to our mission to empower students to make meaningful contributions to the world,” she said. “We saw PBL as a bridge.”

Dawn Haddock, principal at Emerick Elementary School in Loudoun County, brought the concept to her school and got rapid buy-in.

“I saw the importance behind this type of instruction,” she said. “Giving children the opportunity to have voice and choice, to pick and solve problems, was the direction we needed to go 10 years ago, and we still feel strongly today. ... It has become an everyday, normal type of activity within our school.”

Loudoun County Public Schools does not explicitly require project-based learning, mandatory training every year or a certain number of projects, Ellis said. But in some schools, like Emerick, “it’s become embedded in the work they do to empower their students.”

Emerick has instituted what Haddock calls a “genius hour,” during which students are working on projects that they present about every six weeks to their classmates.

“It can be about everything from black holes to glitter, whatever things interest them,” she said. “This has led to their ability to read and write [to create the project], and then get up and perform. … We do projects throughout the year. Every teacher is doing some type of project.”

The school borrows 30 minutes from library time and 30 minutes from regular classroom time, reasoning that reading and writing still take place, she added. “The children say, ‘You can take me out of anything else; don’t take me out of genius hour!’”

This practice builds toward an end-of-year showcase, for which students pick a project in March that “keeps kids working right up until the last week” of school — with seven minutes to present and two minutes for questions from the audience, Haddock said.

“We like to have them do a reflection of their teaching and learning,” she said. “Supervisors and administrators are coming to see what the kids are doing.”

Students work with their peers to solve problems, have a good discussion, and ask questions, Haddock said. “How can we get our students to think deeper? Every year, we add to our projects to make our students better thinkers.”

Project-based learning presentations vary at different grade levels, Ellis said. Elementary school principals, for instance, can more easily create showcases and bring communities together during the school day, because families are typically more engaged and “want to be in the school seeing what the kids are doing,” she said.

“At the middle and high school levels, schools have evening or even weekend symposiums,” Ellis said. “They invite community members, school board members — we’re truly fortunate to have such an active community in our schools.”

Haddock believes project-based learning and presentations help students become more aware of the world around them and instill the notion that they can help solve problems. During a “field day” at the end of the school year, when staff took hoses out to let students get doused, one student said to her, “Mrs. Haddock, we’ve got to conserve our water. I know you like to do that, but you have to turn that off.”

She added, “They got an understanding of a problem by the project we used — it’s neat to see how these projects [impact their thinking] because they become invested in it.”

#7152. How to navigate AI adoption — with faculty and student input

Artificial intelligence is transforming the mission of public education and “can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” one instructor said.

Colleges and universities nationwide have faced waves of student protests pushing back on institutional policies that embrace the use of artificial intelligence tools on campus.

That includes students and faculty at Columbia University who in May railed against the use of AI to announce graduates' names during commencement ceremonies, and students and employees at Emory University who in April called on administrators to remove AI-powered surveillance cameras from campus.

Universities have used AI tools to assist with a host of administrative functions, such as scheduling courses and nudging students to register for classes. In addition, many college officials are pushing to embed instruction on AI into curricula as businesses increasingly use generative AI platforms like OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini.

The embrace of AI has caused tension among students and faculty wary about its environmental, ethical, pedagogical, social and academic integrity impacts, said Victor Lee, faculty lead at the Stanford Accelerator for Learning's initiative to support educators in navigating the AI era.

Holding intentional conversations with students and faculty about AI use on campus can help ease concerns, said Lee.

Faculty members aren’t necessarily opposed to the technology, experts say, but training on the tools is spotty and they would like to be part of conversations about how their institutions use AI.

Robert Ovetz, a San José State University professor, is among the faculty members who have vocally opposed universities integrating AI into curricula with little to no input from faculty or their unions.

“This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” said Ovetz, who is co-author of the forthcoming book “Organizing Against AI in Higher Education.”

SJSU formally launched an initiative called AI Everywhere in fall 2025 with the aim of ethically and responsibly integrating the technology throughout campus. The initiative is part of an effort to prepare students for the future workforce, said Sandra Hirsh, the university's special assistant to the provost for AI initiatives.

It’s also part of a larger push by the California State University system, which signed contracts with several AI-focused tech companies in 2025, including a nearly $17 million deal with Open AI to provide ChatGPT Edu to more than 460,000 students and 63,000 faculty and staff throughout its 22 campuses. CSU renewed its contract with OpenAI this year to the tune of $13 million annually for three years.

By the start of the 2027-28 academic year, SJSU expects to have 28 AI-related programs and 101 courses, Hirsh said. That would represent a 75% and 36% increase, respectively, since fall 2025.

The university also launched a host of new AI events and initiatives, including faculty training on one of tech company Nvidia’s platforms. In the same vein, it worked with the city of San Jose to open the AI Center for Civic and Social Good, a space at a joint public and university academic library where community residents, students and workers can use AI education tools, Hirsh said.

"This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top."

While the scale of SJSU's AI efforts are significant, it's hardly the only institution to embrace the technology. Almost every institution is using or plans to use AI in some capacity, said Hironao Okahana, formerly managing researcher and chief of planning and impact at the American Council on Education.

In many places, that has translated into new courses. Over one-third of surveyed faculty members, 37%, said their institution had created new AI-focused classes, according to a report released in January by the American Association of Colleges and Universities and Elon University, which polled over 1,000 instructors in fall 2025.

And as of last July, a quarter of senior higher ed leaders reported adopting generative AI technologies at their institutions for student support, with another 22% planning to do so within the next six months, according to an ACE fall 2025 survey. Another 43% said they planned to adopt AI technologies within the next one to two years.

Many students, meanwhile, have issues with the ways large language models use data without their knowledge and consent, and how these systems reproduce and amplify existing forms of discrimination in society, among a host of other concerns, said Lindsay Weinberg, a professor and director of Purdue University’s Tech Justice Lab.

And some students are apprehensive about their universities forging partnerships with companies that use AI for military purposes, Weinberg said.

In April, SJSU students protested their university’s ties to companies including Nvidia, OpenAI, and Google for that reason, Spartan Media Group reported.

Many faculty share the students' concerns. And both faculty and students express misgivings about the environmental consequences from AI and how data centers cause noise pollution and negatively impact local water and electrical systems, said Weinberg.

Students are also hearing widespread reports about how AI will automate entry-level jobs, shrinking their job opportunities, Ovetz added.

Faculty have broad worries on AI’s impact on higher education, said C. Edward Watson, vice president for digital innovation at AAC&U and co-author of the association’s faculty survey report.

Nearly 52% of staff and faculty at CSU institutions indicated they were skeptical of the benefits of AI in higher education, with about 85% indicating they were concerned about the ethical use of the technology, according to 2025 system survey data.

Meanwhile, nearly all faculty members, 95%, expressed concern about students becoming overreliant on AI tools, and large majorities indicated the technology would diminish students’ critical thinking skills and attention spans, according to the report.

Overall, Ovetz said, many educators say that by using AI, students are cheating themselves out of the reasons they are attending college — to learn and expand their understanding, consciousness and awareness.

Jackson was an early member of the Luddite Club, a New York City-based anti-tech youth group that has turned into a global movement with roughly 40 campus and regional chapters over the past five years.

When students enroll at a university, they are expecting to receive an education, not a requirement that they have to interact with certain technologies, said Jackson.

But some students are using AI tools to help with their homework or exams, giving them an unfair advantage, and getting a “way out of education” — allowing them to earn a degree “without having to do much,” Jackson said.

“That creates a culture that isn’t necessarily something that everybody wants to live in or that everyone wants to follow,” said Jackson. “That’s where a lot of the pushback comes from.”

#7147. Think out loud, then write: A recipe for overcoming writing challenges

Hearing oneself speak and capturing those thoughts can be a powerful tool for all students — especially those who struggle to write, educators say.

The ability to transfer thoughts to the written word varies widely among K-12 students. But using verbal rehearsal to have students think out loud about what they want to write is usually an effective device to expedite the process, writing educators say.

As a middle school classroom teacher, Tanya Baker, executive director of the National Writing Project, said verbal rehearsal was a practice that worked well for all students, but especially those who had difficulty getting their thoughts on paper.

“It’s not just seen as a way to start or prewrite, but you can also use verbal rehearsal during the process of writing,” said Baker.

The literature around verbal rehearsal originated in the multilingual learning space, said Cami Condie, professor in the McKeown School of Education at Salem State University and an adjunct at Harvard Graduate School of Education. “It’s a tool to help [students] solidify the language they want to use in their writing,” she said.

When a student has thoughts they can’t seem to get out of their heads, educators have several options, Baker said. Students can talk and write down what they say, or they can record themselves, replay the recording, and recapture their ideas at that point. Their teacher can also take notes on what they are saying.

“I’ve seen real benefits in increased independence over time, and confidence from a student. It’s frustrating as a writer not to have anything to say, or believe you have anything to say, or get it from your mind to paper,” she said. “That gives them the confidence in their ability to do that.”

“There’s an empowerment that happens when students are able to learn and use these strategies,” Baker said.

Verbal rehearsal can also be used to fill in gaps in students’ understanding or knowledge, she said.

“Students will talk to me about a topic,” Baker said, and she asks, “Is there anything else you want to talk about? What about this? You just said you were interested in this.”

She added, “I can extend their understanding or knowledge, asking those follow-up questions. Me talking or a peer talking, and eventually themselves — they can start to hear where the gaps are.”

The techniques can work for any age, from K-2 all the way through graduate studies, Condie said.

“Particularly for young writers, whether they’re young in age or young in development, they write like they speak. It’s almost verbatim,” she said. “If there’s any speech growth, you’ll see it in their writing.”

When working with her graduate students, Condie said, “I can just say, ‘Talk about what you’re thinking about.’ That can be enough of a prompt. But for younger children, it needs to be more focused. Sometimes, it’s repeating exactly what the teacher said.”

Pairing students with the same partner for long periods of time can be a useful practice because the pairs get to know how one another thinks and can prompt them more knowingly, she said.

In middle and high school, Condie gives more of an open-ended prompt when following up: “What did you talk to your partner about? How did talking to your partner help you? Have them be more reflective, and build meta-cognitive skills. How did that solidify your idea? What changed because you talked to your partner? It helps them name why they’re stuck.”

Younger students require more hand-holding and more time with the teacher in general, while older students and those with more developed writing abilities become independent more quickly, Baker said.

But grade level alone doesn’t predict how much time students will need, she said.

“You can have a student in high school who doesn’t have confidence in their ability to put something on the page, and may need additional time. Whereas you may have a middle-school student who may have lots of practice and may not need verbal rehearsal,” Baker said. “It has to be scaffolded based on what the student needs.”

When comparing the teacher pay gap with over 30 countries, Brookings found that U.S. teachers only make two-thirds of what other college graduates earn.

Public school teacher pay in the U.S. lags significantly when compared to other industrialized countries, according to a late October analysis by the Brown Center on Education Policy at the Brookings Institution.

Examining average pay for teachers at the primary school level versus that of other college graduates across more than 30 countries, Brookings found the U.S. ranked last — and that its primary school teachers earn about two-thirds of what other college graduates are paid annually.

That pay gap is wider than what the Economic Policy Institute has found in its ongoing teacher pay penalty studies. According to a late August EPI report, U.S. teachers in 2025 earned about 25% less than college graduates in other professions.

In its report, Brookings described the U.S. teacher pay gap as “striking” and said that one possible explanation for the disparity may be widening income gaps in the U.S.

The teacher pay gap seems to widen particularly for teachers who fall in the middle-to-high range of the salary scale, according to Brookings.

Teacher salaries and benefits typically account as the largest expense in school district budgets.

But in examining federal data from the National Center for Education Statistics, Brookings found that the share of public school expenditures on teacher salaries has dropped over the last 100 years. For instance, the report said, teacher salaries made up 57% of public school expenditures in 1919, compared to 26% in 2019.

Brookings added that one explanation for this trend is that school districts have chosen to increase their staffing capacity instead of increasing teacher salaries.

“If you go back 100 years, more than half of school spending went to teacher salaries. Where the data ends — just before the start of the COVID-19 pandemic — the fraction going to teacher salaries had fallen to a quarter,” Brookings said. “Basically, schools are spending more money, just not on teacher salaries.”

The latest findings from Brookings come as some education policy experts have suggested schools should lean into strategic teacher pay approaches — especially as districts have to tighten their budgets due to challenges from widespread enrollment declines. Strategic teacher pay policies can include increasing teacher salaries in hard-to-staff schools and subjects, as well as boosting pay for high-performing teachers.

The National Education Association reported in April that the average teacher salary was $74,495 during the 2024-25 school year. Despite that being a 3.5% increase from the previous school year, the nation’s largest teacher’s union said, the latest teacher pay figures reflect a 5% decline in national averages compared to a decade ago when adjusting for inflation.

The overall student population shrank between the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years, but the number of students restrained and secluded rose.

These trends align with ongoing patterns across the country as districts ramp up student recruitment efforts and consider school closures and consolidations to adjust to falling enrollments fueled by lower birthrates, competition from school choice, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

The 2023-24 civil rights data collection summary, released by the Education Department this fall, also includes statistics about discipline, restraint and seclusion, academic access and student characteristics.

The mandatory data collection takes place every two years. For the 2023-24 school year, the CRDC included data from more than 17,000 school districts and 97,000 schools nationwide. The Education Department may change data collection prompts between collection years or make other data collection or release changes. In releasing the 2023-24 civil right data this fall, the Education Department redacted data collected about nonbinary students’ experiences, such as enrollment, discipline, bullying and harassment, and whether districts have policies prohibiting the harassment or bullying of students on the basis of their gender identity.

Here, we break down what you need to know about the latest release of civil rights data.

About 5% of K-12 students — or 2.3 million — in 2023-24 received one or more in-school suspensions, which was the same percentage in 2021-22. About 5% of students in both school years also received one or more out-of-school suspensions.

Black boys and girls, Hispanic or Latino boys, White boys, and boys of two or more races in public schools were overrepresented in suspensions and expulsions in both the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years.

During the 2023-24 school year, students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act were also disproportionately disciplined — a trend also noted in the 2021-22 school year. Although students with disabilities comprised 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, they accounted for 24% of students who received one or more in-school suspensions, 27% of those who had one or more out-of-school suspensions, and 23% of those who were expelled.

More K-12 students were physically restrained, mechanically restrained or placed in seclusion at schools during the 2023-24 school year (129,700) compared to the 2021-22 school year (105,700). About 84,300 students were physically restrained and 32,300 students were secluded in 2023-24.

Overrepresented student subgroups that were restrained or secluded during the 2023-24 school year included boys, Black students, White students, students of two or more races, and students with disabilities.

For instance, Black students accounted for 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, while making up 19% of students secluded, 29% of students physically restrained, and 46% of students mechanically restrained.

Of the country's 27,200 high schools, three-quarters offered chemistry, 59% offered physics, 51% offered computer science, and 45% offered calculus in 2023-24, which is relatively on par with data reported for the 2021-22 school year.

Boys were almost twice as likely to enroll in computer science compared to girls — at 11% compared to 6%. Girls, on the other hand, had slightly higher enrollment rates in advanced mathematics, biology and chemistry compared to boys.

High School students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act had lower enrollment rates in all these courses — except Algebra I — compared to their nondisabled peers.

About 3.1 million, or 18%, of high school students are enrolled in Advanced Placement courses and about 2.1 million high schoolers participated in dual enrollment or dual credit programs in 2023-24.

Of the 2023-24 pre-K-12 student population, 15% were students with disabilities served under IDEA and 4% were students with disabilities served only under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. #7111. DOL final rule revamps antidiscrimination requirements for apprenticeships

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The U.S. Department of Labor issued a final rule to rescind “affirmative action requirements” for federally registered apprenticeships, including in education.

Education is the top occupation in the U.S. for degree-based apprenticeships, according to an August report by New America. Beyond teachers, K-12-related registered apprenticeship programs also include principals and lead teachers.

Registered Apprenticeship programs are meant to bridge the gap between employer needs and entry-level professionals, per the official federal apprenticeship fact sheet. What sets them apart is that they are industry-vetted and paid opportunities, they offer structured on-the-job learning and supplemental classroom education, and give participants portable credentials.

The programs span several industries, including agriculture, construction, education, energy, financial services, health care, hospitality, manufacturing, technology and transportation.

In the final rule, DOL summarized public commenters’ concerns with the new regulation’s potential effect on educator apprenticeship programs.

Specifically, one commenter said that students need access to teachers who they can relate to, who can “affirm their identities,” and who can “teach with an understanding of the cultural context of their lives,” according to DOL. Otherwise, the commenter said, students “may feel invisible,” and their learning outcomes may suffer when the teaching workforce is "homogeneous."

DOL noted that another commenter said the rule would “restrict active and expansive recruitment efforts that address gaps in the educator workforce.”

In response to those comments, DOL said the final rule reduces “prescriptive Federal mandates” that will provide apprenticeship sponsors — including in the education sector — greater flexibility to design their programs, such as with outreach and recruitment strategies.

“The Department acknowledges the important role that educator apprenticeship programs can play in fortifying the teaching workforce,” DOL’s response said. “The Department’s final rule does not prohibit sponsors from engaging in lawful recruitment efforts.”

More broadly, DOL “expects that removing these obligations will significantly reduce administrative burdens for sponsors,” and encourage further adoption of Registered Apprenticeship programs, the final rule document said.

Since 2024, DOL has continued to deregulate apprenticeship programs. DOL originally proposed a rule in December 2023 seeking to “modernize” apprenticeship programs. At the time, then-Acting Secretary of Labor Julie Su said in a press release, “Equity and job quality have marked the most successful Registered Apprenticeship programs for workers and employers alike. This proposed rule codifies the Department of Labor’s strong commitment to these principles.”

#7043. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

Enterprise ambitions around AI are running into a fundamental problem: Orgnanizations aren't always confident in the networks those projects depend on, according to a report from telecommunications provider Arelion.

That put AI ahead of security and compliance programs at 44%, digital transformation at 42%, and cloud migration at 38%.

The findings come from Arelion's report, "The Trust Illusion: Why AI and Digital Transformation Need More Than a Reliable Network," based on a survey of 518 senior enterprise network decision-makers in the U.S., U.K., Germany, and France conducted by Savanta.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

Another tension runs through Arelion's findings. Enterprises need networks they can trust to support AI projects, but many of the same decision-makers are concerned about AI's growing role inside those networks. A total of 54% of respondents identified AI and automation in network management and security as the factors most likely to negatively affect network trust over the next three years. That ranked ahead of data sovereignty expectations and geopolitical instability, both at 39%, and regulatory requirements and quantum computing threats to current encryption standards, both at 38%.

At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. Proactive AI-driven fault prediction was among the capabilities they said would matter most to future trust, alongside stronger built-in security, greater accountability, and responsive support. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. "As businesses become increasingly reliant on digital infrastructure, enterprises are looking for network providers that can demonstrate transparency, accountability, and operational excellence, not just availability."

#7024. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#7022. Before & After: How an Artist and Architect Couple Turned the “Worst House” on the Block Into Their Dream Home

In Brooklyn, artist Katherine Keltner and architect Douglas Gauthier transform a crumbling townhouse with equal parts drama and restraint.

Before they stepped foot inside the crumbling brownstone on a landmark block in Brooklyn’s Boerum Hill, artist Katherine Keltner and architect Douglas Gauthier had toured 30 homes and browsed more than 200 online. Neither of them expected to become instantly captivated by the 1869 Italianate townhouse, which was being sold in a stately yet dilapidated condition, having once served as a single-room occupancy (SRO) residence. But it happened nonetheless.

“This was the first one that had the bones that Katherine wanted,” recalls Gauthier, who runs the firm GAuthier Architects. “It follows the classic real estate rule of buying the worst house on the best block.”

The strategy paid off: Once their offer was accepted, the couple embarked on an 18-month-long odyssey that saw them jump through a series of hoops (preservationists; concerned neighbors; skeptical engineers) to completely transform the moldering pile into a creative haven for them to live, work, and raise their daughter.

A true joint-effort, Gauthier oversaw a modest two-story rear expansion and structural alterations, while Keltner took charge of spatial arrangements as well as color, pattern, texture, and artwork—much of it her own.

Once deteriorating, the interiors now have an easy elegance, with plaster walls, arched passageways, and curved corners that give the soaring ceilings an intimate feel. Pink takes an unexpected starring role, appearing throughout the parlor floor and also in the zellige-clad primary bath, but other adventurous hues like mint green and crimson also feature prominently. Capping it off is a plywood-sheathed office for Gauthier’s practice, allowing the family retreat to double as a place of creative inspiration.

Below, Keltner and Gauthier share their biggest lessons (and some “before” pictures) from the painstaking passion project.

When the townhouse was converted to an SRO, it had been chopped up into at least 13 units and two kitchens, and few of the original decorative elements were still intact. A couple had bought it in the 1960s and made it a family home again and added an art gallery space. But by 2021, when Keltner and Gauthier first saw it, the house was in a state of severe neglect, with peeling paint, crumbling floors, ancient appliances—not to mention a 1,500-pound printing press in the studio (that the couple later preserved). Yet, Keltner and Gauthier saw an opportunity, not to recreate the past, but rather forge their own set of rules.

“The house wasn’t burdened with pristine historic details—our moldings were trashed,” says Gauthier. “There were a few things that we kept and celebrated, but to me, it was like a tabula rasa. We can just go in and make it ours.” Katherine agreed: “I feel like sometimes having to preserve things is more expensive than just starting clean,” she says. “And so I thought that was an advantage for us.”

Not having to be too precious with ornamentation allowed the couple to make a bold statement about crown moldings, eschewing tradition and installing curving corners and coved ceilings instead. Nowhere does the effect have more impact than in the parlor, where Gauthier devised a ceiling light in the shape of a giant oval that casts light upward rather than down. “I really like indirect lighting,” he says.

Crafted out of laser-cut plywood, the elliptical fixture is an abstracted homage to the ceiling medallions found in neighboring historic brownstones. “Some of them are incredibly ornate and beautiful, but they don't really have any use—they’re just decoration,” says Gauthier. “Adding ambient lighting to the living space takes that historical reference in pocket.”

Once they were through the process of changing the structure’s zoning from an SRO to a single-family home, Gauthier and Keltner had to contend with its landmarked status, meaning a series of approvals were required for any additions or exterior modifications.

The couple originally planned to construct a three-story extension in the back, but they ended up compromising on two floors. This expansion includes a bump-out for the parlor floor’s kitchen, leading to a rear deck that steps down to the garden. On top, a private terrace connects to the primary bath. “Instead of having that third story, we now have a deck off of our primary suite,” says Keltner. “It’s kind of amazing,” adds Gauthier.

One decision the couple wrestled with mightily was whether to replace the original, 150-year-old pine flooring with ebony-stained white oak. “The floors were part of the reason we liked the house so much,” says Gauthier. But their contractor talked them out of keeping them. “It’s a lot of extra work for them,” says Keltner. “They have to take it up, save it, put something else down, and then put it back. I think we reached a point in time and money where we were like, Okay, fine.”

Another flooring regret was not adding underfoot heating. “I was pushing the mechanical engineers to do radiant heating and radiant cooling,” says Gauthier. “Radiant heat is really lovely—you have warmth under your feet all the time.” But the mechanics behind the system proved complicated and technically redundant, as the home was already configured with a forced-air HVAC system.

“In doing research and other projects, it’s clear that the cost-to-pleasure ratio would have been worth it,” he says now. “We regret not putting the warm floors in.”

While windows might sometimes be an afterthought, Keltner and Gauthier have advice: Go with triple-glazed. “Windows provide heat and cool insulation as well as security,” explains Keltner. “And they can define both the outside and the inside of a space.” The couple went with triple-glazed windows from Austrian-based firm Bewiso that come pre-wired for easy connection to alarm systems. “They’re almost like hurricane windows,” she says.

“You can't break through three panes of glass,” says Gauthier. Due to the building’s landmark status, they had to use historic-style windows on the front facade. “We were able to get these old-fashioned type of windows that when you close them, they feel like you're closing a Mercedes,” says Gauthier. “They completely silence the outdoors.”

The final piece of advice is perhaps the most crucial: “Hire the best contractor you can afford,” says Gauthier.

“The typical advice is to get a few bids and throw out the lowest and the highest,” says Keltner. “People usually take the second lowest.” However, that could be the determining factor between an on-time project and one that drags. “You’ll end up paying the difference both in money and time,” she says. “It’s often hard for people to bite the bullet and go with the slightly higher price, but it really pays off.”
