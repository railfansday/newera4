#7238. How to navigate AI adoption — with faculty and student input

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Artificial intelligence is transforming the mission of public education and “can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” one instructor said.

Colleges and universities nationwide have faced waves of student protests pushing back on institutional policies that embrace the use of artificial intelligence tools on campus.

That includes students and faculty at Columbia University who in May railed against the use of AI to announce graduates' names during commencement ceremonies, and students and employees at Emory University who in April called on administrators to remove AI-powered surveillance cameras from campus.

Universities have used AI tools to assist with a host of administrative functions, such as scheduling courses and nudging students to register for classes. In addition, many college officials are pushing to embed instruction on AI into curricula as businesses increasingly use generative AI platforms like OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini.

The embrace of AI has caused tension among students and faculty wary about its environmental, ethical, pedagogical, social and academic integrity impacts, said Victor Lee, faculty lead at the Stanford Accelerator for Learning's initiative to support educators in navigating the AI era.

Holding intentional conversations with students and faculty about AI use on campus can help ease concerns, said Lee.

Faculty members aren’t necessarily opposed to the technology, experts say, but training on the tools is spotty and they would like to be part of conversations about how their institutions use AI.

Robert Ovetz, a San José State University professor, is among the faculty members who have vocally opposed universities integrating AI into curricula with little to no input from faculty or their unions.

“This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” said Ovetz, who is co-author of the forthcoming book “Organizing Against AI in Higher Education.”

SJSU formally launched an initiative called AI Everywhere in fall 2025 with the aim of ethically and responsibly integrating the technology throughout campus. The initiative is part of an effort to prepare students for the future workforce, said Sandra Hirsh, the university's special assistant to the provost for AI initiatives.

It’s also part of a larger push by the California State University system, which signed contracts with several AI-focused tech companies in 2025, including a nearly $17 million deal with Open AI to provide ChatGPT Edu to more than 460,000 students and 63,000 faculty and staff throughout its 22 campuses. CSU renewed its contract with OpenAI this year to the tune of $13 million annually for three years.

By the start of the 2027-28 academic year, SJSU expects to have 28 AI-related programs and 101 courses, Hirsh said. That would represent a 75% and 36% increase, respectively, since fall 2025.

The university also launched a host of new AI events and initiatives, including faculty training on one of tech company Nvidia’s platforms. In the same vein, it worked with the city of San Jose to open the AI Center for Civic and Social Good, a space at a joint public and university academic library where community residents, students and workers can use AI education tools, Hirsh said.

"This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top."

While the scale of SJSU's AI efforts are significant, it's hardly the only institution to embrace the technology. Almost every institution is using or plans to use AI in some capacity, said Hironao Okahana, formerly managing researcher and chief of planning and impact at the American Council on Education.

In many places, that has translated into new courses. Over one-third of surveyed faculty members, 37%, said their institution had created new AI-focused classes, according to a report released in January by the American Association of Colleges and Universities and Elon University, which polled over 1,000 instructors in fall 2025.

And as of last July, a quarter of senior higher ed leaders reported adopting generative AI technologies at their institutions for student support, with another 22% planning to do so within the next six months, according to an ACE fall 2025 survey. Another 43% said they planned to adopt AI technologies within the next one to two years.

Many students, meanwhile, have issues with the ways large language models use data without their knowledge and consent, and how these systems reproduce and amplify existing forms of discrimination in society, among a host of other concerns, said Lindsay Weinberg, a professor and director of Purdue University’s Tech Justice Lab.

And some students are apprehensive about their universities forging partnerships with companies that use AI for military purposes, Weinberg said.

In April, SJSU students protested their university’s ties to companies including Nvidia, OpenAI, and Google for that reason, Spartan Media Group reported.

Many faculty share the students' concerns. And both faculty and students express misgivings about the environmental consequences from AI and how data centers cause noise pollution and negatively impact local water and electrical systems, said Weinberg.

Students are also hearing widespread reports about how AI will automate entry-level jobs, shrinking their job opportunities, Ovetz added.

Faculty have broad worries on AI’s impact on higher education, said C. Edward Watson, vice president for digital innovation at AAC&U and co-author of the association’s faculty survey report.

Nearly 52% of staff and faculty at CSU institutions indicated they were skeptical of the benefits of AI in higher education, with about 85% indicating they were concerned about the ethical use of the technology, according to 2025 system survey data.

A nationwide survey from AAC&U also highlighted additional sweeping concerns from faculty. About three-quarters of faculty said cheating spiked on their campuses when generative AI tools became widespread and that they believed the technology would impact degree integrity.

Meanwhile, nearly all faculty members, 95%, expressed concern about students becoming overreliant on AI tools, and large majorities indicated the technology would diminish students’ critical thinking skills and attention spans, according to the report.

Overall, Ovetz said, many educators say that by using AI, students are cheating themselves out of the reasons they are attending college — to learn and expand their understanding, consciousness and awareness.

For some students, their opposition to AI is part of broader resistance to technology and social media shifting from being optional to seemingly mandatory in today’s society — including on campuses, said Lucy Jackson, a sophomore at Binghamton University, in New York.

Jackson was an early member of the Luddite Club, a New York City-based anti-tech youth group that has turned into a global movement with roughly 40 campus and regional chapters over the past five years.

When students enroll at a university, they are expecting to receive an education, not a requirement that they have to interact with certain technologies, said Jackson.

But some students are using AI tools to help with their homework or exams, giving them an unfair advantage, and getting a “way out of education” — allowing them to earn a degree “without having to do much,” Jackson said.

“That creates a culture that isn’t necessarily something that everybody wants to live in or that everyone wants to follow,” said Jackson. “That’s where a lot of the pushback comes from.”

Despite the concerns, faculty aren’t broadly rejecting AI tools, Ovetz said. Many view generative AI as an interesting tool to incorporate into their teaching, for example by using chatbots to help students engage with course material, said Ovetz.

At CSU, 61% of faculty and staff said they regularly use AI-powered tools or applications when teaching, researching or performing their work, and 64% say it positively affects those tasks, according to the survey of CSU campuses.

Higher ed leaders have been grappling with how to integrate AI literacy into the curriculum, who should be teaching courses on how to use AI, what type of professional development should be required for instructors and what type of tools they should provide to faculty and students, Watson said.

While colleges and universities are good at adding new courses to meet student needs,they are not good at doing it fast, said Watson. It usually takes a few semesters of discussions with faculty and employers to determine what students need to know, he said.

That was reflected in the AAC&U survey, with 68% of faculty reporting their institutions had not prepared educators to use generative AI “for effective teaching and mentoring.” Less than half reported their institutions had written guidelines for AI use, and just 13% had adopted AI literacy as “a general learning outcome.”

That’s despite a widespread belief among faculty members that it’s important to teach students about issues related to AI during class, the AAC&U survey found. Large majorities stated it was important to teach students about inaccurate statements produced by GenAI tools, how the tools are used to produce misinformation and deep fakes, and how they erode copyright and authorship, according to the survey.

It has been largely left to individual professors to design or restructure their courses with AI, said Lee. The vast majority of faculty, 87%, said they have created their own policies on students’ use of generative AI for assessments, projects and tests, the survey found.

That’s not surprising, however, as decisions about what faculty teach are not usually made top-down, Lee said.

“The law and philosophy of academic freedom and trusting the learning expertise of the faculty leaves a little bit more of a hands-off orientation,” Lee said.

When administrators form partnerships with tech companies and push technologies without discussing those initiatives with unions or faculty members on their campuses, they often draw the ire of the students and employees most impacted by those decisions, said Weinberg.

Last year, the California Faculty Association filed an unfair labor practice charge against CSU, alleging the university system failed to notify the union when it launched an AI initiative in 2025 and refused to meet to discuss faculty rights and the impacts that it would have. CFA represents some 29,000 professors, lecturers and other workers at CSU.

The union complaint underscored the need for administrators to slow down on AI adoption so those initiatives can go through the shared governance process and be bargained over with academic unions, said Ovetz, who has been involved in the bargaining efforts.

CFA in February introduced an article in their negotiations with the university to protect faculty from being required to use the tools and provide them with intellectual property protections. Those negotiations have been ongoing for a year and a half, said Ovetz, but no agreement or contract has yet been reached.

“We are planning to vote to strike in the coming months, including over AI,” Ovetz said.

Those protections are important, as workloads increase for faculty who have to learn how to use AI tools as well as constantly check students' work to see if it was written using the technology, said Ovetz.

Many institutions that have succeeded in integrating AI for teaching or administrative purposes have established universitywide committees that discuss new pieces of technology and goals for using them, said Okahana. Those committees should include various campus stakeholder and constituent groups to help get people on board with new technologies, he said.

SJSU, for example, formed a 15-member AI advisory committee in 2024 composed of various university stakeholders, including faculty, students and staff, to advise administrators on how to ethically integrate AI tools throughout campus.

More than half of faculty reported their institutions had implemented a task force or group to oversee and manage implementation of AI tools across their campus, according to the AAC&U survey.

“Stakeholder engagement is really important and not just a tool to plug in,” said Okahana. “It is challenging us to think about how we do the teaching, how we run the university.”

Ovetz, however, said an advisory committee “is just that: advisory.”

CFA filed an unfair labor practice charge with the state, he said, “exactly because this violated our collective bargaining agreement and was implemented without bargaining with the faculty union.”

The artificial intelligence industry is expected to generate $2.67 trillion in worldwide spending this year, according to a new forecast from Gartner. But relatively little of that money will be spent on the generative AI models dominating the industry's headlines.

The much bigger business is everything required to make them run.

Worldwide spending on AI infrastructure will reach nearly $1.5 trillion in 2026, accounting for about 56% of all AI spending. Gartner expects spending on generative AI models themselves to total just $28.3 billion. Put another way, for every dollar Gartner expects to be spent on generative AI models this year, more than $52 will be spent on AI infrastructure.

It is a striking measure of how the economics of the AI boom are changing. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and other models may be the most visible products of the generative AI era, but much of the money is going to the enormous buildout of servers, semiconductors, networks, cloud capacity, and other infrastructure required to support them.

"The buildout of AI data center capacity is the largest infrastructure project humanity has ever undertaken," John-David Lovelock, distinguished vice president analyst at Gartner, said in announcing the forecast.

Gartner now expects worldwide AI spending to increase 49.5% this year, from about $1.79 trillion in 2025 to $2.67 trillion in 2026. Infrastructure represents $1.484 trillion of that total. The next-largest categories are AI services, at $576.5 billion, and AI software, at $461.6 billion. Spending on AI agents and assistants is expected to reach $29.2 billion, only slightly more than the $28.3 billion Gartner forecasts for generative AI models.

The numbers also show just how quickly expectations are changing. In January, Gartner forecast $2.53 trillion in total AI spending for 2026, including $1.37 trillion for infrastructure. By May, those forecasts had risen to $2.60 trillion and $1.43 trillion, respectively. The latest forecast raises them again, to $2.67 trillion overall and $1.48 trillion for infrastructure.

That is significant because infrastructure was already, by far, the largest component of Gartner's AI spending estimate. As the firm's expectations for the AI market have risen during 2026, most of the additional money has accumulated there.

The infrastructure category is broad. Gartner includes AI-optimized cloud infrastructure, servers, networking, AI processors, and devices. Demand for those systems remains strong despite rising memory prices, Lovelock said.

Dawn Haddock, principal at Emerick Elementary School in Loudoun County, brought the concept to her school and got rapid buy-in.

Emerick has instituted what Haddock calls a “genius hour,” during which students are working on projects that they present about every six weeks to their classmates.

“It can be about everything from black holes to glitter, whatever things interest them,” she said. “This has led to their ability to read and write [to create the project], and then get up and perform. … We do projects throughout the year. Every teacher is doing some type of project.”

The school borrows 30 minutes from library time and 30 minutes from regular classroom time, reasoning that reading and writing still take place, she added. “The children say, ‘You can take me out of anything else; don’t take me out of genius hour!’”

#7209. Think out loud, then write: A recipe for overcoming writing challenges

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Hearing oneself speak and capturing those thoughts can be a powerful tool for all students — especially those who struggle to write, educators say.

The ability to transfer thoughts to the written word varies widely among K-12 students. But using verbal rehearsal to have students think out loud about what they want to write is usually an effective device to expedite the process, writing educators say.

As a middle school classroom teacher, Tanya Baker, executive director of the National Writing Project, said verbal rehearsal was a practice that worked well for all students, but especially those who had difficulty getting their thoughts on paper.

“It’s not just seen as a way to start or prewrite, but you can also use verbal rehearsal during the process of writing,” said Baker.

The literature around verbal rehearsal originated in the multilingual learning space, said Cami Condie, professor in the McKeown School of Education at Salem State University and an adjunct at Harvard Graduate School of Education. “It’s a tool to help [students] solidify the language they want to use in their writing,” she said.

When a student has thoughts they can’t seem to get out of their heads, educators have several options, Baker said. Students can talk and write down what they say, or they can record themselves, replay the recording, and recapture their ideas at that point. Their teacher can also take notes on what they are saying.

“I’ve seen real benefits in increased independence over time, and confidence from a student. It’s frustrating as a writer not to have anything to say, or believe you have anything to say, or get it from your mind to paper,” she said. “That gives them the confidence in their ability to do that.”

“There’s an empowerment that happens when students are able to learn and use these strategies,” Baker said.

Verbal rehearsal can also be used to fill in gaps in students’ understanding or knowledge, she said.

“Students will talk to me about a topic,” Baker said, and she asks, “Is there anything else you want to talk about? What about this? You just said you were interested in this.”

She added, “I can extend their understanding or knowledge, asking those follow-up questions. Me talking or a peer talking, and eventually themselves — they can start to hear where the gaps are.”

The techniques can work for any age, from K-2 all the way through graduate studies, Condie said.

“Particularly for young writers, whether they’re young in age or young in development, they write like they speak. It’s almost verbatim,” she said. “If there’s any speech growth, you’ll see it in their writing.”

When working with her graduate students, Condie said, “I can just say, ‘Talk about what you’re thinking about.’ That can be enough of a prompt. But for younger children, it needs to be more focused. Sometimes, it’s repeating exactly what the teacher said.”

Pairing students with the same partner for long periods of time can be a useful practice because the pairs get to know how one another thinks and can prompt them more knowingly, she said.

In middle and high school, Condie gives more of an open-ended prompt when following up: “What did you talk to your partner about? How did talking to your partner help you? Have them be more reflective, and build meta-cognitive skills. How did that solidify your idea? What changed because you talked to your partner? It helps them name why they’re stuck.”

Younger students require more hand-holding and more time with the teacher in general, while older students and those with more developed writing abilities become independent more quickly, Baker said.

But grade level alone doesn’t predict how much time students will need, she said.

“You can have a student in high school who doesn’t have confidence in their ability to put something on the page, and may need additional time. Whereas you may have a middle-school student who may have lots of practice and may not need verbal rehearsal,” Baker said. “It has to be scaffolded based on what the student needs.”

Microsoft said the platform helped criminals determine "who to target, who to impersonate," and how to get the most money from a compromised organization.

Business e-mail compromise has traditionally required attackers to manually comb through messages, learn how an organization handles payments, and find the right person to impersonate. However, EvilTokens simplified the entire process by automating much of that work.

The tools could quickly sort through a stolen inbox, summarize or translate messages, find invoices and wire-transfer discussions, and identify the people who moved money. EvilTokens could then draft messages that sounded like they came from the victim. It packaged all of this with mailbox access, phishing tools, management dashboards, and even customer support.

The service was sold through Telegram for a $1,500 startup fee, followed by a $500 monthly subscription. Microsoft's complaint also described a bulk e-mail tool that cost $600 a month and a more advanced e-mail delivery platform priced at $1,000 a month.

To break into a target, EvilTokens relied on device-code phishing. Victims were sent to a real Microsoft sign-in page and persuaded to enter an authentication code. The login process looked legitimate because it was, but the code quietly authorized a session controlled by the attacker.

That also made cleanup more complicated. The attackers stole OAuth tokens and authenticated sessions instead of passwords, so a password reset alone might not remove their access. Administrators also needed to revoke the stolen tokens and active sessions.

According to Microsoft's complaint,  EvilTokens used fake Microsoft 365 security notices and other branded templates to direct users into the device authentication flow. Once inside, attackers could use Microsoft Graph and other APIs to search and monitor a mailbox.

The company also found that AI played a role in creating EvilTokens itself. Investigators said much of the platform appeared to have been "vibe coded," with its developers using AI to quickly build and expand the service. EvilTokens reportedly drew on multiple AI models, including improperly integrated services from Groq and OpenAI.

The court authorized Microsoft to take control of or disable domains connected to the operation. Microsoft worked with Cloudflare, Coinbase, OpenAI, Railway, SpyCloud, the Shadowserver Foundation, and TRM Labs on the disruption. This marks the Microsoft Digital Crimes Unit's 40th court-authorized disruption and its first targeting an end-to-end, AI-enabled cybercrime service.

EvilTokens' infrastructure may be offline, but the model behind it is unlikely to disappear. The operation showed how AI can turn a stolen inbox into an immediately useful fraud playbook, allowing less-experienced criminals to launch attacks that once required considerable time and skill.

The heads of OpenAI, Anthropic, and Hugging Face recently joined AI researcher Yoshua Bengio at the United Nations headquarters in New York on September 23 to warn the UN Security Council about the security risks of increasingly capable AI.

They did not offer a single plan for managing those risks. Bengio called for licensing and independent safety reviews. OpenAI's Sam Altman proposed common standards that countries could adopt through their own laws. Anthropic's Dario Amodei urged AI developers to slow down when safety work cannot keep pace. Hugging Face's Clément Delangue warned that limiting access to capable systems could leave defenders at a disadvantage.

France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. Such standards should accommodate both open and closed models without favoring established companies, he said.

Amodei made a more direct commitment about the pace of Anthropic's releases: "We will slow down as much as necessary," he told the council. He identified two principal concerns: the misuse of AI to develop biological weapons and the possibility that advanced systems could outstrip developers' ability to control them.

The Anthropic chief proposed placing outside evaluators inside AI companies with access comparable to employees. He suggested that governments begin with a narrow agreement against using AI to make biological weapons, then develop ways to evaluate models and verify commitments across borders. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. He called for stronger monitoring and disclosure, but argued that safety also depends on giving defenders access to capable tools. The central danger, he said, was "asymmetry of powerful AI" between attackers and defenders, large companies and everyone else, and wealthy countries and the rest of the world. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

The U.S. government opposed a broader international regulatory role. White House science adviser Michael Kratsios told the council that countries should develop their own ability to use and govern the technology. In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.

The four AI briefers differed on how far oversight should go and how widely capable systems should be available. They agreed on a narrower point: Governments need better ways to understand what advanced AI systems can do and to detect when safeguards fail.

#7166. OpenAI Getting Rid of Custom GPTs and Moving to Plugin Architecture

OpenAI has announced it will retire Custom GPTs and move the specialized workflows built around them to a new plugin architecture spanning ChatGPT and its Codex coding agent.

Custom GPT retirement is currently scheduled for Dec. 11, 2026, although the company says timing and migration availability can vary by account and workspace.

For affected Enterprise workspaces, OpenAI's current migration schedule targets Oct. 26 for ending creation of new Custom GPTs. Qualifying Enterprise customers with approved deferrals can continue using them until Feb. 11, 2027.

OpenAI has changed its UI to make GPTs less prominent, and when users choose to try creating a new Custom GPT, they are now prompted with an option to create a plugin instead.

Rather than maintaining separate customized versions of ChatGPT, OpenAI is shifting customization toward plugins that can package reusable instructions, reference material, and connections to outside services.

Under OpenAI's plugin architecture, a plugin can include one or more skills containing instructions and resources for repeatable workflows, a Model Context Protocol (MCP) server exposing tools and external systems, or both. ChatGPT and Codex share a universal plugin directory, although some capabilities can remain specific to a particular environment.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#7132. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

#7114. Report: Cyber Attacks Increasingly Exploit Trusted Workplace Tools

In its latest threat intelligence report, Ontinue found that attackers are increasingly using trusted workplace tools to trick users into giving them access to enterprise networks. Approximately 30% of organizations monitored by the cybersecurity company experienced at least one attempt to impersonate IT support through Microsoft Teams during the first half of 2026. About one in every 990 external Teams contacts showed signs of someone pretending to be an internal IT worker.

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. The person warns of a security issue, a software problem or an update that can't wait. Then they walk the employee through opening Windows Quick Assist, installing remote-access software, or running a PowerShell command. Following those steps can hand the attacker control of the employee's computer, often without the employee realizing it.

The approach is very similar to the Black Basta ransomware group, which helped popularize similar attacks in 2024. Unfortunately, Ontinue found the technique has since been adopted by other cybercriminal groups, including some with no known link to Black Basta.

Some threat agents are also combining tactics. They might first overwhelm an employee's inbox with unwanted e-mails, then reach out through Teams pretending to be IT support offering to fix the problem.

"Phishing no longer resides solely in your inbox. It has spread across the entire corporate communications environment," said Mika Aalto, co-founder and CEO of Hoxhunt.

Teams impersonation is only part of the problem. Ontinue also found attackers taking advantage of legitimate Microsoft sign-in processes. Between May and June, researchers identified 10 confirmed device-code phishing compromises across five customer tenants.

In these attacks, criminals trick employees into completing what appears to be a normal Microsoft authentication process. But doing so can give the attacker access to the employee's account and system without actually breaking their password.

The report also found attackers using blockchain-based infrastructure to hide malicious activity, accounting for more than 25% of confirmed malicious ClickFix findings.

"Attackers are no longer looking for gaps in your perimeter. They are looking for the systems your organization already trusts and finding ways to move through them undetected," said Shane Barney, chief information security officer at Keeper Security.

The security firm says that IT needs to keep a closer eye on unexpected Teams contacts, remote-support requests, suspicious PowerShell activity, and changes to authentication settings. Employees should also confirm sensitive requests through established internal channels, rather than relying on the name or account that appears on their screen.

Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.

In a company blog post, Hayete Gallot, executive vice president of Microsoft Security, outlined how Microsoft plans to use Project Perception to monitor, manage, and contain what it calls "agentic insiders." These are AI agents deployed within an environment that can access data, use software tools, and make decisions without constant human supervision. While they can improve productivity, Microsoft warns that they also introduce security risks, even when they haven't been compromised by an attacker.

"Agents are unpredictable," Gallot wrote. "They will adapt as the context changes, may not behave as intended, and will collaborate and collude with other agents to reach their objectives, without needing to be compromised."

Project Perception was first introduced in July. The platform, which entered public preview Aug. 3, is designed to help security teams respond to increasingly sophisticated cyber threats using AI-powered automation.

Instead of leaning only on traditional security alerts and manual investigations, Project Perception uses three kinds of specialized AI agents working together. Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

To spot threats and respond faster, the system pulls in security data from identities, endpoints, cloud environments, applications, and other sources.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. Project Perception would find those agents, give them identities and permissions, and keep an eye on what they do across different systems. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

"Monitoring and securing those agents must become continuous," Gallot wrote.

The initiative also reflects concerns raised by Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella in an Oct. 10 essay on AI security.

Nadella argued that enterprises should treat powerful AI models much like potential insider security risks, particularly when those models can access confidential information or perform critical business operations.

"We simply can't outsource responsibility for what intelligence does on our behalf," Nadella wrote.

He called for security controls that operate independently of AI models, including restricted permissions, detailed activity logging, and mechanisms that allow administrators to stop an agent's actions.

"In other words, we need to separate the supply of intelligence from the authority over it," Nadella wrote.

Microsoft admits that watching what AI agents do is only part of the answer. Gallot said that future security tools will also need to show why AI models make the choices they do, not just what actions they end up taking.

#7084. Hublot Just Relaunched Its First Watch. Founder Carlo Crocco Explains How It Changed Everything.

In Milan, celebrating the release of the Classic Fusion Tributo, I got to catch up with the guy who altered the aesthetics of modern watchmaking.

If you spend a lot of time following the world of watches, Hublot’s origin story might feel as familiar as one of the fairytales you heard at bedtime as a child. If you don’t, though, it might feel just as fantastical as some yarn about a princess who feels a pea under a stack of mattresses or a prince who slays a fire-breathing dragon.

You start with an unlikely hero. This time around, it’s an Italian entrepreneur with a big, bold, brazen idea. He wants to make a watch, something unlike anyone has ever seen. It’ll be gold, with an angular case shaped like the porthole on a ship. Not so crazy yet. But wait. Because, breaking entirely with the conventions of the day, the strap won’t be made of exotic leather or precious metal but rubber. Humble, industrial rubber.

This is exactly what Hublot founder Carlo Crocco envisioned nearly half a century ago, and what he brought to the Basel Watch Fair—pretty much the place to be if you wanted to be a part of the business of timekeeping—in 1980. He spent three years developing the idea, all without doing a bit of market research. He just believed in his vision. And then the fair began.

“I was very anxious, because for the first two days, no one came into our booth,” he says. But the anxiety didn’t have long to fester. Word was spreading. And then? People galore. “On the third day, we realized that everyone was speaking about us because it was really something different from all the other watches.”

That first watch—36mm in diameter, with a quartz movement and a black rubber strap—quickly became a hit. Industry buzz gave way to the kind of clientele most brands can only imagine. The King of Greece bought one in Gstaad for himself and another for his brother-in-law, the King of Spain. More royals followed. (“By the way, they’re all related,” Crocco quips.)

But the biggest thing wasn’t getting insiders to a booth at a trade fair or convincing kings to strap Hublot watches—named, if you’re curious, for the French word for porthole—on their wrists. It was changing how the industry thought about the way watches are made.

“At that time, the mentality of watchmaking was very classical,” Crocco says. “They couldn’t think, normal people, of putting together rubber and gold. I personally thought it was a really good idea because it was against the current. It was very risky, but if you don’t risk, you have no result.”

The result was a watch industry that completely recalibrated itself. Crocco says that within four or five years of the original Hublot’s release, the brand was already dealing with imitators. Now, the idea of combining disparate materials is firmly embedded in the minds of both consumers and designers. You can see it on watches from brands that sell at the local department store and brands that only sell to folks who haven’t seen the inside of one of those since the first time their private jet took off. Crocco’s big idea changed the whole game.

Which means it’s a pretty good time to go back to the beginning of this thing. Last month in Milan, Hublot debuted a new take on that first, groundbreaking watch. Dubbed the Hublot Classic Fusion Tributo, it’s an homage but not a nuts-and-bolts recreation. Instead of the original 36mm case, it’s been upsized to a more 2026-appropriate 38mm. And instead of a quartz movement—Crocco thought it was “more practical” at the time but has come around to the enduring appeal of mechanical construction—a self-winding HUB1711 movement with a 50-hour power reserve keeps things ticking along. Oh, and now you can get it in white gold, too.

Then there’s that rubber strap. When Crocco designed the original Hublot, he was insistent that the strap was entirely straight; he felt it was crucial to the aesthetic proposition. On the Tributo, it’s ever-so-slightly, almost imperceptibly tapered, a necessary concession to keep the proportions intact after increasing the case size (which, handily, lets that automatic movement sit comfortably inside).

But, as with the first Hublot, it’s not all about tech specs and watch-nerd particulars. It’s about the spirit of the thing. And the spirit of this watch stays true to that pioneering design. There are twelve screws around the bezel, so you can keep an eye on the hour despite the dial’s lack of indices—something Crocco says was a non-negotiable when he started consulting on the new release. The yellow gold version keeps the abyss-like black lacquer dial and the white gold throws us a tiny (and welcome) curveball by using onyx. The combo of precious metal and rubber still hits, 46 years later.

If you prod him how he feels about the way that he shifted the watch industry, Crocco won’t brag. He left Hublot nearly 20 years ago, when it sold to LVMH. He knows his idea changed things; he’s happy about it. But this isn’t his day job anymore. Still, when he’s asked if he could be considered the godfather of fusing high and low in watchmaking—and you give him a minute to ponder it—he’ll tell you the truth.

#7054. Survey Finds 84% of Organizations Reported at Least One AI-Related Incident in the Past Year

Companies are running into problems as they give artificial intelligence more control over critical business applications, with 84% reporting at least one AI-related incident in the past year, according to a new survey from identity security firm Delinea.

The company's 2026 Application Access Governance report found that some organizations have experienced incorrect financial transactions and AI actions they couldn't trace or explain. The findings raise concerns about whether enterprises have enough oversight of AI systems that can make decisions and take actions on their own

The findings are based on an independent survey of 1,000 senior finance and finance technology leaders in the United States and United Kingdom. The AI-specific analysis included 879 respondents whose organizations use the technology in critical business applications.

The report found that 35% of surveyed organizations have deployed AI agents capable of taking actions within business applications. However, only 11% of organizations using AI said their governance and security controls were keeping pace with deployment.

Delinea also found gaps in centralized visibility into agent deployment and permissions, including who owns agents and who approved their use.

Some respondents reported segregation-of-duties conflicts involving AI, while others identified agents that continued operating after the employees who deployed them had left the organization.

The research also highlighted gaps in monitoring access across business systems. Only 46% of respondents said they continuously monitor and manage cumulative access risks across applications.

Those gaps can make it difficult to identify segregation-of-duties conflicts, in which a person or agent has permissions across systems that should be kept separate.

Despite those issues, 95% of respondents said they were confident their access controls would withstand scrutiny in an external audit.

However, 26% reported access-related audit findings that had recurred in consecutive years, while 25% said some findings had remained unresolved for three or more years.

Only 22% of organizations had a single named owner for each application, with ownership tracked in one system of record.

Meanwhile, 60% reported an increase over the past two years in the number of critical applications operating outside the scope of access governance or Sarbanes-Oxley Act compliance.

The findings highlight the oversight challenges associated with giving AI agents authority to act within business systems, particularly when organizations cannot consistently identify who owns an agent or trace its actions.

Frank Vukovits, chief security scientist at Delinea, said organizations remain responsible for the actions AI agents take within their systems.

"When an agent can approve a transaction or change permissions, the business owns the outcome. You need to know what every agent can do, who owns it, and who approved it, before an auditor asks or your financials are misstated," Vukovits said.
