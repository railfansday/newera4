#7109. 87% of educators use personal devices in teaching. The next great 1:1 investment might be the one at the front of the room.

Even in districts that issue laptops to staff, there is a good chance a teacher’s personal laptop, tablet or phone is carrying some of the workload. In fact, a recent study by the University of Arizona found that 87% of educators surveyed use their personal devices for at least some teaching-related tasks. Those personal devices were purchased, set up and managed by themselves, with two-thirds reporting that they download institutional documents (for example student data, grade books) onto them.

While districts are providing teaching devices, these numbers suggest that alongside the school-issued laptop, a share of teaching work, from grade books to student records, ends up on devices the district cannot see or manage.

In the movement around enabling technology in classrooms for students, this study presents a larger conversation on how technology is being used by educators who are working closest to them.

Over the last two decades, classroom devices have gone from shared computer labs to one-to-one deployment. Today, 96% of teachers say their students use learning devices in class. This did not happen overnight. It took years of planning from administrators, careful execution by IT teams, significant investment and district grants. As a result, student devices have a tried-and-true playbook. Districts know what to expect with refresh cycles, IT teams understand device management and superintendents can anticipate their budgets and funding for new devices.

Teachers are the cornerstone of classroom tech, and it is time their devices got a playbook too. As they regularly carry heavy computing loads, in everything from running lessons, online grading, to joining calls, they deserve the same level of detail and detail to device deployment and management. This is more handing out a laptop. It means giving teachers a device capable enough that they don’t need to reach for their own.

This is even more important as teachers regularly handle sensitive data. As the study found, more than half of participants who downloaded institutional documents onto personal devices, reported that their devices had automatic cloud backups through personal accounts.

While using a personal device for teaching might seem harmless in nature, the device is not under the districts' scope. Updates, encryption and sign-in guidelines are not under the control of the district, rather, this responsibility falls to the teacher.

This is alarming as education remains the fourth most targeted sector for cyber-attacks. Robust student, teacher and parent data combined with increased digitalization and lack of cybersecurity resources have left devices and information vulnerable to malicious attacks.

Paired with the right training on cybersecurity best-practices, a well-managed district-deployed device overseen by IT teams helps to enable schools to secure sensitive student data. These devices deploy continuous updates, encourage fast and secure sign-in and ensure private information stays secure.

The ASUS P5 featuring the Intel® Core™ Ultra 7 Series 3 Processor, is a strong example of a device that can be managed at scale with the performance to multitask through a full teaching day. Engineered to seamlessly juggle grade books, lesson plans and video calls, Intel processors deliver all-day battery life and built-in hardware-based security that helps protect student data — that’s the power of Intel Inside®. For IT teams, the device supports centralized management tools that let them deploy updates, monitor device health and enforce policies across a district’s fleet.

The planning, budgeting and IT expertise that has put a device in nearly every student’s hands in the United States is ready to extend to teachers. With the right devices, they can do even more and districts can protect the data that matters most. The next great 1:1 investment might be the one at the front of the room.

Connecting workflows cuts down operational drag and restores educator capacity for direct student mentorship.

#7092. Title IX sex bias employment case about to get its day in Supreme Court

A lower court said institutions may not know when accepting federal funds that they are accepting liability under Title IX, when Title VII typically covers employment claims.

School districts could get a hint of how the U.S. Supreme Court will rule on a case that could change how teachers and other school employees bring sex discrimination claims when the justices hear oral arguments next week.

In Thomas Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, the 11th U.S. Court of Appeals ruled in 2024 that Title IX’s right to sue doesn’t apply to employees, diverging from at least eight other appeals courts. At odds is whether school employees have the right to sue privately under Title IX outside of protections under Title VII.

Title IX protects anyone who interacts with a school — including students, employees and parents — from sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs and is often invoked by students who are harassed, assaulted or bullied due to their sex. Title IX protections for school employees include, for instance, prohibiting discrimination against teachers due to pregnancy or pregnancy-related conditions, among other things.

Meanwhile, Title VII prohibits employment discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex and national origin.

In Crowther, two University System of Georgia employees — a basketball coach at Georgia Tech and an art professor at Augusta University — said the university system discriminated against them on the basis of sex when it fired them.

Georgia Tech women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph was fired in 2019 shortly after she filed an internal complaint that alleged the university was discriminating against her and her program by not providing the same resources as the men’s program.

At Augusta University, art professor Thomas Crowther was told his contract would not be renewed in 2021 after several students reported allegedly inappropriate conduct in his class — including sexual harassment — which triggered a Title IX investigation. That investigation process and ensuing negative performance reviews, he said, discriminated against him on the basis of him being male.

Joseph and Crowther both reported the alleged discrimination to the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, but also sued in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Georgia under Title IX.

The two cases were combined before the 11th Circuit, which said in its 2024 decision that schools may not understand when accepting federal funds that they accept potential liability for damages of Title IX claims by employees, when those types of claims are typically filed under Title VII.

The Association of Title IX Administrators called the Supreme Court case "one of the most consequential decisions impacting the intersection of Title IX and employment law in decades."

Multiple major education and legal organizations have filed briefs in the case, including the National Education Association and the National Women's Law Center.

"The American education system faces a severe and worsening crisis in educator recruitment and retention," said NEA's brief that was filed earlier this year in favor of the former University System of Georgia employees. "Anti-discrimination protections affect whether talented professionals enter, remain in, or depart from careers."

The NEA said that a court decision implicates "a nationwide educator workforce crisis, the professional environment in which recruitment and retention decisions are made, and ultimately the quality of education available to students across the country."

"When sex discrimination drives talented educators from the profession, the consequences extend beyond individual career harm and cause systemic degradation of educational quality," the teachers union said.

Granting teachers the right to sue privately under Title IX would help improve an environment where sex discrimination "has remained pervasive," the NWLC said in its own brief. The center cited teachers' high rates of sexual harassment from their colleagues and students, unequal pay for female teachers despite their overrepresentation in the profession, a lack of leadership opportunities, and pregnancy discrimination, such as a lack of accommodations and supportive school policies.

Such a decision would also help address lower investments in girls' school sports and barriers to STEM in education programs, NWLC said.

Title IX "can only be meaningful for women who work in education if they can enforce their right to be free from sex discrimination, including in court," the organization argued.

However, the Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia maintained in its opposing brief filed in October 2025 that Title IX does not include a private right to sue and that petitioners "had every chance to prove their claims for employment-based sex discrimination under Title VII."

"Had Congress desired that employees of educational institutions have a bonus remedy for employment discrimination under Title IX, unburdened by Title VII’s exhaustion requirements and damage caps, it could have provided as much," the respondents said. "It has not."

The case invokes another — Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education, a 2005 Title IX case that was decided 5-4 in favor of the plaintiff.

That case was brought by Roderick Jackson, a high school girls’ basketball coach who spoke out to his supervisors after he noticed that his team was not receiving equal funding and equal access to athletic equipment and facilities. Following his complaints, he received negative work reviews and was ultimately removed from his position.

His lawsuit reached the high court, which decided that Title IX's private right to sue included retaliation claims for complaining about sex discrimination.

"Reporting incidents of discrimination is integral to Title IX enforcement and would be discouraged if retaliation against those who report went unpunished," the Supreme Court said in its decision. "Indeed, if retaliation were not prohibited, Title IX’s enforcement scheme would unravel.”

However, more than two decades later, Crowther will be heard by a much different bench, given the justices in the majority on the Jackson opinion have long since retired or died.

Between the 2018-19 and 2024-25 school years, the portion of students experiencing homelessness increased from 2.7% to 3.1%, with the overall population of students experiencing homelessness increasing 12%. During that same period, overall public school enrollment fell by about 1.3 million students.

Some of the largest increases in the share of homeless students were seen in states where homeless student identification increased alongside drops in overall public school enrollment. Those states include West Virginia, Montana, Arkansas, Hawaii, Maine and Vermont.

West Virginia had the largest share of homeless students compared to its overall public school population at 1 in 16.

Districts in that state have also closed around 140 since 2011, according to the West Virginia Center on Budget and Policy. Thirteen school closures have been proposed or approved in just the 2026-27 school year, according to the center's last update in mid-October.

Overall, the state has lost 3.5% of its students since last school year and 17% over the past decade, according to an October count by the West Virginia Department of Education.

"The drop in enrollment raises two concerns for students experiencing homelessness: tighter school budgets, and children who may not appear in school records at all," said the SchoolHouse Connection report about the issue nationally.

The budget squeeze, drop in overall enrollment, and increase in students identified as homeless come on the heels of federal pandemic relief funds drawing to an end in September 2024. The American Rescue Plan's Homeless Children and Youth funds provided a historic $800 million to identify and serve students experiencing homelessness. The additional funding allowed over half of U.S. school districts to access Education for Homeless Children and Youth funds.

In 2025-26, the first full year without ARP-HCY funds, many schools no longer had the staff and resources that helped them find these students, said SchoolHouse Connection. The report noted that the lost funding is a potential reason most states identified fewer homeless students despite a national increase in that population.

In fiscal year 2026, Congress appropriated only $129 million for the EHCY program, which means about 1 in 5 districts receive dedicated funding for homeless students. The EHCY program was established under the McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act in 1987.

However, regardless of whether these funds are provided to districts, all districts are required under the McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act to serve homeless students and remove barriers to their education.

Enrollment could be inching up due to a rebound from sharp drops during the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as expansion of public preschool programs in recent years, said Gopalan, who is also an associate professor at the University of Oregon's College of Education.

The analysis by EdTrust and AdvancED Equity of preschool discipline rates uses data from the U.S. Department of Education's Civil Rights Data Collection and does not include information about what types of incidents led to the discipline.

The CRDC only includes statistics from school district-supported preschool programs, Gopalan said. That means preschool enrollment would be included for district-operated early childhood Head Start programs, but not for Head Start programs run by community grantees.

She said there is no federal collection that tracks more informal preschool discipline activities like shortened days, repeated pick-up calls or when parents are counseled to withdraw their child from a preschool.

Social-emotional learning interventions can help prevent escalating and challenging student behaviors, Gopalan said. And teacher training and resources also can reduce educators' stress and increase their capacity to address difficult behaviors.

"I think we as a society need to ask, 'Do we want to suspend them, or do we want to provide additional supports for teachers to help manage this problem?'" Gopalan said.

In the meantime, Mississippi had noticed Alabama’s early success and replicated the model. “They flip-flopped,” Loewus explained. And by 2019, Alabama looked at Mississippi and passed comprehensive legislation that more or less reinstituted its reading initiative, with funding for 750 school-based coaches, 100 regional coaches, and 10 staff at the state Department of Education to monitor the entire process, she said.

The Alabama legislation also protects coaches’ time: they cannot be pulled into substitute teaching when someone calls in sick, proctor tests, or help with recess. “That is so unusual,” Loewus said. “In other schools, coaches are the first ones to be pulled. They don’t have their own class.”

The state superintendent Eric Mackey requires district superintendents to sign a memorandum of understanding before they receive their annual allocation, and “when principals start to pull coaches for other things, he gets a phone call,” she added.

Overall, the legislation in Mississippi is mostly similar, Loewus said. The program there was initially funded by a now-shuttered philanthropic group called the Barksdale Reading Institute, but the state has since taken over the budget. “It’s really top down in both places,” she said. “It’s key having really resolute state leadership, champions at the top who are not letting this go.”

Loewus emphasizes that the coaching alone won’t achieve desired results unless the coaches are centrally trained in research-based reading instruction.

“The fact that everyone is getting the same training, and it’s centralized, and based on research, is really important. Protecting coaches’ time is really important. Having steady funding is important," Loewus added. "Having the structure of coaches having coaches is incredibly important.”

Helping students build skills around gratitude can have benefits ranging from daily mindset to overall classroom culture, a UC Berkeley professor says.

Building skills and habits around finding and expressing gratitude can improve students’ day-to-day mindset, their longer-term emotional and mental health, their interpersonal interactions and overall classroom culture.

Educators can help bring this about, although they need to be schooled in how to do so at each developmental stage, according to Vicki Zakrzewski, education director at the Greater Good Science Center at the University of California, Berkeley.

Gratitude is a relational virtue that requires recognizing that someone has done something for you, and that there was a cost to what that person did, Zakrzewski said.

“Gratitude communicates that you really matter,” she said. “Students have to have that ability to recognize that somebody else has done something for you. Gratitude can be fairly complicated when you break it down, cognitively and emotionally.”

For the youngest students, that means teaching what gratitude is in the first place by reflecting on what the emotions surrounding gratefulness feel like, Zakrzewski said.

“You’re helping them define it,” she said. “When is a time somebody did something kind for you? How did that feel? … Students don’t really get a sense of pure gratitude until they have the ability to empathize with others. You’ll see it become more relevant to students who are around 2nd or 3rd grade.”

By middle school, students have the ability to think larger, beyond their own circles, about people who have made a difference in society. When addressing people they know personally, Zakrzewski suggests having them write a gratitude letter.

“That’s one of the most powerful activities,” she said. “If they can, have them deliver it to the person and read it out loud.”

However, educators might have to take a different tack with high school students, who can tend to roll their eyes at a gratitude-related activity. Zakrzewski suggests starting by involving them in a conversation about what gratitude is and how to express it, and then launch an activity so it becomes part of their daily life.

“Of course, there’s the traditional gratitude journal, every day or once a week, where you say what you’re grateful for,” she said. “A powerful thing with high school students in particular is to do self-science: have them notice how they feel before and after they do a gratitude journal."

Students could then decide how often they want to keep a journal, and keep notes about how they feel in week one compared to week four.

Research has shown that when somebody does something for you, it enhances your self-worth and improves relationships, Zakrzewski said. And improved relationships are important in building classroom culture.

Another activity Zakrzewski recommends is transforming complaints into gratitude.

"If you find your students are starting to go down the complaining route, and you want to help change that mindset, take the complaint, but turn it into something you feel grateful for,” she said.

For example, if there’s a math test on Friday, a student might reflect that they are grateful they get to exercise their brain and how that can help them in the future. Cultivating the habit of gratitude and seeing the world through that lens removes a feeling of entitlement, which research has shown provides mental, emotional and physical health benefits for adults, Zakrzewski said.

“That’s a beautiful way of going through the world,” she said. “If you can cultivate that as a student, the benefits are tremendous.”

Research also has shown that not all cultures practice gratitude in the same exact way, and educators should delve into that aspect, as well, Zakrzewski said.

“That’s really important for teachers, if they’re going to do gratitude activities,” she said. “Have conversations with students’ parents, or students themselves, about how gratitude shows up in your culture.”

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Red team agents hunt for possible vulnerabilities, blue team agents look into suspicious activity, and green team agents step in to fix problems and strengthen defenses.

Now Microsoft wants to use those same capabilities on the AI agents already running inside enterprise networks. It would also keep assessing the risks that come with their actions.

Microsoft also described a plan for "Guardian Agents," specialized AI agents that supervise other agents and step in when their behavior gets risky. That might mean limiting an agent's permissions or blocking certain actions before they cause harm.

#6922. Cisco Intros Privileged Infrastructure Access for People and AI Agents

Cisco has introduced Privileged Infrastructure Access, an approach to infrastructure administration that gives people and AI agents temporary, task-specific permissions instead of leaving standing privileges available after their work is done.

In a recent blog post, Cisco's Matt Caulfield described a system that uses cryptographic identity to connect an administrator or agent to the infrastructure needed for a particular task. Access is governed by short-lived privileges, with session recording providing a record of the work performed.

"If there is no work being done, no privilege exists," Caulfield wrote. Cisco positions the offering as a way to bring identity controls directly into infrastructure operations while reducing dependence on static credentials, shared secrets, and broad access.

The announcement follows Cisco's previously announced Teleport partnership and its Infrastructure Identity concept, which connects the identity of a person or machine with the resources it can access, the actions it performs, and the permissions governing those actions.

Caulfield described a gap between the controls organizations apply to workforce applications and those used to administer network infrastructure. Companies have invested in single sign-on, multifactor authentication, and risk-based controls for application access, he wrote, but those protections often do not follow an engineer logging into a router or switch.

Instead, infrastructure access may depend on long-lived credentials, shared accounts, or separate policy systems. According to Cisco, that separation makes it harder to enforce least privilege and establish which person or system performed a particular action.

The company illustrates the problem with an administrator updating hundreds of switches during a maintenance window. If some updates fail, the administrator needs to troubleshoot before the window closes. The permissions available to handle that work, however, may cover more infrastructure and remain active longer than the task requires.

Traditional privileged access management can reduce risk through credential vaulting and rotation, Caulfield wrote. But he argued that operational friction can undermine those controls when teams create exceptions to complete urgent work. Those exceptions can leave credentials and privileges usable after the immediate need has passed.

Cisco's stated objective is to give infrastructure teams a consistent route to the devices they manage while providing security teams with policy enforcement, temporary permissions, and a record of activity. The company describes operational convenience as part of the design, rather than treating access governance solely as a credential-management problem.

Privileged Infrastructure Access makes access an on-demand action, according to the announcement. Cryptographic identity establishes the actor, and the resulting connection provides only the privileges needed to complete the task. Those privileges expire when the task concludes.
