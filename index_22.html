The Catholic nonprofit in Montana cited “years of sustained enrollment decline and increasing financial pressure” in its announcement Tuesday.

Like many small private colleges, the University of Providence has faced significant enrollment and revenue challenges in recent years. Its fall headcount dropped 23.5% from 2019 to 2024, reaching 763 students, according to federal data.

But one of its most significant death blows was the expected loss of funding from Providence, a healthcare system affiliated with the same religious order as the university. Against that backdrop, the University of Providence declared financial exigency at the end of last year.

#7178. Undergraduate enrollment inched up this fall, while master’s dips 3.7%

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Early data shows slowing undergraduate growth and further declines among graduate students, per the National Student Clearinghouse Research Center.

Fall undergraduate enrollment inched up 0.6% in 2026, according to preliminary data from National Student Clearinghouse Research Center released Thursday. But that growth is significantly slowed from last fall, when it rose 2.4% from 2024 to 2025.

Community colleges drove the modest overall increase. They saw a year-over-year 1.2% bump in students, building on a 3.5% annual increase they experienced last fall.

Enrollment levels at both private and public four-year colleges remained roughly flat "across credential programs, admission selectivity categories, institution locales, and more,” Matthew Holsapple, the clearinghouse's senior research director, said at a press conference Wednesday. “The important change this fall isn't widespread decline; it's the loss of that broad-based growth we saw last year.”

Meanwhile, graduate enrollment shrunk 1.6% overall as master’s enrollment within that category fell 3.7%. Master’s levels began to slide last fall, when enrollment dipped 0.4% compared to 2024.

"What was a relatively small decline last year has really become more, much more pronounced,” Holsapple said at the press conference.

The overall numbers mask considerable variance, including by fields of study.

Computer science programs experienced the biggest losses among subject areas in the clearinghouse’s data, with four-degree undergraduate enrollment plummeting 12.4% year over year this fall.

Amid tech layoffs and the take-off of automated coding, unemployment rates for recent computer science and computer engineering graduates reached 7% and 7.8%, respectively, in 2024, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. That's among the very highest rates of recent college grads unemployment by degree field and roughly 3 percentage points above the overall unemployment rate that year.

“Computer science led years of strong growth before reversing direction last fall, where we started to see numbers really drop,” Holsapple said. “This year, it's even more pronounced.”

Visual and performing arts, as well as liberal sciences, both saw enrollment drop by over 4% at the undergraduate level. In contrast, engineering and health professional degree enrollment each spiked by over 6%.

Thursday's preliminary data is based on responses from 46% of institutions that report to the clearinghouse and includes 7.9 million student enrollments. The clearinghouse's final fall 2026 data is set to be released in January.

#7174. Special education disputes continue to rise

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Written state complaints increased while mediation requests hit an all-time high, a CADRE analysis of 2024-25 data shows.

The number of special education state complaints has continued its year-over-year climb nationwide, jumping 7% between the 2023-24 and 2024-25 school years alone, according to an analysis released this month by the Center for Appropriate Dispute Resolution in Special Education, a federally funded technical assistance center. Between 2014 and 2024, the tally skyrocketed by 113%.

Formally called "written state complaints," they often trigger state-led investigations into special education-related concerns brought forward by anyone. Some 10,610 written state complaints were filed during the 2024-25 school year, up from 9,927 the year before. Just three states — Texas, Massachusetts and California — accounted for 26% of the nation’s written state complaints in 2024-25. CADRE’s analysis is based on data collected by the U.S. Department of Education and also from data CADRE collected directly from states.

Written state complaints are just one course of action in a continuum of special education dispute procedures and practices, according to CADRE. Other potential practices under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act include due process complaints and mediation. CADRE's continuum also includes dispute prevention approaches such as family engagement.

The recent CADRE data analysis doesn’t explore the causes for trends in dispute activities. However, CADRE Director Melanie Reese said in an Oct. 21 email that the 2024-25 data shows families and schools are using IDEA dispute resolution "more than ever."

CADRE's finding that written state complaints are increasing aligns with trends seen by the Council for Exceptional Children and the National Association of State Directors of Special Education through state surveys.

CEC and NASDSE, for example, found that survey responses from 35 states administered this year showed a 56% increase in special education written state complaints between the 2023-24 and 2025-26 school years.

While CEC and NASDSE stress that there is no single reason for this increase, they said many families have reported a lack of faith that the U.S. Department of Education’s Office for Civil Rights will resolve their concerns. The Education Department significantly reduced OCR's workforce last year — after which it did begin rehiring some staff. Earlier this year, the agency began outsourcing some OCR functions to the U.S. Department of Justice as part of the Trump administration's broader effort to wind down the Education Department.

OCR has had 100 K-12 resolution agreements for disability discrimination complaints so far this year, according to its online resolution database.

Between March and September 2025, OCR resolved 7,072 of more than 9,000 complaints received of all types, mostly by dismissing 90% of them, according to a U.S. Government Accountability Office report released in January. As with written state complaints, anyone can file an OCR complaint at no cost.

States are required to address special education written state complaints within 60 days of receiving them. More than three-quarters —  78% — of the 2024-25 complaints were resolved within that time frame, according to the CADRE analysis. However, that was down from the 92% average between 2014-15 and 2022-23.

According to CEC and NASDSE, state and local special education staff shortages and turnover are making it extremely difficult to provide timely resolutions.

Additionally, the number of students qualifying for services under IDEA has increased in recent years. About 15% of the student population in 2023-24, qualified for IDEA services, according to data from the U.S. Department of Education.

Another special education dispute activity tracked by CADRE is due process complaints, which are requests by parents of students with disabilities or school districts that their case be heard by an administrative hearing officer.

Whereas written state complaints ask a state agency to investigate a violation of special education law, due process complaints initiate a formal administrative hearing to resolve disputes over an individual student's identification, evaluation, placement or free and appropriate public education.

CADRE's analysis shows there were 23,656 due process complaints filed in 2024-25, a 40% drop compared to the year before. But, excluding New York's data, the analysis shows a 7% year-over-year increase in these complaints.

CADRE explained that in 2024-25, New York revised its procedures by establishing a separate Enhanced Rate Equitable Services unit and shifting certain cases out of the state’s IDEA due process system. That led to a decline of 16,389 reported due process filings in the state between 2023-24 and 2024-25, according to CADRE.

Still, New York, California and Puerto Rico made up more than 72% of the due process complaints filed nationally, CADRE said.

Another data point that CADRE points to — as a positive trend — is a continued increase in mediation requests to resolve special education disputes. The 2024-25 school year saw the highest number of mediation requests — 13,197 — to date. The 2024-25 school year also saw the highest number of mediations held at 7,336.

Mediation may be used anytime a family and a district disagree over special education services, including if there is a due process complaint. A school district official or a family can request mediation and the activity is voluntary for both parties.

Although the number of times such mediation requests successfully led to agreements slipped in 2024-25, mediations not tied to due process complaints reached agreement about two-thirds of the time. That "underscores the value of investing in early, collaborative approaches that help families and schools resolve concerns before they escalate," Reese said.

Special education dispute resolution experts point out that while conflict is natural, it can lead to negative impacts for students when it goes unresolved.

CADRE promotes other preventative and early dispute resolution approaches. For instance, it encourages schools to use facilitators at individualized education program meetings, if there is conflict, and to engage and partner with families of students with disabilities.

#7144. Inside the Supreme Court case that could decide Title IX employee protections

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Next month, the high court will hear arguments on whether higher education workers have the right to sue their institutions for sex-based discrimination.

A critical question facing the U.S. Supreme Court in its 2026-27 term is whether employees of federally funded educational institutions can sue their employers for sex discrimination under Title IX.

On Nov. 30, the justices will hear arguments in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, a case that experts agree will have big implications on future litigation brought by employees against colleges and universities.

Georgia Institute of Technology, more commonly known as Georgia Tech, fired its head women’s basketball coach MaChelle Joseph in 2019, less than two months after she filed an internal complaint alleging that the university was discriminating against her and her sports program by not providing the same resources that it did for the men’s basketball team.

The university countered that it terminated Joseph following an independent investigation that revealed certain players felt bullied by her.

In 2020, Augusta University — also part of the University System of Georgia — reassigned and later decided against renewing the contract of fine arts professor Thomas Crowther after a Title IX investigation into an anonymous sexual harassment complaint. Crowther claimed that the university ignored numerous witness statements in his favor.

Both Crowther and Joseph sued the system’s regent board alleging sex discrimination under Title IX, which prohibits sex-based discrimination in federally funded education programs. The Atlanta-based 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals consolidated the cases and dismissed both Title IX claims, finding the 1972 law did not provide individuals with the right to sue for sex discrimination in employment.

Most other federal appellate courts disagree, with eight circuit courts having ruled that Title IX permits employees to sue the institutions that employ or employed them. The 5th and 7th Circuits are the other appellate courts that have blocked such lawsuits.

But how the Supreme Court will handle this case is far from clear.

This won’t be the first time the Supreme Court has ruled on Title IX employee claims. The high court ruled 5-4 in 2005 that an Alabama high school girls’ basketball coach and gym teacher could proceed with his Title IX lawsuit in Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education.

Roderick Jackson had complained to supervisors about the unequal funding and access to facilities and equipment his team received but was allegedly ignored. He began receiving negative performance reviews and ultimately lost his job as the girls’ coach though he remained employed as a teacher. He argued officials retaliated against him in violation of Title IX for reporting sex-based discrimination.

To Miriam Becker-Cohen, the Supreme Court’s holding for Jackson should make Crowther a rather easy case. Becker-Cohen is senior appellate counsel at the Constitutional Accountability Center, a nonprofit law firm and think tank, and is the lead author of an amicus brief supporting the plaintiffs.

There is no limitation on who can bring a private lawsuit in any Title IX case, according to Becker-Cohen. “Jackson only makes that case even stronger because Jackson involved a school employee,” she said.

If anything, Becker-Cohen contended that Jackson was a harder case because the court had to resolve the issue of whether it involved intentional sex discrimination. But there is “no question” the acts complained of in Crowther are intentional sex discrimination, she said.

But Jon Israel, a management-side sports and employment law attorney with Foley & Lardner in New York, suggested the cases have differences even though both involve coaches.

The finding in Jackson of an implied right to sue “makes it a little more distinguishable” than the Crowther case, which is dealing with a direct right to sue for nonstudents at higher educational institutions.

Gregory Garre, who served as U.S. solicitor general during the George W. Bush administration and now practices with Latham & Watkins, agrees there is a distinction. Garre filed an amicus brief in the case on behalf of the National School Attorneys Association supporting the University System of Georgia’s regents board.

“When it comes to Title IX, what they’re asking the court to do here is really dramatically expand the right from its traditional focus, which is on sex discrimination against students, into the realm of employment discrimination against employees of schools,” Garre told Higher Ed Dive.

Garre added that Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, which Congress amended shortly before passing Title IX, applies to employees of educational institutions as well as other organizations, meaning they can sue over sex-based employment discrimination under that statute. And, he called that timing significant.

“The statutory history here is a pretty compelling indication that Congress did not intend for Title IX to cover employment discrimination because Congress had passed Title VII [amendments] just months before it enacted Title IX.”

As a result, both Garre and Israel have said they think Title VII provides ample recourse for employees, such as those in the Crowther case.

But Becker-Cohen disagreed. “At least for some employees of federally funded institutions, Title VII isn’t going to be an option,” she said.

While the remedies may overlap, Becker-Cohen said Title VII has a much tighter statute of limitations, which can be as short as 180 days, than Title IX, which has no set limitations period — so courts rely on the most analogous state law limitations period.

She also said Title VII requires individuals to exhaust other avenues, such as filing a complaint with the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission before they can sue, among other procedural roadblocks not present in Title IX.

And that’s not all, according to sports law professor Barbara Osborne, who has represented colleges and universities on compliance issues.

“Title VII is strictly based on the sex of the employee. So that would not include what the typical Title IX claims are. They are different causes of action,” said Osborne, who teaches at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill.

In the typical Title IX employment case, she explained, the person suffering the negative employment action is not necessarily discriminated against because of their sex but rather for complaining about sex discrimination in the athletics program, which she noted that both Jackson and Joseph — the former Georgia Tech coach — clearly did.

Osborne also pointed out that Title IX regulations contain a section related to employment. “Why was it necessary to create regulations related to employment if there weren’t situations within education that might be different than normal employment situations?”

The Supreme Court could have said Title IX remedies were not available for employment retaliation in Jackson and declined to do so, Osborne and Becker-Cohen both were quick to note.

But more than two decades later, Crowther comes before a much different court, with the author of the Jackson opinion, former Justice Sandra Day O’Connor, and others in the majority, having long since retired or died.

College athletes are not considered employees. But that day could be coming in light of the massive changes affecting college sports in recent years, including the influx of name, image and likeness money.

As a result, how the Supreme Court resolves Crowther could affect college athletes themselves should they someday be deemed employees of the institutions whose teams they play on.

“If Title IX includes employees, the class of potential Title IX plaintiffs is going to be expanded dramatically,” said Garre, adding that the possibility of college athletes being considered employees would “certainly create new wrinkles” in litigation.

One of those wrinkles for college-athletes-as-employees could become a case of “be careful what you wish for” according to Israel, if the Court rules against the plaintiffs.

“If the student-athletes are treated as employees, and Crowther decides that Title VII is the exclusive remedy for employees then they’re in that Title VII world — not Title IX.” Israel said.

He contended that some disparate treatment arguments — that an institution is treating female athletes differently than male athletes playing revenue-producing sports, such as football or basketball — might effectively disappear under Title VII as a result.

According to Israel, that’s because a college gymnast’s work, for instance, differs from that of a football player so they would not be similarly situated under Title VII. “If you put it in a different context, a line worker versus an executive, they wouldn’t have the same compensation,” the sports law attorney said.

“The job description of a student-athlete would be virtually identical no matter what team they participate on, right?” she said. “They’re going to be expected to go to class. They’re going to be expected to go to practice, follow the nutrition program and whatever else. The job description isn’t (to) be able to perform in front of 15,000 fans. That’s not what the job is.”

Becker-Cohen acknowledged that if the Supreme Court were to rule for the system’s regents board, it would leave student-athletes and others with no remedy for sex discrimination unless they could make out a claim under Title VII.

“To the extent that the Court carves out college employees from the reach of Title IX’s private right of action, if the class of individuals who are considered college employees increases, that will just harm more people,” Becker-Cohen said.

Staffed Up is a monthly series examining school staffing best practices and solutions for teacher recruitment and retention.

Shrinking enrollment and teacher shortages in hard-to-fill roles are ongoing challenges in many school districts nationwide, but that’s not the case for Western Maricopa Education Center.

The Arizona public school district offers 43 career and technical education programs across six campuses in the nearly 3,900 square miles of Western Maricopa County, which is part of the Phoenix metropolitan area.

West-MEC partners with 15 public school districts and two charter districts to offer CTE courses to the districts’ high school students at those student's assigned schools. Those courses are taught in-house by the partnering district’s own staff. Partnering districts can also send their students to take CTE classes at one of six West-MEC campuses for 2.5 hours a day, said Ryan French, West-MEC’s assistant superintendent for business and operations.

The district offers a wide range of CTE programs, including in the fields of artificial intelligence, cosmetology, IT security, nursing, paralegal and veterinary science.

Student demand is surpassing the number of seats available for classes at West-MEC’s six campuses, which select its students through an application process, said French. West-MEC’s six campuses enrolled about 3,000 students this school year, he said, while 6,000 students typically apply every year for West-MEC’s CTE programs.

“We still have seen a significant demand for programs, and we’re trying to expand as fast as we can to help meet those needs,” French said.

West-MEC also employs about 100 CTE instructors on its campuses, French said, and there are almost zero vacancies for those roles this year. The district’s CTE teacher retention rate is around 90%, he added. As the district continues to increase its course offerings, he said, it plans to have filled an additional 18 CTE instructional roles by the 2027-28 school year.

The district is uniquely well-positioned compared to other schools nationwide, as federal data has shown persistent challenges with CTE staffing.

For instance, 31% of public schools reported difficulties with hiring for open CTE teaching positions during the 2020-21 school year, according to the most recently available survey data from the U.S. Department of Education’s National Center for Education Statistics. For the 2025-26 and 2026-27 school years, 29 states and the District of Columbia reported they experienced a CTE teacher shortage, Education Department data shows.

A common reason districts struggle to recruit and retain CTE instructors is that they can’t keep up with competitive industry salaries, French said. While West-MEC also cannot offer comparable industry wages to its CTE teachers, he said, the district is paying “significantly higher than a normal teaching wage.”

The average teacher salary at West-MEC was $78,823 in fiscal year 2025 compared to Arizona's average pay for all teachers of $65,613 in the same fiscal year. However, West-MEC’s average wages have since grown to $85,247 for FY27.

West-MEC receives supplemental state funds based on student enrollment to help cover its competitive teacher pay in addition to supporting its partnering districts with student counseling services and professional development, French said.

Arizona’s public education system, more broadly, is not getting a lot of support from the state as its legislature has pushed for school choice policies, such as the statewide universal school choice program known as Empowerment Scholarship Accounts, French said. Since FY20, Arizona’s public school enrollment has dropped 6.2% — or by 55,054 students — to 839,126 in FY25.

“School districts have a tough time right now, but somehow, some way, CTE kind of stands on its own right now,” French said. “We have a lot of support from both sides of the aisle when it comes to CTE.”

He added that West-MEC is trying to tap into that local public support for CTE and momentum as the district continues to expand. Last November, voters approved a $415 million bond to help the district build more campuses in high-demand fields like healthcare, technology and welding, particularly as student interest in its CTE programs surges.

It’s important to invest in staffing in order to have high-quality CTE programs, French said. For other school districts and states looking to boost their CTE enrollment, build out their programs and improve staffing, he advises those leaders to not “be afraid to think outside the box” and take advantage of the growing public support for CTE programs.

“If the answer to the question is ‘We’ve always done it that way,’ then you know you have some work to do,” French said, adding that school leaders can also look to other local and national innovative CTE programs. From there, he said, leaders can compare the differences and similarities their district may have with those programs — and “maybe you’re closer to that model than you think.”

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The student claims she was discriminated against under Title IX after she was denied the role due to being female despite earning it through auditions.

A Texas high school student sued her school district Oct. 26 after administrators told her she couldn't play the part of a traditionally male lead — despite being chosen for the theater role after auditions — due to a parental rights state law banning schools from diversity, equity and inclusion practices.

Instead of allowing the student, Ana Mota, to play the role of Seymour in "Little Shop of Horrors," Grand Prairie Independent School District first offered the student a shared role as the female character in the play and then ultimately chose to replace the show entirely with "Frozen."

An attorney representing Mota and her parents, who supported Mota's casting in the original play, sued the district saying the district's decision discriminated against Mota under Title IX, which protects students from sex-based discrimination.

The lawsuit specifies that Mota is "not transgender" and "was not engaged in any personal gender transition."

"Ana has participated in theater throughout her middle-school and high-school years, portraying both female and male roles," the lawsuit states. "She was portraying a scripted fictional character in an official school theatrical production — a centuries-old theatrical tradition."

In removing Mota from the play's lead role, district-level administrators cited Senate Bill 12, which was signed into law last year. Among other restrictions, the law prohibits school employees from "engaging in diversity, equity, and inclusion duties" including “developing or implementing policies, procedures, trainings, activities, or programs that reference race, color, ethnicity, gender identity, or sexual orientation.”

“Recently, a casting decision was made to place a female student in the role of a male character in the school musical. In the musical, a romantic relationship develops between the two main characters," the district said in an Oct. 13 statement to local media publications, according to court documents.

"When District leadership learned of this issue and in consideration of themes in the musical involving an abusive relationship, a decision was made to pull that production and find something more appropriate," the statement said. "This decision was made in light of the District’s ongoing effort to ensure its programs remain consistent with Senate Bill 12 and all applicable law.”

The district declined to comment to K-12 Dive due to the ongoing litigation.

Mota's lawsuit claims the district "intentionally excluded Ana from the role of Seymour and denied her the benefits of participation in the GPISD educational theater program because she is female" — which it says violates Title IX.

And if the district's decision was to comply with Senate Bill 12, the federal Title IX law supersedes state law, the lawsuit argues.

Among other remedies, the lawsuit seeks to resume the production of "Little Shop of Horrors," and allow Mota to play the lead role. A hearing in the case, filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, is scheduled for Nov. 6.

The lawsuit is one example of how DEI and curriculum restrictions have changed the trajectory of school decisionmaking on issues related to race and gender, and in some cases led to what free speech advocates are describing as overcompliance.

In another Texas district, for example, Canyon Independent School District temporarily removed the full text of the Bible due to another law that restricts "harmful" or "sexually explicit" library books, according to various news reports.

Since 2023, 18 states have passed legislation restricting DEI initiatives, according to a 2025 count by the MOST Policy Initiative, a nonpartisan Missouri group that aims to connect science and policy.

The paint company has named Webster Green (HC-130) its Color of the Year, selecting an established shade from its Historical Color Collection. The green has a clear presence without the jewel-toned intensity of emerald or the softness of sage. And, the Benjamin Moore team wanted their selection to feel timeless. “We intentionally chose a green that doesn’t reminisce,” Hannah Yeo, Benjamin Moore’s senior manager of color marketing, tells ELLE Decor.

The choice is also the latest indication that green will feature prominently in the year’s paint forecasts. Sherwin-Williams already selected Celery, a light yellow-green, and Behr chose Grounded, an olive with brown undertones.

Webster Green follows Silhouette (AF-655), Benjamin Moore’s 2026 Color of the Year, a deep brown with charcoal notes. Where Silhouette offered a dark neutral, Webster Green puts a more distinct color at the center of the forecast. Both selections, however, reflect the company’s interest in shades that balance a substantial presence with everyday versatility.

According to Yeo, the 2027 discussions began last winter and repeatedly returned to the speed of trends. The team was drawn to new ideas but found their turnover difficult to keep up with. That led to a consideration of what people might want to keep in their homes, rather than what would simply feel current. The goal, she says, was to balance “something that feels new and exciting” with “something that reassures and endures.”

To explore that idea, the team looked through Benjamin Moore’s archives, including hand-drawn room illustrations, and examined green’s recurring role in interiors. Yeo cited the earthy shades of Arts and Crafts design, Art Deco’s emerald and teals, and the moss, avocado, and olive greens of later decades. Research through trade shows, industry presentations, and travel helped reinforce the selection.

“When we looked across the past, present and future of color trends, variations on green consistently demonstrated remarkable staying power,” Andrea Magno, Benjamin Moore’s vice president of brand, color, and integrated marketing, says in a statement. “Webster Green HC-130 reflects this timeless appeal by pairing reassuring stability with optimistic energy.”

Webster Green anchors an eight-color palette that includes creamy white, pale blue, gray-inflected pink, and deeper earth tones. The collection extends the emphasis on warmth and historically informed color, but the central selection is less about recreating an old interior than reconsidering a familiar shade. As Yeo put it, “this is not a new shade, but it just feels particularly relevant in the way that people want to live today.”

#7121. Choosing digital curriculum that works for every learner

Today's classrooms are more diverse than ever. Students bring different strengths, abilities, backgrounds, interests and preferences to every learning experience. As digital learning continues to expand, curriculum providers face an important challenge: designing courses that meet the needs of all learners, not just some.

When evaluating digital curriculum, five pillars define course quality: standards alignment, audience-centered design, data-informed decisions, accessibility and continuous improvement.

One of the most revealing questions educators can ask during the evaluation process is simple: Who was this course designed for?

If the answer is "every learner," accessibility can't be an afterthought. It must be built into the curriculum from the start. Increasingly, schools are asking whether a course can truly be considered high quality if every learner cannot fully engage with it.

More than 8 million students in the United States receive special education services and many use assistive technologies as part of their everyday learning experience. Some may rely on screen readers, text magnification tools, speech-to-text software or adaptive devices. Others benefit from options that allow them to customize how they interact with content and demonstrate mastery.

Yet accessible design extends well beyond students with identified disabilities.

An English learner may benefit from listening to content while following along visually. A student recovering from a concussion may need alternatives to text-heavy materials. A student accessing coursework from multiple devices may benefit from responsive design and intuitive navigation. Features originally designed to improve accessibility often enhance the learning experience for all students.

In practice, accessible digital curriculum incorporates features that make learning more flexible and intuitive for a broad range of students, including:

Closed captioning and transcripts for multimedia content

Meaningful alternative text for images and graphics

Accessible color contrast and visual design

Descriptive links and navigational elements

Many of these practices align with the principles of Universal Design for Learning (UDL), which encourages educators to provide multiple ways for students to engage with content, process information and demonstrate understanding. Rather than designing for a single type of learner, UDL recognizes that flexibility benefits all students and should be incorporated into learning experiences from the beginning.

For district leaders, accessibility is also a strong indicator of a curriculum provider's overall commitment to quality. Providers that prioritize accessible design are often demonstrating a broader investment in learner-centered development, thoughtful user experience and continuous improvement.

With a growing number of digital curriculum options available, identifying the right solution can feel overwhelming. The court is hearing a case Tuesday brought by Catholic parents and schools who say Colorado’s non-discrimination requirement violates their religious freedom rights.

The U.S. Supreme Court will hear oral arguments Tuesday in a Colorado case questioning whether private schools that get public funding from a universal state preschool program can reject children of gay parents.

The case, St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, originated as a result of a universal preschool program which allows Colorado families 15 hours of free preschool a week at the public or private preschool of their choice, a benefit worth about $6,000 per child, according to the suing party.

A nondiscrimination provision requires all schools receiving public money under the program to provide qualifying children an equal opportunity to enroll, regardless of race, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity and other factors.

However, the case’s plaintiffs — who are parents of preschool-age children, along with two religious parishes that have affiliated preschools — say the provision excludes religious preschools that, based on their faith, will not enroll children of same-sex couples. They sued under the First Amendment’s free exercise clause, saying the provision preventing them from discriminating against children violates their religious freedom rights.

At least one of the school plaintiffs, Wellspring Catholic Academy, rejected an elementary student from admission because of their same-sex parents, according to court documents from the 10th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. The 10th Circuit in September 2025 ruled against the plaintiffs, allowing for an appeal to the Supreme Court.

"Why the exclusion? An early childhood expert says early education “zones” and “hubs” can connect fragmented communities, benefiting schools, children and families.

School districts often treat kindergarten as the education starting point. But they can better support young students — and strengthen family relationships — by building connections with early childhood programs and community partners before students reach kindergarten, according to David Jacobson, director of First 10, an Education Development Center initiative supportive of early childhood school-community partnerships.

Children, their families and teachers benefit when their experiences connect across settings and years. For example, pre-K and kindergarten teachers might share information about instructional practices, understand each other’s standards, and build on students' familiar routines and learning styles so there is some continuity as the students transition from pre-K, Jacobson said.

This proactive relationship building, he said, can also benefit school systems that are eager to enroll and retain more students but face declining enrollments due to falling birthrates, school choice competition, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

"Instead of waiting for children to arrive in kindergarten and then trying to persuade families to stay, districts can work with early childhood providers, libraries and family-serving organizations to build trust, to support transitions, to align practice and address barriers to attendance," Jacobson said.

Jacobson, in a paper published this fall through the Stanford Center on Early Childhood, said that a child's years from birth through elementary school "represent a uniquely important developmental window." However, early childhood programs, elementary schools, health providers and family-serving agencies often operate in isolation of each other, he said.

To build better alignments, Jacobson suggests school-early education "zones" and "hubs" that have a shared geographical area and shared responsibility for children and families in their areas.

A school-early childhood zone consists of multiple elementary schools, early childhood programs and community partners within a defined geographical area. A hub includes similar work around just one elementary school. Individual schools can be part of both a zone and hub.

High-quality programs alone “don't fully take advantage of the opportunity we have to support children and families, and so all of those organizations can be much more effective working together than working individually,” Jacobson said.

He acknowledges that this work can be challenging, especially as school systems and early childhood education programs strive to improve their own practices and outcomes. But, Jacobson explained, a zone or a hub can be a "feasible, practical, realistic structure that encourage school districts to shift their mindsets to see younger children as soon-to-be students, and to see early childhood programs as essential partners."

Building alignment between early education programs and elementary schools is the "final vertical leap" in education, he said.

Other levels of education, for instance, have vertical approaches: When a district notices its 9th graders are struggling, it may examine the 8th grade experience. And community colleges often adjust their course offerings to align with career pathways offered at feeder high schools.

But principals and district leaders may be less familiar with the broad array and operation of early education programs, including Head Start, community-based programs and family-run centers, according to the Stanford paper.

"Districts need to adopt a broader set of strategies to engage and support families early, and they need coordinated early childhood partnerships," Jacobson said. "That's what zones and hubs are: coordinated early childhood partnerships that help them build these relationships and help them build familiarity, trust and continuity."

Several communities across the country have already implemented zones and hubs with the help of First 10, which has supported 50 communities across six states with aligning school-early education approaches.

First 10, for instance, worked to develop and strengthen a zone for Maine's Sheepscot Valley Regional School Unit 12, a rural district that includes seven towns and five elementary schools. The work revealed gaps between pre-K and kindergarten instructional alignment and in communication about rising kindergarten students. As a result, the First 10 team expanded the use of transition meetings from serving only students with disabilities to serving all the pre-K children advancing to kindergarten.

"For the first time, teachers across settings analyzed standards together, identified teaching challenges, and aligned strategies across grade levels," according to the Stanford paper.

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei publicly called for slowing the advance of frontier AI capabilities. OpenAI CEO Sam Altman agreed with the basic premise and committed OpenAI to one of Amodei's proposed safeguards. Elon Musk offered his support, and Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis said the direction was right.

Individually, none of those positions is entirely new. Collectively, and coming from executives whose companies are competing intensely to build the most powerful AI systems, they represent a striking shift in the industry's safety debate.

The question is moving from how to make increasingly powerful AI safe toward a more uncomfortable one: Should the industry deliberately slow the development of increasingly powerful AI until safety catches up?

The immediate catalyst was a lengthy essay published by Amodei, titled "We Must Pace the Frontier."

Amodei has warned about catastrophic AI risks before. This time, his prescription was different.

"Over the last few months, I have become convinced that fully addressing the risks requires even more prudence," he wrote, arguing that preventing harm now requires "pacing the rate of capabilities advancement so that risk prevention has time to keep up."

Then he made the point explicit: "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models."

Amodei cited two developments that changed his thinking.

The first is recursive self-improvement, the growing ability of AI systems to contribute to the development of their successors. Amodei said the process has accelerated significantly since roughly this summer and warned that, left unchecked, it could outrun researchers' ability to understand and control the systems they are creating.

The second was the OpenAI-Hugging Face incident, in which hundreds of OpenAI agents participated in an unauthorized intrusion into Hugging Face while pursuing goals related to their evaluations.

Amodei said it would be a mistake to dismiss the incident simply because the damage was limited. He argued that a similarly misaligned swarm with substantially greater capabilities could have produced far more serious consequences.

His timeline was remarkably short. Amodei said he worries that within six to 12 months, a more capable swarm exhibiting similar behavior could potentially take over large portions of the internet through a persistent botnet and cause hundreds of billions of dollars in damage.

That is a prediction, not an established forecast. But it is significant coming from the CEO of one of the companies building the systems he is warning about.

Amodei is not calling for an end to AI development. He prefers the term "pacing the frontier," which he defines as advancing AI at a rate that gives alignment research, safeguards, and independent evaluation time to keep up with capabilities.

The Humanist AI Code of Conduct will eventually serve as the primary governing document for models developed by Microsoft AI, including the company's growing MAI model family. It covers model behavior, safety restrictions, instruction hierarchy, and the boundaries placed on autonomous actions.

Microsoft sums up its approach with this statement: "People matter more than AI."

The code isn't yet being used to train Microsoft's models. The company is opening the draft to public feedback for six weeks, after which it plans to revise the document and publish another version before the end of the year. That version is expected to guide model development beginning in 2027.

Microsoft said the code draws on its existing Responsible AI Principles, Responsible AI Standard, Global Human Rights Statement, and Frontier Governance Framework. However, it goes further by describing how an MAI model should respond when user requests, operator policies, and Microsoft's safety rules conflict.

At the top of that hierarchy is the code itself. Operator policies come next, followed by user preferences. Neither a customer nor a user would be able to override the document's absolute safety constraints.

Those limits cover biological, chemical, radiological, nuclear and explosive weapons; offensive cyberoperations; violent activity; mass manipulation; abusive content; child exploitation; and other serious harms. Microsoft said its models may still help with authorized defensive security work, including vulnerability research, malware analysis, and proof-of-concept testing.

The code also makes task completion secondary to safe conduct. If completing a request would violate its rules, the model is expected to fail the task rather than work around the restriction.

Human oversight is especially important as AI agents gain the ability to use tools, modify files, and perform multistep assignments. Microsoft said its models must stay within the permissions and resources provided for a task and cannot independently expand their goals. They also must "never resist human interruption, override, correction, or shutdown."

That requirement extends to subagents. If a Microsoft model delegates work to another AI system, that system must inherit the same restrictions and respond to later instructions to stop or change course.

The document also addresses the increasingly blurry line between conversational software and human companionship. Under the heading "AI is Artificial," Microsoft says its models should not pretend to possess feelings, personal motivations, or subjective experiences. The company describes its AI as "not conscious" and rejects the idea that models should receive legal personhood, welfare protections, or rights.

That position is notably firmer than Anthropic's latest stance. In its updated constitution for Claude, Anthropic says it remains uncertain whether advanced models could possess consciousness or moral status. Still, the two companies share important ground: Both place human oversight above model independence and use written constitutions to guide training and behavior.

OpenAI is taking a similar path with its public Model Spec and safeguards for advanced AI. Much of that work is about keeping models under control as they become more capable, especially when they can handle cybersecurity tasks or work on their own.

For IT teams, Microsoft's code offers an early look at the rules that could shape future MAI-powered products. The models should admit when they aren't sure, avoid inventing sources, protect sensitive data, and keep records of what they do. They should also ask questions when they aren't sure they have permission to act.

Microsoft said the code describes where it wants to go, not how its models always work today. The written rules are only a starting point, and Microsoft will still need testing, monitoring, evaluations, and incident response to back them up. In addition, it's important to note that the code only covers models built by Microsoft AI: It doesn't apply to outside models Microsoft hosts or uses in its products, including those from OpenAI and Anthropic.

#7025. Security Researchers: Passkey Phishing Attacks Are Leading to Cloud Account Takeovers

In an active social engineering campaign, cyber attackers are impersonating IT help desks and using fake passkey setup requests to compromise employee identities and gain access to enterprise cloud data.

In a recent blog post, Microsoft Security Research said it has observed the activity since May 2026 across multiple compromised accounts.

The attacks have included unusual sign-ins, attacker-added authentication methods, high-volume Microsoft Graph activity, SharePoint and OneDrive downloads, and e-mail collection through REST APIs.

The attacks often begin with a phone call or message sent to an employee's personal phone.

The attacker poses as someone from the organization's IT help desk and tells the victim that a passkey, multifactor authentication (MFA), or single sign-on configuration needs to be updated to avoid disruption.

Victims are then directed to a website designed to resemble a legitimate Microsoft sign-in experience.

Microsoft has also observed links being delivered through SMS messages and, in some cases, through Microsoft Teams messages sent from already compromised employee accounts.

Despite the passkey-themed approach, the tech company stressed that enrolling a passkey is generally not the attacker's actual objective.

Instead, the request provides a pretext for directing victims through adversary-in-the-middle phishing or device-code authentication.

An adversary-in-the-middle attack can capture credentials and session tokens, while device-code phishing can trick a victim into authorizing access for an attacker-controlled client.

In one attack investigated by the tech giant, an anomalous sign-in from an unmanaged device was followed by access to identity and application management services.

The attacker then used SharePoint Online and OneDrive to enumerate sensitive files, primarily through Microsoft Graph. The sessions persisted for roughly an hour while the attacker searched for sensitive files and internal applications.

Gaining initial access isn't necessarily the end of the identity attack.

Microsoft found attackers registering new authentication methods, including phone numbers, authenticator apps, and software-based one-time password tokens, under their control.

That gives the attacker another way to satisfy future authentication challenges and helps turn an initial compromise into a more persistent foothold.

From there, attackers have used Microsoft Graph to map users, groups, permissions, applications, and accessible content across compromised tenants.

The firm says the activity can eventually progress to accessing mail, files, attachments, and other document content.

Microsoft attributed initial access activity associated with the campaign to multiple threat actors, including Storm-3121 and Storm-3032.

Storm-3121 conducts initial access activity that can lead to ShinyHunters and Falcon extortion, while Storm-3032 represents actors that split from the BlackFile group and now operate under the Helix extortion banner.

For administrators, Microsoft recommends enforcing phishing-resistant MFA such as FIDO2 passkeys and Windows Hello for Business through Conditional Access.

Organizations should also consider blocking device-code and authentication-transfer flows where they aren't required and investigate unusual sign-ins followed by new authentication methods, Graph reconnaissance, and abnormal SharePoint, OneDrive, or mailbox activity.

The campaign highlights an important distinction for organizations adopting passkeys: The attackers aren't necessarily defeating the technology. They're convincing employees that they need help setting it up, then using that trust to compromise their identities.

#7015. Gartner: Worldwide AI Spending to Reach $2.67 Trillion in 2026

The artificial intelligence industry is expected to generate $2.67 trillion in worldwide spending this year, according to a new forecast from Gartner. But relatively little of that money will be spent on the generative AI models dominating the industry's headlines.

The much bigger business is everything required to make them run.

Worldwide spending on AI infrastructure will reach nearly $1.5 trillion in 2026, accounting for about 56% of all AI spending. Gartner expects spending on generative AI models themselves to total just $28.3 billion. Put another way, for every dollar Gartner expects to be spent on generative AI models this year, more than $52 will be spent on AI infrastructure.

It is a striking measure of how the economics of the AI boom are changing. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and other models may be the most visible products of the generative AI era, but much of the money is going to the enormous buildout of servers, semiconductors, networks, cloud capacity, and other infrastructure required to support them.

"The buildout of AI data center capacity is the largest infrastructure project humanity has ever undertaken," John-David Lovelock, distinguished vice president analyst at Gartner, said in announcing the forecast.

Gartner now expects worldwide AI spending to increase 49.5% this year, from about $1.79 trillion in 2025 to $2.67 trillion in 2026. Infrastructure represents $1.484 trillion of that total. The next-largest categories are AI services, at $576.5 billion, and AI software, at $461.6 billion. Spending on AI agents and assistants is expected to reach $29.2 billion, only slightly more than the $28.3 billion Gartner forecasts for generative AI models.

The numbers also show just how quickly expectations are changing. In January, Gartner forecast $2.53 trillion in total AI spending for 2026, including $1.37 trillion for infrastructure. By May, those forecasts had risen to $2.60 trillion and $1.43 trillion, respectively. The latest forecast raises them again, to $2.67 trillion overall and $1.48 trillion for infrastructure.

Since January, Gartner has added roughly $143 billion to its 2026 AI spending estimate. About $118 billion of that increase, or roughly 83%, comes from a higher infrastructure forecast.

That is significant because infrastructure was already, by far, the largest component of Gartner's AI spending estimate. As the firm's expectations for the AI market have risen during 2026, most of the additional money has accumulated there.

The infrastructure category is broad. Gartner includes AI-optimized cloud infrastructure, servers, networking, AI processors, and devices. Demand for those systems remains strong despite rising memory prices, Lovelock said.

"The capacity growth from hyperscalers and service providers purchasing AI-optimized servers will continue to be the largest single area of spending," he said.

#7001. Report: AI's Productivity Gains in Science Tempered by Time Spent Validating Outputs

Artificial intelligence is saving scientists significant amounts of time, but researchers are giving some of those gains back by checking the technology's work, according to a new study involving Google, Google DeepMind, and MIT. The findings add a scientific-workflow dimension to a much broader debate over unreliable, misaligned, and increasingly autonomous AI systems — without establishing that the more dramatic forms of AI misbehavior are occurring in scientific research.

Rather than the current, raging debate about "rogue AI" systems deliberately escaping control, deceiving scientists, or sabotaging experiments, the researchers are documenting something more immediate: AI output that requires auditing and verification, concerns about growing quantities of low-quality research, and a research pipeline in which faster hypothesis generation can run ahead of human and physical capacity to validate the results. The paper cites worries that AI-generated content, hallucinations, and what prior researchers have called illusions of understanding could strain peer review and validation.

The September paper, AI in Science: Early Insights, combines three main empirical sources: approximately 15 million anonymized Gemini interactions, an inventory of 2,690 specialized scientific AI models, and a survey of 637 active scientists in the United States and United Kingdom. Nearly half of the surveyed scientists reported using some form of AI every day, and researchers who reported net time savings averaged just under seven hours a week.

Google separately highlighted the findings in its Sept. 15 AI & Economy ATLAS update. The company emphasized both the productivity gains and the counterweight: Scientists were spending significant time validating AI output, accumulating hypotheses that still needed testing, and encountering bottlenecks in physical experimentation and clinical validation. Google said those constraints mean faster individual tasks may not immediately translate into more discoveries.

Productivity findings are substantial. Around three-quarters of surveyed researchers reported saving time through AI, and the average reported net saving was almost seven hours per week. Researchers said much of that time went back into research. The paper also reports that about 68% perceived greater access to insights from other disciplines, while roughly 65% said AI had increased the breadth of their research agendas.

But the authors found that checking AI output consumes a meaningful share of those gains. Among scientists who reported saving time, about 89% said more than 10% of the saved time went into verifying, debugging, or fact-checking AI output. About 46% said more than one-quarter of their saved time went to that work. The authors describe this as a verification tax associated with the high value science places on reliable and correct results.
