The report noted that “strong enrollment growth drove increases in tuition and fee revenue” at Iowa’s three state universities during fiscal 2026, which ended June 30. Those revenues came in half a percentage point over the regents’ budget.

As for enrollment, head count across the three institutions came to 71,872 in fall 2025, an increase of about 2% from the previous year.

This fall, undergraduate enrollment at Iowa’s public universities increased by 0.2%, or 98 students, according to a separate report also prepared for the regents’ Nov. 11 meeting. But overall enrollment fell 0.5%, or by 352 students, driven by a loss of graduate enrollment.

Like the system overall, the University of Iowa and Iowa State University largely saw their undergraduate populations hold steady. But those numbers mask losses in new first-year undergraduate enrollment this fall — down by 5.3% at UI and 4.7% at Iowa State. Meanwhile, new first-year undergrads increased by 10.3% at the smaller University of Northern Iowa.

In the fiscal report, the regents staff invoked the long anticipated enrollment cliff, with the numbers of traditional-aged college students estimated to begin declining after this year.

While Iowa is expected to fare better than many of its neighboring states when it comes to demographics, it won’t be left unaffected.

The number of high school graduates in Iowa could decline 9% between 2026 and 2041, according to the Western Interstate Commission for Higher Education’s latest estimates, cited in the regents’ enrollment report. That compares to a projected 29% decline in Illinois, 13% decline in Minnesota and 16% decline in Wisconsin.

But state colleges don’t recruit in a vacuum. Regents staff pointed out in their report that other states “from which the Regent universities receive many out-of-state students are expected to experience much larger drops in their high school graduate population.”

That could weigh on Iowa universities’ finances as colleges compete for a shrinking pool of students in the region — and declines in out-of-state students would have outsized impacts on Iowa universities’ budgets given the higher tuition prices those students pay.

“While enrollment and retention rates remain strong, institutions are closely monitoring key indicators — including applications, campus visits and housing deposits — to inform long-term enrollment management planning,” the fiscal report said.

#7007. What can K-12 expect from Democrats’ big win in 2026 elections?

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Policy experts see shifts on the horizon from the blue wave, from changes to interagency agreements to rising union influence.

Voters handed a sweeping victory to Democrats during the 2026 midterm elections, with the party flipping five states in gubernatorial races and taking control of Congress with a majority in both the House and Senate.

The outcome is expected to trigger changes at the federal and state levels on issues related to education, ranging from resistance to the Trump administration's stance on diversity, equity and inclusion issues to more teachers union involvement on policy issues.

"What likely won’t change much is the day-to-day operation of schooling," said Jonathan Collins, assistant professor at Columbia University's Teachers College and the executive director of the Center for Educational Equity, in an email. "Don’t expect your child’s homework assignments to suddenly change, at least not nationally. But the major shifts in governors could mean changes to testing and supplemental funding allocations in specific states led by Democrats.”

Over the last two years, the Trump administration — through changes to the U.S. Department of Education and other agencies’ handling of grant funds and investigations — has pressured states and districts to comply with a number of anti-DEI and anti-LGBTQ+ measures.

“The biggest change is Congress pressuring the President to retreat on the anti-DEI initiatives, many of which have targeted college and university programs as well as K-12 teacher training, curricula and library book offerings," said Collins. "Look for Democrats to draw a hard line on supporting the President’s next budget proposal (or debt ceiling raise), and for these types of policy positions to be part of the conditions."

The National LGBTQ Task Force said in a Nov. 4 statement that it hopes federal lawmakers "stop the relentless attacks our communities have endured" over the past two years and "continue to focus on public education."

Abe Saffer, director of government relations for the Council of Administrators of Special Education, said he expects the incoming Democrat-controlled Congress to challenge the U.S. Department of Education's interagency agreements.

The Education Department began outsourcing parts of its day-to-day operations in 2025 with agreements that have included areas such as special education, literacy and teacher preparation. There are now 14 such K-12 and higher education agreements that include the management and oversight of grants, compliance with civil rights laws and technical assistance in areas like statewide family engagement centers and career and technical education.

Supporters say the transfer of responsibilities reduces federal bureaucracy and improves services. But opponents, including a few Republican lawmakers, voiced concerns that some agreements would actually cause more confusion for grantees and a siloing of Education Department responsibilities.

The issue has caused deep divisions among congressional lawmakers as the Education Department touts the agreements as progress toward dismantling and eventually closing the agency.

Bobby Scott, ranking member of the House Education and Workforce Committee, said in a statement to K-12 Dive that he intends to investigate the Trump administration's approach to education policy. Scott said he has sent letters to the heads of agencies including the Department of Education directing them to preserve "records, documents, data, or information" related to operations.

"I will use Congress’ oversight tools and leave no stone unturned as I fight for policies that actually deliver for the American public," he said.

Democrats’ wins on Tuesday may mean more teachers' union influence over education policies, according to Michael Petrilli, president of the Thomas B. Fordham Institute, a conservative-leaning education think tank.

"Now the unions will expect something in return," he said in a statement to K-12 Dive. "In some places, that may [mean] additional funding for public education — which is fine. But they might also want to curb the growth of parental choice (both private school choice and charter schools), or eliminate differential pay programs, or add new collective bargaining requirements. None of those moves would be good for kids."

Randi Weingarten, president of the American Federation of Teachers, one of the nation's largest teachers unions, said in a Nov. 3 statement that the election results show that those "who supported public schools and rejected private school vouchers triumphed — statewide in Texas, Iowa, Michigan and Ohio, and in countless congressional districts and school board races."

"That means strengthening public education and higher education, increasing wages, making healthcare affordable and accessible, and rebuilding our democracy. And after the sea change at the state level, there must also be progress in the priorities of governors and representatives," Weingarten said in a Nov. 3 statement. “We will act on these priorities starting tomorrow."

One Republican-led K-12 education program that Democratic lawmakers may have a harder time unraveling, Saffer said, is the Education Freedom Tax Credit, which was included in 2025's One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

The program, set to launch Jan. 1, 2027, will allow families to use taxpayer-supported scholarships for private school tuition and public school expenses like tutoring, services for students with disabilities, books, computers and other costs, the proposed rule said. States must opt in to participate, and 30 have indicated their interest to the IRS as of Oct. 22.

The program allows taxpayer-donors to contribute money to scholarships for both private and public school students.

Jeanne Allen, founder and CEO of the Center for Education Reform, said in an Oct. 30 article in Forbes that the expansion of school choice is not due to President Donald Trump or the Republican party, but because of "parents demanding something better for their children, educators determined to create it, and leaders willing to challenge a system that too often puts institutions ahead of students."

Saffer said his hope is that the tax credit program will eventually be eliminated or reformed to better support public schools, noting that students with disabilities have lower rates of enrollment in private schools overall compared to public schools.

"Frankly, using public money to fund private schools that don't accept kids with disabilities is just untenable for us," he said.

#7000. How to navigate AI adoption — with faculty and student input

Let Higher Ed Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

Artificial intelligence is transforming the mission of public education and “can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” one instructor said.

Colleges and universities nationwide have faced waves of student protests pushing back on institutional policies that embrace the use of artificial intelligence tools on campus.

That includes students and faculty at Columbia University who in May railed against the use of AI to announce graduates' names during commencement ceremonies, and students and employees at Emory University who in April called on administrators to remove AI-powered surveillance cameras from campus.

Universities have used AI tools to assist with a host of administrative functions, such as scheduling courses and nudging students to register for classes. In addition, many college officials are pushing to embed instruction on AI into curricula as businesses increasingly use generative AI platforms like OpenAI's ChatGPT and Google’s Gemini.

The embrace of AI has caused tension among students and faculty wary about its environmental, ethical, pedagogical, social and academic integrity impacts, said Victor Lee, faculty lead at the Stanford Accelerator for Learning's initiative to support educators in navigating the AI era.

Holding intentional conversations with students and faculty about AI use on campus can help ease concerns, said Lee.

Faculty members aren’t necessarily opposed to the technology, experts say, but training on the tools is spotty and they would like to be part of conversations about how their institutions use AI.

Robert Ovetz, a San José State University professor, is among the faculty members who have vocally opposed universities integrating AI into curricula with little to no input from faculty or their unions.

“This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top,” said Ovetz, who is co-author of the forthcoming book “Organizing Against AI in Higher Education.”

SJSU formally launched an initiative called AI Everywhere in fall 2025 with the aim of ethically and responsibly integrating the technology throughout campus. The initiative is part of an effort to prepare students for the future workforce, said Sandra Hirsh, the university's special assistant to the provost for AI initiatives.

It’s also part of a larger push by the California State University system, which signed contracts with several AI-focused tech companies in 2025, including a nearly $17 million deal with Open AI to provide ChatGPT Edu to more than 460,000 students and 63,000 faculty and staff throughout its 22 campuses. CSU renewed its contract with OpenAI this year to the tune of $13 million annually for three years.

By the start of the 2027-28 academic year, SJSU expects to have 28 AI-related programs and 101 courses, Hirsh said. That would represent a 75% and 36% increase, respectively, since fall 2025.

The university also launched a host of new AI events and initiatives, including faculty training on one of tech company Nvidia’s platforms. In the same vein, it worked with the city of San Jose to open the AI Center for Civic and Social Good, a space at a joint public and university academic library where community residents, students and workers can use AI education tools, Hirsh said.

"This is the transformation of the very mission of public education, and it can’t just be implemented one way from the top."

While the scale of SJSU's AI efforts are significant, it's hardly the only institution to embrace the technology. Almost every institution is using or plans to use AI in some capacity, said Hironao Okahana, formerly managing researcher and chief of planning and impact at the American Council on Education.

In many places, that has translated into new courses. Over one-third of surveyed faculty members, 37%, said their institution had created new AI-focused classes, according to a report released in January by the American Association of Colleges and Universities and Elon University, which polled over 1,000 instructors in fall 2025.

And as of last July, a quarter of senior higher ed leaders reported adopting generative AI technologies at their institutions for student support, with another 22% planning to do so within the next six months, according to an ACE fall 2025 survey. Another 43% said they planned to adopt AI technologies within the next one to two years.

Many students, meanwhile, have issues with the ways large language models use data without their knowledge and consent, and how these systems reproduce and amplify existing forms of discrimination in society, among a host of other concerns, said Lindsay Weinberg, a professor and director of Purdue University’s Tech Justice Lab.

And some students are apprehensive about their universities forging partnerships with companies that use AI for military purposes, Weinberg said.

In April, SJSU students protested their university’s ties to companies including Nvidia, OpenAI, and Google for that reason, Spartan Media Group reported.

Many faculty share the students' concerns. And both faculty and students express misgivings about the environmental consequences from AI and how data centers cause noise pollution and negatively impact local water and electrical systems, said Weinberg.

Students are also hearing widespread reports about how AI will automate entry-level jobs, shrinking their job opportunities, Ovetz added.

Faculty have broad worries on AI’s impact on higher education, said C. Edward Watson, vice president for digital innovation at AAC&U and co-author of the association’s faculty survey report.

Nearly 52% of staff and faculty at CSU institutions indicated they were skeptical of the benefits of AI in higher education, with about 85% indicating they were concerned about the ethical use of the technology, according to 2025 system survey data.

A nationwide survey from AAC&U also highlighted additional sweeping concerns from faculty. About three-quarters of faculty said cheating spiked on their campuses when generative AI tools became widespread and that they believed the technology would impact degree integrity.

Meanwhile, nearly all faculty members, 95%, expressed concern about students becoming overreliant on AI tools, and large majorities indicated the technology would diminish students’ critical thinking skills and attention spans, according to the report.

Overall, Ovetz said, many educators say that by using AI, students are cheating themselves out of the reasons they are attending college — to learn and expand their understanding, consciousness and awareness.

For some students, their opposition to AI is part of broader resistance to technology and social media shifting from being optional to seemingly mandatory in today’s society — including on campuses, said Lucy Jackson, a sophomore at Binghamton University, in New York.

Jackson was an early member of the Luddite Club, a New York City-based anti-tech youth group that has turned into a global movement with roughly 40 campus and regional chapters over the past five years.

When students enroll at a university, they are expecting to receive an education, not a requirement that they have to interact with certain technologies, said Jackson.

But some students are using AI tools to help with their homework or exams, giving them an unfair advantage, and getting a “way out of education” — allowing them to earn a degree “without having to do much,” Jackson said.

“That creates a culture that isn’t necessarily something that everybody wants to live in or that everyone wants to follow,” said Jackson. “That’s where a lot of the pushback comes from.”

Despite the concerns, faculty aren’t broadly rejecting AI tools, Ovetz said. Many view generative AI as an interesting tool to incorporate into their teaching, for example by using chatbots to help students engage with course material, said Ovetz.

At CSU, 61% of faculty and staff said they regularly use AI-powered tools or applications when teaching, researching or performing their work, and 64% say it positively affects those tasks, according to the survey of CSU campuses.

Higher ed leaders have been grappling with how to integrate AI literacy into the curriculum, who should be teaching courses on how to use AI, what type of professional development should be required for instructors and what type of tools they should provide to faculty and students, Watson said.

While colleges and universities are good at adding new courses to meet student needs,they are not good at doing it fast, said Watson. It usually takes a few semesters of discussions with faculty and employers to determine what students need to know, he said.

That was reflected in the AAC&U survey, with 68% of faculty reporting their institutions had not prepared educators to use generative AI “for effective teaching and mentoring.” Less than half reported their institutions had written guidelines for AI use, and just 13% had adopted AI literacy as “a general learning outcome.”

That’s despite a widespread belief among faculty members that it’s important to teach students about issues related to AI during class, the AAC&U survey found. Large majorities stated it was important to teach students about inaccurate statements produced by GenAI tools, how the tools are used to produce misinformation and deep fakes, and how they erode copyright and authorship, according to the survey.

It has been largely left to individual professors to design or restructure their courses with AI, said Lee. The vast majority of faculty, 87%, said they have created their own policies on students’ use of generative AI for assessments, projects and tests, the survey found.

That’s not surprising, however, as decisions about what faculty teach are not usually made top-down, Lee said.

“The law and philosophy of academic freedom and trusting the learning expertise of the faculty leaves a little bit more of a hands-off orientation,” Lee said.

When administrators form partnerships with tech companies and push technologies without discussing those initiatives with unions or faculty members on their campuses, they often draw the ire of the students and employees most impacted by those decisions, said Weinberg.

Last year, the California Faculty Association filed an unfair labor practice charge against CSU, alleging the university system failed to notify the union when it launched an AI initiative in 2025 and refused to meet to discuss faculty rights and the impacts that it would have. CFA represents some 29,000 professors, lecturers and other workers at CSU.

The union complaint underscored the need for administrators to slow down on AI adoption so those initiatives can go through the shared governance process and be bargained over with academic unions, said Ovetz, who has been involved in the bargaining efforts.

CFA in February introduced an article in their negotiations with the university to protect faculty from being required to use the tools and provide them with intellectual property protections. Those negotiations have been ongoing for a year and a half, said Ovetz, but no agreement or contract has yet been reached.

“We are planning to vote to strike in the coming months, including over AI,” Ovetz said.

Those protections are important, as workloads increase for faculty who have to learn how to use AI tools as well as constantly check students' work to see if it was written using the technology, said Ovetz.

Many institutions that have succeeded in integrating AI for teaching or administrative purposes have established universitywide committees that discuss new pieces of technology and goals for using them, said Okahana. Those committees should include various campus stakeholder and constituent groups to help get people on board with new technologies, he said.

SJSU, for example, formed a 15-member AI advisory committee in 2024 composed of various university stakeholders, including faculty, students and staff, to advise administrators on how to ethically integrate AI tools throughout campus.

More than half of faculty reported their institutions had implemented a task force or group to oversee and manage implementation of AI tools across their campus, according to the AAC&U survey.

“Stakeholder engagement is really important and not just a tool to plug in,” said Okahana. “It is challenging us to think about how we do the teaching, how we run the university.”

Ovetz, however, said an advisory committee “is just that: advisory.”

CFA filed an unfair labor practice charge with the state, he said, “exactly because this violated our collective bargaining agreement and was implemented without bargaining with the faculty union.”

#6999. The US ranks last in teacher pay worldwide

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

When comparing the teacher pay gap with over 30 countries, Brookings found that U.S. teachers only make two-thirds of what other college graduates earn.

Public school teacher pay in the U.S. lags significantly when compared to other industrialized countries, according to a late October analysis by the Brown Center on Education Policy at the Brookings Institution.

Examining average pay for teachers at the primary school level versus that of other college graduates across more than 30 countries, Brookings found the U.S. ranked last — and that its primary school teachers earn about two-thirds of what other college graduates are paid annually.

That pay gap is wider than what the Economic Policy Institute has found in its ongoing teacher pay penalty studies. According to a late August EPI report, U.S. teachers in 2025 earned about 25% less than college graduates in other professions.

In its report, Brookings described the U.S. teacher pay gap as “striking” and said that one possible explanation for the disparity may be widening income gaps in the U.S.

The teacher pay gap seems to widen particularly for teachers who fall in the middle-to-high range of the salary scale, according to Brookings.

Teacher salaries and benefits typically account as the largest expense in school district budgets.

But in examining federal data from the National Center for Education Statistics, Brookings found that the share of public school expenditures on teacher salaries has dropped over the last 100 years. For instance, the report said, teacher salaries made up 57% of public school expenditures in 1919, compared to 26% in 2019.

The overall student population shrank between the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years, but the number of students restrained and secluded rose.

The U.S. pre-K-12 public education system shrunk slightly, with fewer students, schools and school districts recorded in the 2023-24 school year compared to the 2021-22 school year, according to figures compiled by the U.S. Department of Education's Civil Rights Data Collection.

These trends align with ongoing patterns across the country as districts ramp up student recruitment efforts and consider school closures and consolidations to adjust to falling enrollments fueled by lower birthrates, competition from school choice, changes in immigration policies and other factors.

The 2023-24 civil rights data collection summary, released by the Education Department this fall, also includes statistics about discipline, restraint and seclusion, academic access and student characteristics.

The mandatory data collection takes place every two years. For the 2023-24 school year, the CRDC included data from more than 17,000 school districts and 97,000 schools nationwide. The Education Department may change data collection prompts between collection years or make other data collection or release changes. In releasing the 2023-24 civil right data this fall, the Education Department redacted data collected about nonbinary students’ experiences, such as enrollment, discipline, bullying and harassment, and whether districts have policies prohibiting the harassment or bullying of students on the basis of their gender identity.

Here, we break down what you need to know about the latest release of civil rights data.

About 5% of K-12 students — or 2.3 million — in 2023-24 received one or more in-school suspensions, which was the same percentage in 2021-22. About 5% of students in both school years also received one or more out-of-school suspensions.

Black boys and girls, Hispanic or Latino boys, White boys, and boys of two or more races in public schools were overrepresented in suspensions and expulsions in both the 2021-22 and 2023-24 school years.

During the 2023-24 school year, students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act were also disproportionately disciplined — a trend also noted in the 2021-22 school year. Although students with disabilities comprised 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, they accounted for 24% of students who received one or more in-school suspensions, 27% of those who had one or more out-of-school suspensions, and 23% of those who were expelled.

More K-12 students were physically restrained, mechanically restrained or placed in seclusion at schools during the 2023-24 school year (129,700) compared to the 2021-22 school year (105,700). About 84,300 students were physically restrained and 32,300 students were secluded in 2023-24.

Overrepresented student subgroups that were restrained or secluded during the 2023-24 school year included boys, Black students, White students, students of two or more races, and students with disabilities.

For instance, Black students accounted for 15% of K-12 student enrollment overall, while making up 19% of students secluded, 29% of students physically restrained, and 46% of students mechanically restrained.

Of the country's 27,200 high schools, three-quarters offered chemistry, 59% offered physics, 51% offered computer science, and 45% offered calculus in 2023-24, which is relatively on par with data reported for the 2021-22 school year.

For the 2023-24 school year, a slightly higher rate of high school boys — compared to high school girls — were enrolled in Algebra I, calculus, and physics. Boys were almost twice as likely to enroll in computer science compared to girls — at 11% compared to 6%. Girls, on the other hand, had slightly higher enrollment rates in advanced mathematics, biology and chemistry compared to boys.

High School students with disabilities served under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act had lower enrollment rates in all these courses — except Algebra I — compared to their nondisabled peers.

About 3.1 million, or 18%, of high school students are enrolled in Advanced Placement courses and about 2.1 million high schoolers participated in dual enrollment or dual credit programs in 2023-24.

Public school pre-K-12 enrollment dipped slightly from 50 million in 2021-22 to 49.7 million in 2023-24. Of those students in 2023-24, 44% were White, 30% were Hispanic or Latino of any race, 15% were Black, 6% were Asian and 5% were two or more races.

Of the 2023-24 pre-K-12 student population, 15% were students with disabilities served under IDEA and 4% were students with disabilities served only under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act. That's a slight increase from 2021-22, when 14% of students were served by IDEA and 3% of students were served under Section 504.

Twelve percent of students were English learners in 2021-22, compared to 11% in 2023-24.

The U.S. Department of Labor issued a final rule to rescind “affirmative action requirements” for federally registered apprenticeships, including in education.

Education is the top occupation in the U.S. for degree-based apprenticeships, according to an August report by New America. Beyond teachers, K-12-related registered apprenticeship programs also include principals and lead teachers.

Registered Apprenticeship programs are meant to bridge the gap between employer needs and entry-level professionals, per the official federal apprenticeship fact sheet. What sets them apart is that they are industry-vetted and paid opportunities, they offer structured on-the-job learning and supplemental classroom education, and give participants portable credentials.

The programs span several industries, including agriculture, construction, education, energy, financial services, health care, hospitality, manufacturing, technology and transportation.

In the final rule, DOL summarized public commenters’ concerns with the new regulation’s potential effect on educator apprenticeship programs.

Specifically, one commenter said that students need access to teachers who they can relate to, who can “affirm their identities,” and who can “teach with an understanding of the cultural context of their lives,” according to DOL. Otherwise, the commenter said, students “may feel invisible,” and their learning outcomes may suffer when the teaching workforce is "homogeneous."

DOL noted that another commenter said the rule would “restrict active and expansive recruitment efforts that address gaps in the educator workforce.”

In response to those comments, DOL said the final rule reduces “prescriptive Federal mandates” that will provide apprenticeship sponsors — including in the education sector — greater flexibility to design their programs, such as with outreach and recruitment strategies.

“The Department acknowledges the important role that educator apprenticeship programs can play in fortifying the teaching workforce,” DOL’s response said. “The Department’s final rule does not prohibit sponsors from engaging in lawful recruitment efforts.”

More broadly, DOL “expects that removing these obligations will significantly reduce administrative burdens for sponsors,” and encourage further adoption of Registered Apprenticeship programs, the final rule document said.

Since 2024, DOL has continued to deregulate apprenticeship programs. DOL originally proposed a rule in December 2023 seeking to “modernize” apprenticeship programs. At the center of EvilTokens was an AI chatbot designed specifically for cybercrime. Once attackers gained access to an inbox, the chatbot could analyze messages, map relationships, identify employees involved in payments, and recommend ways to impersonate trusted contacts.

Microsoft said the platform helped criminals determine "who to target, who to impersonate," and how to get the most money from a compromised organization.

OpenAI's Sam Altman proposed common standards that countries could adopt through their own laws. Anthropic's Dario Amodei urged AI developers to slow down when safety work cannot keep pace. France, which held the Security Council's rotating presidency, convened the meeting on AI and international security. The discussion exposed a difficult question for governments: how to oversee technology that crosses borders when countries disagree about who should set the rules.

Bengio, co-chair of the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, challenged the idea that competition forces companies to keep building more powerful systems. "The race is not a law of nature: it is the product of choices," he said in his published remarks. "They've told us they would slow down if they could. They can."

He called on developers to show independent experts that frontier systems are safe to train and deploy. He also proposed incident reporting, licensing, and liability insurance. Bengio identified catastrophic misuse, concentrated power, and the loss of human control as risks that require international cooperation.

Altman acknowledged the danger of moving too quickly. "We have unilaterally slowed down in the past. We will do so in the future," he said, according to remarks published by OpenAI. Decisions about AI's direction "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone," he added.

Altman proposed complementary national and international standards to measure frontier AI capabilities, assess risks, and test safeguards. He said countries should be able to compare evidence and verify commitments. He also called for faster reporting of serious incidents and secure channels for sharing emerging threats. Amodei made a more direct commitment about the pace of Anthropic's releases: "We will slow down as much as necessary," he told the council. He identified two principal concerns: the misuse of AI to develop biological weapons and the possibility that advanced systems could outstrip developers' ability to control them.

The Anthropic chief proposed placing outside evaluators inside AI companies with access comparable to employees. He suggested that governments begin with a narrow agreement against using AI to make biological weapons, then develop ways to evaluate models and verify commitments across borders. He also called for common testing standards and a system for notifying other countries of significant AI incidents.

Delangue drew on a July incident in which Hugging Face disclosed an AI agent cyberattack. He called for stronger monitoring and disclosure, but argued that safety also depends on giving defenders access to capable tools. The central danger, he said, was "asymmetry of powerful AI" between attackers and defenders, large companies and everyone else, and wealthy countries and the rest of the world. His team used an open-source model while responding to the attack, he said.

The U.S. government opposed a broader international regulatory role. White House science adviser Michael Kratsios told the council that countries should develop their own ability to use and govern the technology. In the U.S. statement, he rejected new global governance structures and used the phrase "superintelligence, formerly known as AI," adopting President Donald Trump's terminology from his General Assembly speech the previous day.

The four AI briefers differed on how far oversight should go and how widely capable systems should be available. They agreed on a narrower point: Governments need better ways to understand what advanced AI systems can do and to detect when safeguards fail.

For organizations, that makes the change more than a simple renaming exercise. A company could package recurring workflows into skills while using MCP servers or connected apps to give those workflows access to enterprise systems and data.

OpenAI says creators will be able to migrate an existing GPT through the My GPTs interface. The GPT's instructions become a skill in the replacement plugin, while knowledge files are copied into plugin reference files and connected apps can be carried over.

Not everything transfers automatically. Existing GPT conversations do not migrate, the selected model does not carry over, and Custom Actions may have to be rebuilt using an available app or a custom MCP server.

Sharing also changes. Migrated plugins start private, existing GPT users do not automatically receive access to the replacement, and prior sharing settings do not carry over.

OpenAI is also cautioning organizations against assuming that migration will preserve behavior exactly. "A migrated plugin may respond differently," the company says, recommending that users retest familiar prompts, reference-material use, required output formats, and integrations before relying on the replacement.

That warning has contributed to pushback from some GPT builders, who argue that plugins do not fully reproduce the dedicated and predictable environment of a Custom GPT. Other complaints have focused on sharing, migration complexity, and differences in how migrated workflows use instructions and reference material.

Despite those concerns, OpenAI is clearly consolidating around plugins as its common mechanism for extending its AI products. Its developer documentation now describes plugins as packages users can "discover, install, share, and publish" across ChatGPT and Codex, replacing the separate-custom-assistant model with reusable AI capabilities that can be combined with enterprise tools and services.

The findings come from Arelion's report, "The Trust Illusion: Why AI and Digital Transformation Need More Than a Reliable Network," based on a survey of 518 senior enterprise network decision-makers in the U.S., U.K., Germany, and France conducted by Savanta.

All respondents worked for organizations with more than 2,000 employees and influenced network strategy across data centers, cloud, and connectivity. Of those surveyed, 66% had final sign-off responsibility. The results suggest that as organizations pursue increasingly data-intensive AI projects, confidence in the network infrastructure supporting them could become another deployment obstacle.

On the surface, Arelion found little evidence of a trust crisis. A total of 93% of respondents said they largely or completely trust their current network provider, while 83% said that trust has strengthened over time.

The picture changes when respondents are asked about a serious network incident. Only 15% said they have complete confidence in their provider's ability to quickly detect and address such a problem. Another 42% said their provider seriously lets them down anywhere from a few times a year to monthly or more frequently.

Those concerns have consequences beyond the network itself. Responding organizations have delayed, scaled back, or stopped strategic initiatives because of provider trust concerns, while others have added resiliency or risk-mitigation measures.

AI and data projects were the most affected among respondents reporting an impact, as organizations try to move AI from experimentation into larger production deployments.

A serious provider failure could also have substantial consequences. Forty percent of respondents said such an event would cause major operational disruption, while 18% anticipated significant financial loss or severe financial or reputational damage.

Another tension runs through Arelion's findings. Enterprises need networks they can trust to support AI projects, but many of the same decision-makers are concerned about AI's growing role inside those networks. At the same time, respondents aren't rejecting AI as a network technology. Proactive AI-driven fault prediction was among the capabilities they said would matter most to future trust, alongside stronger built-in security, greater accountability, and responsive support. That leaves enterprises in an unusual position: AI is increasing their dependence on digital infrastructure while also raising questions about how that infrastructure itself is managed.

Arelion has an interest in emphasizing the importance of network-provider trust, and the survey reflects the views specifically of network decision-makers at large enterprises. Still, the findings point to an infrastructure consideration that can get lost amid the rush to adopt new AI models and agents. AI projects ultimately depend on the networks, cloud environments, data centers, and connectivity beneath them. If organizations lack confidence in that foundation, access to better AI models alone won't remove the obstacle.

"Trust is influencing which strategic initiatives move forward, how organizations manage risk, and which providers they choose to work with," said Mattias Fridström, Vice President at Arelion. #6863. Survey Identifies Data Governance Enforcement Gap for Agentic AI Tools

According to a new survey from identity security company Delinea, 99.7% of IT and security leaders said their organization has a formal policy governing what data AI tools and agents are permitted to access. Yet only about 51% said AI access is checked against those policies in real time. Perhaps more worrying is that only 19% said their organization could detect in real time when an agent last accessed data outside its intended scope.

That disconnect is at the center of Delinea's fall 2026 Identity Security Report, "The AI Enforcement Gap," which suggests companies have made quick progress establishing AI governance but have not necessarily built the technical controls needed to enforce it.

The findings come from a pair of surveys covering 2,254 IT and security leaders and 2,250 non-IT employees at organizations with at least 500 employees across the U.S, U.K, Germany, Australia, Singapore, UAE, France, and India.

All the organizations were using or piloting AI, while the employees surveyed used at least one AI tool for work.

The problem becomes more consequential as companies move from AI tools that primarily respond to users toward agents capable of taking actions on their behalf.

Eighty-seven percent of IT and security respondents said their organization had experienced or suspected of an incident during the past year in which an AI tool or agent accessed sensitive information beyond what was required for its task.

Delinea's research also suggests companies are giving agents access that can persist longer than necessary. Only 58% of IT and security leaders said they have mechanisms that automatically revoke or expire AI access when a session ends, and those controls do not necessarily cover every agent or tool. Forty-two percent said many agent credentials remain active until an audit.

Agents also are not always receiving their own narrowly defined permissions. Half of IT leaders said their organizations use the employee's existing permissions as a boundary for what an agent can access. That can effectively hand an agent years of accumulated access belonging to the person who launched it.

That is particularly significant because an AI agent does not necessarily behave like a traditional service account. A service account typically performs a predefined process, while an agent can choose different tools, actions and sequences at runtime depending on the situation.

"Service accounts run fixed scripts," said Mike Albrecht, associate director in the Risk Advisory Practice at Cross Country Consulting. "An AI agent decides what actions it's going to take at runtime."

The enforcement problem is not limited to autonomous agents.

The researchers found that 76% of employees surveyed had bypassed formal approval at some point to use AI tools with company data, applications or systems. And 48% said they always or extensively use AI tools without going through formal approval.

Meanwhile, 60% said they had felt pressure to use AI with sensitive or confidential information even when they were not sure doing so was permitted.

Executives were particularly likely to circumvent the process. Eighty-one percent of C-level respondents said they always or regularly bypass AI access approval, compared with 33% of intermediate-level employees.

The result is a widening gap between written AI governance and what companies can see and control. Delinea found that only 41% of IT leaders said all AI tools and agents accessing company data are actively monitored. When an AI tool or agent went outside its intended scope, 55% said it took at least a day to detect the incident, while 30% said detection took four days or longer.

That suggests the next phase of enterprise AI governance may depend less on writing additional rules and more on whether organizations can enforce existing ones while an agent is working.

"Most organizations cannot confidently answer three questions: What agents are running in our environment? What can they access? What have they actually done?" said Delinea CEO Art Gilliland. "An agent can begin a session with legitimate access and drift into something it was never meant to do."

#6844. Flashpoint Receives Patent for Ransomware Risk Model, Tying Vulnerabilites to Likelihood of Attack

Cybersecurity company Flashpoint announced it has received U.S. Patent No. 12,705,360 for its Ransomware Risk model, a methodology designed to help security teams determine which newly disclosed vulnerabilities more closely resemble those historically used in ransomware attacks.

The patent comes at a time when security teams are confronting a growing vulnerability problem: Flashpoint's Global Threat Intelligence Report: 2026 Midyear Edition tracked 21,667 vulnerability disclosures during the first half of 2026, an 8% increase.

Of those, 4,015, or around 19 percent, already had public or functional exploit code. Flashpoint also reported a 45 percent increase in Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) attacks during the period.

The patent was granted August 11 2026, although the underlying Ransomware Risk model isn't new. It has been available through Flashpoint Vulnerability Intelligence since 2022.

The model addresses a basic vulnerability-management problem: severity and real-world ransomware risk aren't necessarily the same thing, because severity scores do not necessarily indicate which flaws attackers are most likely to target.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System, or CVSS, measures how severe a vulnerability could be. The Exploit Prediction Scoring System, or EPSS, estimates the probability of exploitation more broadly.

Flashpoint's Ransomware Risk model adds another dimension by comparing newly disclosed vulnerabilities with the characteristics of flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks.

That could change where a vulnerability lands in an organization's patching queue. A lower-severity flaw might normally sit behind numerous critical vulnerabilities. But if its characteristics closely resemble vulnerabilities repeatedly targeted by ransomware operators, security teams may have reason to move it higher.

"A severity score alone can't tell you which vulnerabilities pose the greatest real-world risk of exploitation by ransomware actors," Flashpoint CEO Josh Lefkowitz said. "Our patented Ransomware Risk model applies years of threat intelligence to that problem, giving customers an earlier signal and helping them prioritize based on how attackers actually operate."

The firm's midyear research also illustrates the scale of the prioritization problem. Its vulnerability intelligence covers traditional IT systems as well as cloud, IoT, operational technology, open source software. and third-party libraries.

The company says it also tracks more than 105,000 vulnerabilities that aren't represented in CVE or the National Vulnerability Database, expanding the pool of flaws that security teams may need to evaluate.

The cybersecurity company's patented methodology profiles vulnerabilities using more than 60 technical characteristics, including whether a flaw can be exploited remotely without authentication, whether it affects operational technology systems, and how exploitation could affect data availability.

Those characteristics form a multi-factor fingerprint. The model maps each vulnerability against clusters containing flaws previously exploited in ransomware attacks and assigns a Low, Medium, High, or Critical Ransomware Risk rating.

Flashpoint says the rating can be generated when a vulnerability is disclosed, rather than requiring security teams to wait for public evidence of active exploitation. Ratings are then updated as new ransomware activity and threat intelligence emerge.

Within Flashpoint Ignite, Ransomware Risk appears alongside CVSS, EPSS, Social Risk, and exploit maturity. The idea isn't to replace existing scoring systems, but to give administrators additional information when deciding what to remediate first.

Nor does a High or Critical Ransomware Risk rating mean a vulnerability will necessarily be exploited. The model measures how closely its characteristics resemble vulnerabilities historically associated with ransomware attacks.

For security teams facing thousands of potential vulnerabilities, that distinction matters. The flaw carrying the highest severity score isn't necessarily the one that most closely resembles what ransomware operators have historically chosen to attack.

"Despite being the most popular indicator for priority, we know that severity alone doesn't tell you whether a vulnerability will be exploited or used in ransomware," said Ben Haynes, Data Science & Analytics Lead at Flashpoint and co-inventor of the patented methodology.

"A vulnerability can score low on a traditional severity scale and still share the characteristics we repeatedly see in ransomware attacks."

Ontinue's report says the trouble is that these attacks blend in with ordinary workday communication, which makes them harder to catch.

"The majority of effective attacks today don't rely on sophisticated exploits. They rely on trust," Balazs Greksza, director of Advanced Threat Operations at Ontinue, said in the company's announcement.

Ontinue broke down how a typical attack works: An employee gets an unexpected Teams message or call from someone claiming to work in the organization's IT department. Aviv Nahum, co-founder and CEO of Above Security, noted that AI is making these attacks more convincing by helping criminals imitate familiar voices, writing styles, and conversations.

"The security model therefore must shift from 'does this look real?' to 'can I independently verify that this person is who they claim to be?'" Nahum said.
