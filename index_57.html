#46. Denmark: Top court backs hardline immigration "ghetto law"

Denmark's Supreme Court has found that evictions of "non-Western" residents do not amount to discrimination. The government in Copenhagen argues its "ghetto law" encourages integration.

Denmark's Supreme Court on Monday ruled in favor of a housing association that terminated 91 leases in order to prevent the label "hard ghetto" being applied to their housing complex. The decision align's with Copenhagen's hardline approach to immigration.

The court on Monday upheld Denmark's 2018 "ghetto law," which allows for the relocation of "non-Western" residents from minority-heavy neighborhoods in order to foster integration. The Danish court ruling diverges from a top EU court decision last year that found application of the law could amount to discrimination.

The Danish laws requires social housing estates to rebuild, renovate and adjust their social mix if these become minority heavy, stipulating that 60% of apartments be rented to "Westerners" at market value by 2030.

Denmark's pioneering role in spearheading tougher immigration rules has made it a model for some in the EU. Denmark was also one of five countries that last week announced its intent to set up an asylum seeker deportation hub in Rwanda.

Danish Housing Minister Jacob Mark welcomed Monday's decision, saying it "strengthens security, social cohesion and integration."

The court's decision upheld a 2022 lower court ruling against seven tenants who fought their evictions in Nojsomhed, Helsingor, just north of Copenhagen.

In Monday's ruling, the law was found to be non-discriminatory when it comes to the eviction of "immigrants and descendants from non-Western countries" as it is not broadly based on ethnicity. The EU ruling focused on this particular aspect, saying the law would be discriminatory if criteria were race based, while ultimately allowing national courts to have the last say in the matter.

"The Supreme Court notes that applying the criterion of the law involves dividing people into two groups [immigrants and descendants from non-Western countries, and all other persons]. #20. What failure of COP31 reveals about a divided world

Some have hailed the lack of consensus as a sign of a positive shift, despite the Turkey summit failure

Some may have found it ironic that the world's biggest conference on climate change should collapse over the very mention of fossil fuels - the main cause of global warming.

Yet that is what happened in Antalya, Turkey, on Sunday.

The failure tells us something about the state of climate diplomacy. But it may tell us even more about the increasingly divided world in which the negotiations take place.

Chris Bowen, the Australian minister who led the negotiations, spoke of a backdrop constituting "the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory". The competing demands, he said, had become "unbridgeable red lines".

Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister and the summit's president, was blunter. Governments should not disappoint people desperate for climate action, he suggested, by getting "hung up on one word or phrase".

And Kurum had a point. Three years ago, at COP28 in Dubai, there was agreement to transition away from fossil fuels. The proposed Antalya Pact failed to mention them at all.

Was it really worth sacrificing the agreement over two words, then?

The countries that rejected it said the distinction was fundamental. They argued that there was a world of difference between promising to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and committing to move away from the fuels responsible for most of them. Without that explicit commitment, they feared the new agreement would weaken the one made in Dubai.

Co-leader of the negotiations Chris Bowen blamed the failure to reach an agreement on "the most complicated and fraught geopolitical environment in living memory"

Normally, it is difficult to establish exactly who has said what at these conferences. Much of the bargaining takes place behind closed doors, and governments are often reluctant to admit which proposals they have opposed.

This time, some of the opposition was public - mostly from oil-producing nations.

Mohammad Ayoub, a Saudi negotiator speaking on behalf of the 22 Arab League countries, argued that Arab nations had contributed less than 2% of historical greenhouse gas emissions. It was wealthy industrialised nations, he said, that bore the overwhelming responsibility for the problem.

He defended the Arab countries' right to choose how to tackle climate change according to their national circumstances.

Russia's delegate Sergei Kononuchenko went further. Cutting oil and gas supplies before demand fell, he warned, would cause shortages, higher prices and energy poverty.

"For that reason, we cannot agree to any formulation on fossil fuel," he said.

The wider political backdrop was still more revealing.

The US, which in previous rounds of talks had helped broker compromises - sometimes working alongside China - had no official delegation in Antalya. President Donald Trump has rejected the entire international climate process.

And while delegates argued over fossil fuels in Turkey, Trump, China's President Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin issued a joint declaration in Shenzhen that included a pledge to increase production and supply of refined petroleum products.

The priorities of some of the world's most powerful governments appeared very different from those being discussed in the negotiating rooms.

The EU's representative, Irish Minister Darragh O'Brien, said a deal that did not mention fossil fuels was "unconscionable"

Yet, perhaps, the picture is more optimistic than it first appears. About 130 governments backed a demand for explicit language on moving away from fossil fuels - roughly two-thirds of the countries represented in Antalya.

Christiana Figueres, who led the UN negotiations that delivered the Paris Agreement, argued that countries were right to walk away rather than endorse a weaker political commitment that might undermine the progress already made.

Looked at this way, the collapse is not evidence that the world has lost its appetite for climate action. Quite the opposite - a substantial majority of governments were demanding more ambitious action.

So why not simply find a way for them to move ahead without waiting for the countries determined to block them?

The difficulty becomes clearer when you look beyond the dispute over fossil fuels.

Kononuchenko had another target in his sights: climate finance. Much of the money wealthy countries provide to poorer nations, he complained, comes as loans rather than grants. He said that developing countries ended up repaying more than they received.

"This is an old colonial distribution of labour under a new banner," he declared.

Then, in a striking moment, he leaned towards the microphone and switched from Russian into English to address his criticism directly to the Europeans: "First of all distinguished representatives of the EU."

Russia has its own reasons for attacking Europe, but the complaint about finance is one that many developing countries share. They argue that they are being asked to pay for damage they did little to cause while receiving too little help to adapt to climate change or build cleaner economies.

The EU had ruled out significant new funding before the negotiations began. Mohamed Adow, the Kenyan campaigner who heads Power Shift Africa, accused Europe of rejecting a flawed deal while refusing to acknowledge its own responsibility for the failure on finance.

The UN's climate chief, Simon Stiell, said he would seek advice on potential reform to the COP process

So is it possible for a majority of countries to outvote those blocking progress?

The UN climate chief, Simon Stiell, has acknowledged the need to "improve, modernise and reform" the process. Some governments have proposed introducing voting when consensus proves impossible. Others want countries prepared to move faster to make their own agreements outside the formal talks.

But there is a reason the UN climate process depends on consensus. International climate co-operation is largely voluntary.

Voting might produce more ambitious agreements on paper, but would it persuade the governments that had been outvoted to act? Consensus also offers some protection to smaller and poorer nations that might otherwise find themselves overruled by the most powerful countries.

Meanwhile, the failure to agree a summit declaration does not mean climate action stops. Solar panels, batteries and electric vehicles are becoming increasingly competitive, while the upheaval in global oil and gas supplies is giving countries another reason to generate their own electricity rather than depend on imported fossil fuels.

The energy transition is moving forward, whatever happens in the UN negotiating rooms. The problem is that it is not moving nearly fast enough for the climate.

Perhaps the most poignant aspect of the failed Antalya Pact is what the countries had apparently been prepared to accept.

#4. US to blacklist Asian AI firm Megaspeed after Nvidia scandal

The US has said it intends to ban a Singaporean artificial intelligence (AI) company allegedly tied to the Nvidia scandal from doing business in America.

The Department of Commerce announced it planned to add Megaspeed Ltd to a list of entities that are blacklisted from buying or selling goods in the US.

The company is mainly a developer of data centres, but in recent weeks a former executive of Megaspeed was accused of working as spy for China while having a romantic relationship with Nvidia head Jensen Huang.

The relationship led to additional scrutiny by US officials and heightened concern that Megaspeed was funnelling Nvidia's advanced AI computer chips to China.

When exactly the ban against Megaspeed will go into effect was not specified in the commerce department announcement. The BBC has asked the agency for comment, along with the White House and Megaspeed.

Megaspeed was not well known within the US until last month when media reports emerged that a former Megaspeed executive, Alice Huang, had an affair with Jensen Huang.

Alice Huang (no relation) was thought by US intelligence to be working as an asset for Beijing, creating national security concerns within the US.

China has denied that she is a spy and warned the US not to use the scandal as a "pretext for imposing a technological blockade" on AI technology.

On 12 November, Jensen Huang resigned after allegations he had had another affair, this one with a Nvidia employee.

On Monday, Nvidia's board reinstated him after US President Donald Trump said he had "convinced" the company and "made the deal".

The commerce department has said it had been investigating Megaspeed for almost two years.

The department said it had suspected the company was violating US export controls by helping to get Nvidia's chips into the hands of companies within China.

China has been blocked from buying such technology since 2022.

Although based in Singapore, Megaspeed has significant business dealings in China.

Alibaba, for instance, the major Chinese online retailer, is its largest customer.

Megaspeed was a known buyer of billions of dollars of Nvidia computer chips, including through its subsidiary Speedmatrix, which has also been added to the Commerce blacklist.

Nvidia's computer chips, originally designed for use in gaming consols, in recent years have become crucial to the continued growth of the AI industry.

Without enough supply or access to computer chips that are capable of processing the huge amount of data needed to make things like AI chatbots and models run, AI companies will not be able to meet or grow demand from people using AI tools.

That has driven up the value of Nvidia on the stock market, as well as its influence in the AI industry and the White House.

Although tech stocks have in recent months been volatile and trending lower, when Jensen Huang resigned as CEO of Nvidia because of his affairs, the company's stock fell by more than 30%.

Anxiety about the company's influence and ability to operate within the AI ecosystem without its famed executive appeared to take down shares of other tech companies, too.

Huang was reinstated on Monday to his position as head of Nvidia, a move that US President Donald Trump said he told the company's board to take.

The company's stock rallied on his reinstatement, as did several other tech stocks, but shares of Nvidia did not climb to where the company had been before being immersed in scandal.

In the weeks ahead, Huang and the company he now runs again are facing multiple government agency investigations.

Huang and the chief legal officer of Nvidia are set to appear next for a closed-door briefing with the House Foreign Affairs Committee.
