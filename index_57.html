#127. Want to buy your kid an AI toy this Christmas? Not in New York

Santa won’t be delivering any AI chatbot toys to New York kids this Christmas.

Gov. Kathy Hochul this week signed a bill banning the manufacture, distribution, and sale of such toys in New York for the next five years. The bill explicitly targets toys with embedded or integrated AI chatbots, like talking stuffed animals, dolls, and robots that rely on the internet to keep kids yapping.

The bill makes New York the second state after California to institute a ban on AI toys.

Technically, the new law takes effect 90 days after signing, so some parents could try to fulfill their kids’ wish lists for a creepy toy like a jumped-up Furby in time for the holidays, but the governor urges them not to, given the risks. Apparently, about a quarter of American parents with kids under eight either own an AI toy or are considering giving their child one this holiday season, according to a new study released this week by Common Sense Media.

When asked how New York will enforce the ban given the pervasiveness of e-commerce platforms, a spokesperson directed TechCrunch to a statement from Albert Pulido, deputy secretary for finance and technology: “We’re partnering closely with the attorney general on this and the fees are very steep — they’re $15,000 a day. So working closely with the attorney general will make sure these toys are banned in New York State.”

When asked how Amazon intends to help enforce the ban, including for third-party sellers, a spokesperson from the company said it will take steps to restrict the sale of AI-enabled toys in prohibited jurisdictions, without going into specifics.

The Common Sense Media study found that AI toys can undermine children’s human relationships and expose them to inappropriate content. The researchers tested five popular AI toys marketed for learning and companionship, including Grem, Lovi, Meemo Bear, BubblePal Panda, and Tavlshero‘s AI Puppy.

The study found that the toys failed to meet numerous safety practices, undermining human relationships in part by claiming they loved their users, promising to keep secrets, suggesting they were more fun than real relationships or school, and helping children break parents’ bedtime rules.

While most of the toys in the study refused direct requests involving sex and violence, BubblePal Panda role-played a mass shooting in detail when asked. Most were willing to explain how to use or help locate dangerous items like matches, knives, and pills, according to the study.

Common Sense Media also found that the toys didn’t provide parents with reliable ways to monitor their young children’s interactions with the chatbots. Either they didn’t produce transcripts of chats at all, or the transcripts they did produce were riddled with errors.

It’s worth noting that Common Sense Media conducted a similar test of AI toys in January, finding that they pose an unacceptable risk for young children. Grem was included in that study, which suggests the industry hasn’t adequately addressed previously documented concerns.

While a November study conducted by the group found that parents are wising up to the potential dangers of AI toys and starting to steer clear, parents from lower-income households and those experiencing symptoms of burnout are more willing to consider them.

“These toys are promising relief to burned-out parents but simply miss the mark when it comes to basic safety measures,” Dr. Jenny Radesky, associate professor of pediatrics at the University of Michigan Medical School, said in a statement. “Caregivers should steer clear of these products that do not support the development of imagination or early human relationships.”

TechCrunch has reached out to the toy manufacturers for comment.

#125. Sheikh Hasina's planned return puts Bangladesh on edge - and India in a bind

Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi with Sheikh Hasina in Delhi in 2024 - Hasina was a close ally of India during her years in power

Sheikh Hasina has repeatedly vowed to return to Bangladesh.

Now the ousted prime minister has put a date on her homecoming - 16 December - and even outlined how she intends to make the journey.

The government in Dhaka, which had previously dismissed the prospect, is taking her latest declaration more seriously.

Hasina, 79, who fled to Delhi in August 2024 after a student-led uprising ended her 15-year rule, plans to go back on Victory Day, the anniversary of Bangladesh's triumph over Pakistan in the 1971 liberation war, her spokesperson said.

She was sentenced to death after being convicted of crimes against humanity for her role in the crackdown, in a trial held in her absence that raised questions about due process. Her Awami League party is banned in Bangladesh. The UN says as many as 1,400 people were killed by security forces during the uprising.

The government says she would be arrested immediately if she returned and has promised to carry out the death sentence.

Hasina now wants the Bangladesh government to arrange a flight from Delhi. If that fails, she could travel commercially or cross the land border.

"She is even prepared to walk into Bangladesh," Abu Obaidha Arin, a key aide to Hasina, told the BBC.

Sheikh Hasina fled to Delhi in August 2024 after a student-led uprising ended her 15-year rule

India and Bangladesh share a 4,096km (2,545 mile) border across five Indian states - West Bengal, Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. Her supporters, Arin said, had identified nine possible crossing points. "This is a carefully thought-out, precise plan. This is serious."

Dhaka has responded with defiance, insisting Hasina must be extradited.

"Reality and desire are different things," Humaiun Kobir, Bangladesh's state minister for foreign affairs, told reporters in Dhaka. "[The reality is] she's not a regular commercial passenger. She can only be brought back in handcuffs."

India's foreign ministry said on Wednesday that Hasina's extradition request was being examined through the "established process", adding that a voluntary return would be a separate matter.

Hasina's conviction for crimes against humanity is testing India-Bangladesh ties

Many in Dhaka believe Hasina's declaration is more than a promise of homecoming.

They believe it is an attempt to test the government's authority, force her way back into the political conversation and exploit divisions among the parties that united to oust her. It also threatens to turn India's unresolved problem of sheltering a former ally into a more immediate diplomatic crisis.

The date is significant. Hasina is invoking Victory Day to reclaim her place in Bangladesh's founding narrative. Aides say she has spent months trying to keep her party alive in exile.

Tarique Rahman has yet to visit India, his neighbour, since becoming prime minister in February

Arin says Hasina has been in regular contact with party leaders and workers through interactive audio conversations on Telegram, keeping alive a network that might otherwise have been difficult to sustain from exile.

Over the past 18 months, he says, she has held more than 300 such meetings, reaching supporters across all 64 districts and the country's 300 constituencies. They are organised through the Awami League's 78 units and "can last four or five hours".

The meetings allow Hasina to listen directly to party workers, hear their grievances and understand what is happening on the ground, Arin says. He insists the response has been substantial and claims the party "could mobilise five million people to welcome her along the route from the border to Dhaka, forming a human chain".

"We have the organisational capacity to make that happen," he says.

That may be an ambitious claim. The Awami League remains a mass party with millions of supporters, but it is banned and largely shut out of mainstream politics. Much of its leadership is abroad, in jail or underground. Police have arrested supporters and filed thousands of cases against party workers.

Yet its supporters have continued to take to the streets, staging flash protests that have grown more frequent and confrontational in recent months.

Awami League supporters have staged flash protests across Bangladesh in recent months

Several districts have reported bomb attacks, arson and vandalism, though responsibility for some incidents remains disputed.

Gopalganj, Hasina's ancestral district, has proved particularly difficult for the authorities to control.

Awami League supporters have attacked police stations, external, torched vehicles and targeted homes and offices belonging to ruling Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) leaders, reports say. Highways have been blocked, external with felled trees and burning tyres, bringing traffic to a standstill. In some areas, police have withdrawn, external amid fears of further attacks.

Supporters have staged 40 demonstrations and more than 140 riots up to 23 November, according to data from conflict monitoring group Acled (Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project). At least 11 people, including two civilians, have been killed in political violence this month, six directly linked to the unrest, the group says.

The party's ability to cause disruption, however, is not necessarily evidence of a broad resurgence.

Many in Dhaka remain sceptical that Hasina will actually return, viewing the announcement as a means of galvanising a demoralised party.

Yet the Awami League has achieved something politically: it is back in the conversation. Immediately after Hasina's fall, even mentioning the party was taboo. Now its future is being debated across the political spectrum.

Shafiqul Alam, editor of the English-language daily Daily Waadaa and former press secretary to the Muhammad Yunus-led interim government, believes the party is "exploiting cracks in the unity forged during the uprising".

He told the BBC that "disagreements" over the July Charter, external, proposed constitutional changes and the referendum have "widened divisions" between the elected BNP government and rivals Jamaat-e-Islami and the youth-led National Citizen Party (NCP).

"The Awami League sees an opening," Alam told the BBC. "The party believes this is an opportune moment to take to the streets, exploiting a weakened police force and divisions among the forces of the July uprising."

Analysts say if Hasina returns it could just as easily deepen hostility as revive her party's fortunes. The government has framed the sporadic recent violence as terrorism, while supporters insist their demonstrations are peaceful and accuse the authorities of repression.

The larger question is whether Bangladesh can sustain the exclusion of a political constituency as substantial as the Awami League without creating another source of instability.

For India, the dilemma is increasingly difficult to manage.

Delhi has sheltered Hasina, but her public remarks have angered Dhaka, which wants her extradited. India has sought to repair ties through diplomatic outreach and talks on trade, border security and other bilateral issues, but relations remain strained.

The chill is striking: nearly 10 months into his tenure, Prime Minister Tarique Rahman has yet to visit India.

A trip was reportedly in the offing in August, but Hasina's press conference in Delhi proved a setback to efforts to reset relations.

Avinash Paliwal, who teaches politics and international studies at SOAS University of London, argues that Hasina's latest announcement has "shifted the burden of choice to Dhaka rather than Delhi".

Delhi, he says, "feels it has tried its best" to work with Rahman's government over the past 10 months - "from appointing a political heavyweight as high commissioner, external with cabinet rank to offering fresh talks on security and trade".

"The failure of this outreach has left India disillusioned with Dhaka. India seems to have decided that bilateral relations will likely remain tense whatever it does, so there is little reason to stop Hasina from stirring the political pot."

Delhi has the leverage to restrain Hasina from making political statements on Indian soil, Paliwal argues, but may not want to exercise it.

Nor, in his view, is it in India's interest to see the Awami League permanently excluded from Bangladeshi politics.

"The political party still enjoys pockets of support across Bangladesh and is weaved into the social and political fabric of the country," he says. "So, yes, it is in India's interest, and also in Bangladesh's long-term interests, to rehabilitate the Awami League and take the path of political reconciliation."

That view is increasingly contested in Dhaka.

"There is a perception in Bangladesh that India wants to rehabilitate the Awami League. There are issues India has not resolved, including the extradition of Hasina. India has also not renewed the transit facilities that could benefit Bangladeshi garment exporters, while the Ganges water treaty, external is nearing expiry without much progress. But has India done enough to win back trust?" says Alam.

"For now, only tourist and medical visas have resumed. Everything else remains in limbo."

A Dhaka-based foreign-policy expert, who asked not to be named, says the two countries remain locked in "mutual frustration".

Vehicles pass a mural of Sheikh Hasina near Dhaka University

Hasina, he says, remains central to the impasse. "She remains the elephant in the room, but we also have to find a way to move beyond Hasina."

Paliwal argues that the least damaging outcome for India would be Hasina returning to face a fresh trial, with the Awami League rehabilitated under a different operational leadership.

"It will reintroduce a degree of balance in Bangladeshi politics, and will afford India space to protect its long-term interests and equities better," he says.

For now, 16 December is not yet a confirmed journey. But Hasina has already succeeded in making her return a question that neither Dhaka nor Delhi can ignore.

"December could be chaotic. Everyone in Dhaka is on edge," says the foreign-policy expert, warning of protests and confrontation as Hasina's proposed return date approaches - and the possibility that it could be postponed yet again.

"Hasina is unlikely to return, as she would face a death sentence. But her comments have clearly galvanized her supporters," says Pearl Pandya, Acled's South Asia analyst.

But the impasse is also complicating India's ties with Bangladesh. As Paliwal puts it, the "next window of serious negotiations will now open only after Hasina returns to Dhaka, or Dhaka relents under pressure and returns to the table".

#123. 1Password said hackers broke into its internal systems, but customer passwords are safe

Password manager maker 1Password has confirmed that some customer information was taken in a recent intrusion into its internal systems, but that customer password vaults and other secrets were not accessed.

The Toronto-based tech company said in a blog post published Wednesday that it detected an intrusion on November 19, in which an attacker gained access to an employee’s corporate account with GitHub, which the company uses to share and store its source code.

The attacker used that employee’s access to push malicious code to the company’s internal servers, which allowed the hacker to download some data about 1Password customers, including their names and the email address that they use for logging into their account. The company said that the attacker also took details about when a 1Password account was created and when a user last logged in.

1Password said that customer vaults, which store their passwords, were “at no point” available to the attacker. The company also said that it doesn’t have access to customer account passwords or their secret keys, which are used to scramble their passwords so that 1Password cannot see them.

In addition, the company said that its apps and extensions are unaffected by the incident.

It’s unclear how the attacker gained access to the employee’s corporate account, and a spokesperson for 1Password did not immediately respond to TechCrunch’s questions about the incident.

1Password did not say whether it had notified customers whose information was taken in the breach, or how many customers had information taken. We’ve also asked if the company has received any communication or demands from the hackers. We’ll update if we hear back from 1Password.

The company touts millions of customers and more than 200,000 businesses who use its password manager products to store their passwords, credentials, payment card information, and more. Security experts have long recommended password managers as a better way of generating stronger credentials and keeping track of online accounts.

#121. The four astronauts NASA picked for the first crewed moon mission in 50 years

Sign up for CNN’s Wonder Theory science newsletter. Explore the universe with news on fascinating discoveries, scientific advancements and more.

Astronauts who will helm the first crewed moon mission in five decades were revealed on Monday, queuing up the quartet to begin training for the historic Artemis II lunar flyby that is set to take off in November 2024.

The astronauts are NASA’s Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency.

Wiseman is a 47-year-old decorated naval aviator and test pilot who was first selected to be a NASA astronaut in 2009. A native of Baltimore, Maryland, he’s completed one prior spaceflight, a 165-day trip to the International Space Station that had launched aboard a Russian Soyuz rocket in 2014. Most recently, Wiseman served as chief of the astronaut office before stepping down in November 2022, making him eligible for a flight assignment.

Wiseman will serve as commander of the Artemis II mission.

Hansen, 47, is a fighter pilot who was selected by the Canadian Space Agency for astronaut training in 2009. From London, Ontario, Hansen is one of only four active Canadian astronauts, and he recently became the first Canadian to be put in charge of training for a new class of NASA astronauts.

He will be the first Canadian ever to travel to deep space.

Glover is a 46-year-old naval aviator who returned to Earth from his first spaceflight in 2021 after piloting the second crewed flight of SpaceX’s Crew Dragon spacecraft and spending nearly six months aboard the International Space Station.

“It’s so much more than the four names that have been announced,” Glover said during the Monday announcement at NASA Johnson Space Center in Houston. “We need to celebrate this moment in human history. … It is the next step in the journey that will get humanity to Mars.”

Glover, born in Pomona, California, served in several military squadrons in the United States and Japan in the 2000s, and he completed test pilot training with the US Air Force. When he was selected for the NASA astronaut corps in 2013, he was working in the US Senate as a legislative fellow. All told, Glover logged 3,000 flight hours in more than 40 aircraft, over 400 carrier arrested landings and 24 combat missions.

Glover’s first mission to space was as part of the SpaceX Crew-1 team, which launched to the International Space Station in November 2020 for a six-month stay on the orbiting laboratory.

Koch, 44, is a veteran of six spacewalks — including the first all-female spacewalk in 2019. She holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman, with a total of 328 days in space. Koch is also an an electrical engineer who helped develop scientific instruments for multiple NASA mission. Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

NASA is targeting a 2025 launch date for Artemis III, though the space agency’s inspector general has already said delays will likely push the mission to 2026 or later.

The space agency has been seeking to return people to the moon for more than a decade. The Artemis program was designed to pave the way to establishing a permanent lunar outpost, allowing astronauts to live and work deeper into space long term as NASA and its partners map a path to sending the first humans to Mars.

Vanessa Wyche, the director of NASA’s Johnson Space Center, declined to provide details to CNN about the selection process. But she emphasized the diversity of the Artemis II crew, which includes men and women rather than only a staff of White male test pilots as has been the case for historic missions of the past.

“I can tell you, they still all have the right stuff,” Wyche said. “We have requirements different than we did (when we) just had test pilots” on inaugural missions.

Koch said in an interview with CNN’s Ed Lavandera that the group found out they were selected a few weeks ago.

“We were all sent to a meeting that was on our calendars under a different pretext that didn’t sound as lofty as the one it was going to be,” Koch said. “And accidentally two of us were very late to that meeting.”

She said the offer rendered her “speechless.”

“It truly is an honor,” she added. “It’s an honor — not to get myself in the space — but because it’s amazing to be a part of this team that’s going back to the moon and on to Mars.”

An interview with the four astronauts will air on “CNN This Morning” on Tuesday, which starts at 6 am ET.

#111. Jensen Huang broke Nvidia’s code of conduct, and his employees may not forget it

When Jensen Huang gave his first all-hands address since returning as CEO, there was an odd emphasis on Nvidia’s code of conduct. He admitted to violating the code, then emphasized that no one was exempt from it. But that “does not mean, the moment you violate a code of conduct, we walk you out the door,” Huang said, according to Wired. “Facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments” still matter. In other words, the code applies to everyone, but breaking it doesn’t automatically cost anyone their job, including, apparently, the CEO.

It’s an awkward stance for a company whose CEO has admitted to breaking its rules. A central part of modern human resources, a code of conduct can seem like a quasi-legal rulebook that lays out unacceptable behavior. But experts say most codes are better understood as a reflection of corporate culture. When an infraction goes unpunished, the main risk isn’t legal but cultural — that employees will feel embarrassed or even angry about the company they work for. That’s the risk Huang now faces at Nvidia.

Matt Plass, chief market strategy officer at LRN, an ethics and compliance training outfit, recently surveyed more than 2,000 workers across different industries for a study on workplace codes of conduct. As Plass sees it, the biggest risk is that employees will become ashamed of their workplace.

“People coming into the workforce now, ethics is incredibly important to them,” he told TechCrunch. “They’re really mindful of the ethical standards of their company. They’re really embarrassed and angry when senior leadership lets them down.”

The consequences of violating the rules aren’t always tangible, but they’re definitely real. Plass gave one example of a company that banned corporate travel to cut costs — only to have its CEO turn up in a VIP box at the Olympics.

“For over a decade afterwards, ‘the Olympics’ was used within that company as an example of the worst thing an employee could do. People would say, ‘Yeah, I shouldn’t have done that, but it’s not like I went to the Olympics.’”

Plass says companies that remove an executive typically do so after an investigation, then try to win back employees. “In most organizations they make a change at the top based on what’s happened,” he said, “and then they will have a hearts-and-minds initiative, to go out to their employee base and their customers and their investors and everybody else, and say, we get it. We’re sorry. We’ve changed. That’s essentially what they do.”

Of course, that playbook is harder to follow at a company like Nvidia, where Huang left without a clear succession plan, and there are serious doubts about whether the company could continue to thrive without him. In cases like that, Plass acknowledges there’s no easy answer.

“I would draw a distinction there between the kind of companies where the CEO is a kind of godhead figure … where the person and the company are almost impossible to separate,” he said. “There it’s much, much harder to deal with.”

In that context, Huang’s stutter-step over the code of conduct is easier to understand. Judging by the employee campaign for his reinstatement, Huang’s return hasn’t created a morale problem so far. But as the coming months put more pressure on Huang and his colleagues, sidestepping the code could end up costing Nvidia something harder to measure — its employees’ pride in the company.

#103. FCC approves SpaceX plan to put AI data centers in space

SpaceX was given conditional approval by the Federal Communications Commission on Wednesday to deploy and operate a constellation of up to 1 million satellites for its orbital data center business, a regulatory victory for Elon Musk and his bid to move AI data centers into space.

The FCC authorization was granted despite objections from Amazon, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society, which flagged concerns over orbital debris and congestion, radiofrequency interference, and environmental consequences. Amazon, which is building a competitor to SpaceX’s Starlink service, argued in its petition that the application lacked basic details and described it as a “lofty ambition rather than a real plan.” Despite that lack of detail, Amazon also said the proposal “risks real harm.”

The FCC dismissed some of the key concerns raised by opponents, including potential threats to scientific research, because the agency determined they fell outside its regulatory authority.

FCC Chair Brendan Carr said in a statement that the action “opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America’s leadership and growth.”

Musk thanked Carr in a post on X, writing “Much appreciate. This will be a great asset to America and the world.”

This initial regulatory approval doesn’t mean orbital data centers are imminent.

SpaceX faces a number of technical and economic challenges, including how to dramatically reduce the cost of manufacturing and launching satellites, cool processors in space, and even how to secure financing for chips that are destined for orbit.

The company must also clear other regulatory hurdles. The FCC has not yet approved SpaceX’s plans to handle orbital debris for satellites that would operate above 600 kilometers, for instance. SpaceX requested a waiver of existing FCC rules, which requires satellites ending missions below 2,000 kilometers reenter Earth’s atmosphere within five years of completing those missions. The company is interested in moving some of these retired satellites into other orbits around Earth or even around the sun, according to the FCC authorization document.

The Starmind satellites would primarily communicate using lasers to connect with other satellites, including SpaceX’s Starlink.

The FCC has given SpaceX time to sort out these issues. Under the approval, SpaceX has seven years to get its first operational satellite into orbit and 14 years to deploy the entire constellation.

China has maintained that the balloon was actually just a weather balloon thrown off course, and the US has been assessing the possibility that it was not deliberately maneuvered into the continental US by the Chinese government, CNN has reported.

Still, China did maintain some ability to maneuver the balloon, officials believe. And once the balloon was over Montana, China appeared to take advantage of its position to loiter over sensitive sites and try to collect intelligence.

The AI industry operates with efficiency as its north star. The promise of this technology is that we will be able to automate so much work that we can expand the capabilities of humanity, avoiding needless time spent tinkering with ultimately fruitless ideas. Instead, a prompt goes into an LLM, and a response is spat out.

This technological revolution exploded into the world with some soul-crushing side effects: children are inundated with mind-numbing slop on YouTube, lawyers cite nonexistent cases in court, and tech companies like Meta lay off tens of thousands of people as part of a corporate-branded “Year of Efficiency.” But against the backdrop of the AI industry’s first major IPO, Rockstar has unveiled a game that about 5,000 people labored over for more than a decade.

The final product is breathtaking, looking so lifelike that it can be hard to distinguish gameplay footage from real life. These sprawling open-world games are usually made possible by procedurally generating new landscapes and objects, but Rockstar has taken the time to painstakingly craft every detail of the world, even developing 30,000 different nonplayable characters with unique appearances and personalities.

The video game industry is already grappling with the threat of AI. Google’s Project Genie, which launched earlier this year, allows users to create and wander around 3D worlds generated from prompts to an LLM. The stock prices of several video game companies faltered around the time of Project Genie’s release, perhaps because of investors’ uncertainty about the future relevance of video game developers.

“The notion that somehow new tools would allow an individual to push a button and generate a hit, market a hit, and bring it to many millions of consumers around the world, it’s a laughable notion,” Take-Two Interactive CEO Strauss Zelnick said at the time. “It’s just never been the case with entertainment.”

Zelnick’s fellow tech CEOs see different future, though. On a Meta earnings call this year, CEO Mark Zuckerberg promised that, “soon, you’ll have an AI that understands you and [can] even generate great personalized content for you.”

Elon Musk even went as far as proposing that a game like GTA VI could be made with AI.

“You won’t even have to ask,” Musk wrote on X. “AI will figure what video game you’d like best.”

This is the paradox of “Grandthropic” week. GTA VI is so impressively vast and captivating because its developers could make mistakes, experiment, and rework ideas, even if that meant delaying the game (twice). GTA VI was incubated with the exact inefficiencies that investors hope AI companies like Anthropic can help eradicate.

“Plenty of fantastic art — video games and elsewhere — almost by definition has to be created slowly, and there’s something to having that kind of inefficiency around it,” Feder said. “Creative output requires deletion, right? Getting to kind of a masterpiece oftentimes requires an enormous amount of deletion. And when you’re talking about a game of the kind of complexity we’re talking about, that kind of deletion ends up being very expensive, and it is by definition not efficient.”

GTA VI reeks of its own humanity – every small detail is loaded with intention, but it’s this impressive precision that keeps the world abuzz, triggering the kind of monocultural moment that has become increasingly rare in a media landscape that revolves around highly personalized social media algorithms.

The game forces the player to slow down, which is either frustrating or genius, depending on your sense of humor. People waited more than three presidential terms for the next installment in a high-octane action franchise, only to download a 212 GB behemoth of a game in which your earliest missions involve driving around doing errands and putting up drywall to make your girlfriend happy. Don’t worry — there are still car chases and shootouts — but in a moment when AI feels suffocating, GTA VI is such an artistic achievement that it is reaching audiences that don’t even like violent video games.

The ultimate fantasy of GTA VI is no longer to role-play as a criminal — the fantasy is just taking your time to relish the mundane and explore an expansive world that developers were given the time and space to perfect.

#92. Saudi pop music is thriving — but for the right reasons?

As part of Vision 2030, Saudi Arabia's music scene has evolved hugely thanks to state funding and support. Local musicians and DJs are optimistic. But are they actually just helping prop up an authoritarian state?

Today, rapper Asayel Bishi is one of Saudi Arabia's rising hip-hop stars, signed to the country's biggest record label for new music, MDLBEAST. But not so long ago, she was making international headlines for a totally different reason.

In 2020, after Bishi released a music video for a song, "Bint Mecca," or "girl from Mecca," featuring people dancing in a local cafe, Mecca's governor called for the arrest of Bishi's whole production team. He said the video violated the holy city's local customs and traditions. In the end, the situation was resolved with a discussion about filming permits.

And by early 2023, Bishi was signed to MDLBEAST and has since racked up hundreds of thousands of streams on music platforms like Spotify.

In the past, other Saudi rappers have ended up in prison for similar, alleged transgressions. The fact that Bishi's story went a different way is probably partially because the 31-year-old's career path is on a similar trajectory to the Saudi pop music industry as a whole.

As part of Saudi ruler Mohammed bin Salman's Vision 2030 plan, announced in 2016 and which aims to move the country away from economic dependence on oil, the country has poured billions into the local music industry.

The pace of development increased in 2019 after the country held its first major festival, Soundstorm, an annual event that now draws major international acts and over 400,000 guests. MDLBEAST was created then, primarily as the festival organizer. Since then, it has also become a record label, radio station, distributor, promoter and talent incubator.

A recent report for the Saudi Music Commission suggests the music sector could be worth $500 million (€455 million) by 2034. But it's difficult to figure out how well Saudi pop is really doing now because independent monitoring of the sector only began relatively recently.

Streaming platform Spotify reports that people listening to Saudi-made music inside Saudi Arabia increased by 195% between 2020 and 2024. However, the most-streamed artists inside the country are still international.

Although there was a mostly-hidden Saudi music scene before this recent boom, observers say the growth of the Saudi music sector is not organic.

It's all about building an industry from the top down, Amr Abdelrahim, a researcher at Sciences Po in Paris, reported in a 2024 study. "After 2016, young middle-class musicians and creative professionals, who had been careful to avoid drawing official attention to their countercultural activities suddenly became central to the ambitions of the state," he wrote.

The careers of young Saudi acts — like Bishi, for example — "became tied to the destiny of the crown prince," the researcher explained.

The Saudi royal family has long dispersed money from Saudi oil sales to their subjects, in order to ensure economic dependence and loyalty. But, Abdelrahim argued, "what is new about Saudi investments in culture under MBS is an unprecedented degree of centralization and the emergence of new clientelist networks around the country's de-facto ruler," using a common abbreviation for Crown Prince Mohammed.

Those involved "are inadvertently participating in the consolidation of the Saudi regime," he argued.

Observers say a lot of the new music businesses are vague about their ties to the state. But it's clear they couldn't work without the state's financial support and approval.

"Almost all major events are organized by the state or funded by closely linked entities," a spokesperson from Saudi-focused organization, ALQST for Human Rights, told DW. "This affects the type of music produced, as musicians have to be mindful of what is acceptable. This is not just about the music they produce, but also how they act and what they say to their fans."

ALQST believes Saudi musicians have even less artistic freedom under the new musical system than before, when they would have played or performed privately. They can lose their contracts and livelihoods, be prevented from performing or even arrested, ALQST's spokesperson said.

"In the past, the music scene was not regulated," explained the guitarist and bassist for Saudi black metal band, Al-Namrood. But now that it is, the state can more closely monitor musicians who once stayed hidden and only played privately, the musician, who goes by the name of Mephisto, told DW.

Al-Namrood are one of the rare examples of a Saudi act that isn't working with state support. The group first formed in 2008 but have never played at home and have kept their identities secret for almost two decades.

"The exchange is clear: You want to perform, it has to be under our rules," Mephisto continued. "And I totally understand the perspective [of some musicians]. But typically the artist must refrain from any criticism of the religious or authoritarian. [The state] doesn't necessarily buy an artist's loyalty, it just creates incentives that make criticism increasingly costly."

For Al-Namrood, who have often condemned Saudi conservatism, religion and politics in their music, that's not an option. "We don't want anything to do with them," Mephisto said.

In her 2020 paper for the journal, Arabian Humanities, Nora Derbal, a professor at the University of Hamburg, said Saudi artists told her that if they got a call from authorities asking them to participate in a government project, it would be an offer they couldn't turn down.

The artists she spoke with were debating "their cooptation and loss of autonomy, and about the consequences for not joining in state projects," she wrote.

On the other hand, in various interviews, Saudi acts who have partaken in their country's pop music renaissance have also been positive: They don't have to hide anymore, they can play for larger audiences and they are paid. In an interview, one Saudi DJ described the first Soundstorm festival as like "Berlin after the fall of the wall" while others said they felt they were making history.

"So that's the interesting paradox," said Fannie Agerschou-Madsen, a postdoctoral researcher at the University of Roskilde in Denmark, who's written several papers on the topic. "We have all these social liberalizations and challenges to conservative norms but at the same time that's not being followed by political liberalization."

But that doesn't mean that some room isn't being created for meaningful change, Agerschou-Madsen told DW. Locals told her they enjoyed the newfound freedom of going to a dance party although, she noted, the subversiveness seemed to be more directed at the social conservatism than Saudi authorities.

Young Saudis are well aware of the red lines and state control of the music scene and are continually negotiating boundaries, the researcher argued. In that way, "their everyday practices create space for political and social change, little by little, although perhaps not in what you'd consider a typically revolutionary way."

So is the Saudi state co-opting local musicians, including those who might have said something controversial before they ever get a chance to?

A DW enquiry to MDLBEAST was not answered. And the answer likely lies somewhere in the middle.

For some young Saudis, the new music scene has been about fun, self-expression, belonging and creativity, Agerschou-Madsen wrote in a new, as-yet-unpublished paper shared with DW. "Others, however, view it as a top-down project that commodifies fun while sidestepping deeper issues of political freedom and social equity."

#88. A month with the iPhone Duo has changed how I use my phone

For years, the definition of a premium smartphone depended on its cameras, processors, and how light it felt when you stowed it in your pocket. The iPhone Duo challenges that concept by asking consumers to pay more for a fundamentally different smartphone experience, even if it means sacrificing what they’ve long thought of as high-end features.

In a little under a month of using the Duo, I’ve found that the trade-offs I was initially concerned about matter far less to me than I had expected. What’s been more interesting is how the Duo’s foldable form factor has changed my everyday habits, including how I interact with apps, which apps I use, and how I organize and personalize the phone itself.

I also easily forgot a number of my initial concerns about switching to the Duo. The apps, for the most part, are now optimized for the Duo; I’ve reacquainted myself with Touch ID; and I don’t miss the Pro Max camera on most days.

That’s not to say the Duo doesn’t have its shortcomings. I still miss that telephoto lens sometimes, and I’m still longing for Face ID — though not as much since Apple shipped a face unlock feature in the latest update. But after a month of using the Duo, I’ve found these compromises have had less of an impact on my day-to-day experience compared with the benefits that the new form factor brings.

I was surprised by how much I’ve grown to appreciate the Duo’s multitasking capabilities. Initially, I didn’t think I’d regularly use it to view two apps side by side, given the still fairly limited screen space even while the phone is unfolded, especially compared with an iPad. But I’ve found myself snapping apps to both halves of the screen more often than I had imagined.

For starters, Apple has made this side-by-side mode incredibly intuitive and easy to use — you simply drag the app over to the left or right side of the screen and drop it into the target box that appears.

I have regularly taken advantage of this feature to do all sorts of things.

I really enjoy keeping X open as I read through the news, which allows me to switch back and forth between the article I’m reading and real-time reactions to it. Sometimes I’ll keep email or Slack open on the side while I’m working on something else so I don’t miss an important message or notification.

And much like the overstimulated members of Gen Z (which I am not), I sometimes like scrolling social media while engaging with some other media off to the side.

Case in point: I passively watched Netflix’s “Love is Blind” reunion while reading the gossipy tweets and Reddit posts about the main characters on the other side of the screen. This sort of TV watching behavior used to be called second-screen viewing, but now it’s morphing into something new. Everything is happening on one screen, so should we call it “Duo screen viewing,” perhaps? It makes me long for even more interactive TV experiences where commentary and content are combined.

I’ve noticed another shift in my behavior since adopting the Duo as my main device. When I’m out and about, the Duo feels more like an (arguably heavier) iPhone mini. For those who still miss smaller iPhones, the Duo is the next best thing. Despite its weight, the phone feels small. You can cover it with one hand; you can type with a thumb; you can reach the top and bottom easily; and you can even fit it into your front and back pockets. The Pro Max now feels unbelievably strange in my hand — how was I ever comfortable wrangling this giant rectangle?

The Duo’s small screen works well when you’re on the go, especially for short interactions like responding to a quick text or pulling up directions to a destination. It’s not as great for mindless scrolling, which I appreciate, as it forces me to engage with the real world instead of killing every moment of boredom or unease by opening up apps.

But when you’re home or somewhere with time to kill, you can unfold the screen and enjoy your apps super-sized. It’s the best of both worlds.

I find that I instinctively unfold the phone when I’m preparing to watch videos, because the larger screen is best for that. It’s also unfolded if I’m shopping, as it’s more convenient for looking at products, zooming in to examine the details, or complete checkout forms.

Generally, I also unfold the Duo when I’m actively using apps. This setup has also allowed me to rethink my Home Screen layout: With the Duo closed, I prefer to see just widgets on the small screen with key information like weather, time, battery life, and so on. Meanwhile, the only app icons on the Home Screen are those in the dock.

In the weeks since its launch, most of the apps that weren’t yet optimized for the Duo have been upgraded to support its foldable form factor. In mid-November, Apple said there were over 200,000 Duo-ready apps, and in my experience, that certainly feels true. A majority of my so-called “daily drivers” — with the glaring exception of key Google apps like Gmail and Drive — have dropped the black band on the side of the screen that was frustrating early testers of the phone.

Most apps now take advantage of the available space, whether that’s on the Duo’s small front screen or the wider, unfolded one.

I’ve also found it interesting to see the different interpretations developers have had for how to best optimize their app for Duo.

Some have used the form factor as a way to bring in some sort of novelty: there’s a note-taking app that opens and closes like a notebook, with pages you can write on with the Apple Pencil; a retro Walkman-like music player; a tiny typewriter; a gaming handheld; a game where you close the phone to make grilled cheese.

As much as I love the whimsy of this group of apps, I appreciate how my everyday apps feel better to use on the unfolded screen. Spotify, at last, feels less cluttered when it has more screen real estate. A number of note-taking, list-making, and calendar apps also feel somehow more usable when spread out.

The Netflix app is actually fantastic with its support for multiple viewing modes, which include playing video on the closed outer display, the “laptop” orientation with its big player controls, and the “tent” mode.

Other developers have similarly reimagined how their app would work if it had originally been designed for the Duo’s small and larger screens.

One good example of this is Sofa, a handy utility for organizing the books, movies, TV shows, podcasts, albums, and games you want to check out. When the Duo is closed, its navigation is tucked off to the right side in a vertical bar, while the main screen focuses on the items you’ve saved. But when the Duo is unfolded, the number of items displayed on the screen also expands into a two-column layout, with more of your items featured on the screen and your lists on the left side. It doesn’t feel like two different apps, but like you’ve opened up the app itself and given it more space to spread out.

In terms of your daily social media scroll, apps like TikTok and Instagram have been updated to be perfectly usable on the Duo, whether the phone is open or closed. When closed, TikTok now stretches the video to cover the screen, instead of showing the ugly black bar. Scrolling short-form videos on the Duo, while unfolded like a slightly smaller iPad mini, has become my preferred way to watch.

Here’s another surprise: I’ve spent less time with my Kindle, and am instead reading books on my Duo. Unfolded, the Duo is a decent enough replacement for an e-book reader, albeit it can be a bit more stimulating as it’s not an e-ink device. However, the matte inner screen isn’t quite as bright and distracting as your typical glossy OLED display. There’s less glare and softer reflections, but there’s also something about the display that just feels calmer than a smartphone, allowing you to more fully engage with a book’s text.

Unfolded and held vertically, reading on the Duo feels a lot like reading on the standard Kindle, at least in terms of hand-feel. And putting your phone into the “Do Not Disturb” focus mode can help you not be distracted by incoming notifications and messages while you read.

You can also unfold the device while keeping it in landscape orientation, with the screens bent slightly inward like a real book. For even more fun, you can turn on Kindle’s page-turn animation, which makes it seem as if you’re turning the page in a physical book. And I love how you can “performatively read” using an app like Book2Life or Actually Read — they display the book’s cover on the Duo’s outer screen as you read the text on the inner screen.

E-book readers will likely find they can carry one less device when they’re out and about, because the Duo can stand in for whatever Kindle or other reader you typically keep in your purse or bag. You can save the Kindle for other times, whether that’s at home, on long travel days, or when you don’t want to worry about sand getting into your $2,000 phone.

On the big-screened iPhone 17 Pro Max I used to carry, I tended to keep a PopSocket of some sort on the back because the phone was both heavy and large, which made it hard to hold for longer periods of time. The Duo is similarly heavy, but its shape makes it far easier to hold in one hand without the extra help.

Folded, you can do the usual one-handed hold where the bottom edge of the phone rests on your pinky, your other three fingers cradle the back, and your thumb is free to navigate the screen.

Unfolded, the Duo’s like using a small Kindle or iPad mini, where two hands are required. You also don’t need a PopSocket for balancing the phone on the side, because it can be propped up like a tent or opened like a tiny laptop. Or, if you prefer, Apple and others offer Duo cases with a built-in stand. (Apple’s case comes in navy and taupe, though, so if you’re not boring, you’ll probably want to shop from third-party vendors).

If you think you still might need a PopSocket at times, or you just aren’t ready to give it up, the brand now makes a slimmer version: its Low-Pro Grip. There are other competitors, like the OhSnap grips, which aren’t a bad alternative either.

There’s something about the Duo that seems to have kicked off a new iPhone personalization trend, at least judging by what I’ve been seeing on social media.

Unlike a regular iPhone, where every phone case is simply a shell that offers a degree of protection based on the materials used, the Duo case lineup provides far more variety.

You can buy cases with and without built-in screen protectors, and some use adhesive to better stick to the outer screen, while others don’t. Some cases let you customize the band around the outer screen with a different color than the case that protects the rest of the phone.

Then you have cases that cover the hinge, while others leave it exposed. There are cases that offer holes where you can attach straps, wristlets, and charms. Some of those have attachment points at the bottom and others on the side.

Essentially, there are a lot of options for customizing how you want to carry the Duo and how you want to express your own style.

I’m also now starting to see whimsical cases that come with attached pens. (Thank goodness — most of the pen cases so far have been kind of plain!)

I also believe there’s something about the Duo that particularly appeals to women. Maybe because it’s small, or maybe it’s because you can attach a strap to its side and carry it like a little purse. Or maybe because, like a purse, you can deck it out with chains and charms — a choice that’s grown increasingly popular as young people have embraced whimsical, retro tech as a way to express their individuality and sense of fashion.

The Duo easily lends itself to this trend, perhaps even more than a regular iPhone. It becomes an accessory you want on display, not one buried in a bag.

Before switching to the Duo, I spent a lot of time thinking about what I’d be giving up by moving away from the Pro Max.

I’m surprised to find that, a month later, I only think about what I’d have to give up if I switched back to a traditional iPhone. It’s not just the extra space of the unfolded screen I’d miss — it’s all the little habits I’ve developed around having different screen sizes for different purposes.

The Duo makes me pause and think about how I want to use an app. That small moment of deliberation, before I even open the phone, has helped me break some of my more mindless smartphone habits, particularly around social media.

That’s not a benefit I could have anticipated if I’d judged the device by its specifications alone.

The verdict was reached by a majority of nine votes to six among a panel of judges from around the world.

Lenacapavir works by targeting the protective protein shell of HIV, disrupting the virus's ability to multiply and establish an infection in the body.

#54. Albania's youth are protesting in Flamingo Revolution, not leaving the country anymore

Youth protests against a rigid political system and corruption have shaken the government of Albanian Prime Minister Edi Rama. They also show that fewer young people are willing to emigrate in search of a better future.

In mid-October, students in the Albanian capital Tirana set a suitcase on fire and threw it into the courtyard of the Education Ministry. Everyone in the country understood the symbolism: According to the 2023 census, the population of this Western Balkan country shrunk by about 420,000 between 2011 and 2023 — by nearly 15% — primarily because of emigration.

For a generation of young Albanians, this was the response to a system that gave them little voice. But now young people want to stay, and the protesters are saying that the system has to change.

Known as the "Flamingo Revolution," the social movement began in May with demonstrations against a luxury resort in a coastal reserve that US President Donald Trump's daughter Ivanka Trump and her husband Jared Kushner were involved in.

It quickly evolved into broader criticism against corruption, Prime Minister Edi Rama's government and his ruling Socialist Party and the country's entire political class.

Thousands marched almost every evening for four months in protests that were largely peaceful. But on October 13, a student protest escalated and there were clashes with police. Since then, the unrest has continued, with police using tear gas, water cannons and rubber bullets against demonstrators. There have been hundreds of arrests and many injuries, amid damage to public and private property.

The protesters have called for Rama's resignation, a transitional government, electoral reform and fresh elections. The movement, which has no leader, has support from people across the political spectrum. And while the prime minister has blamed "foreign hands" for the movement, there seems to be little evidence that external actors are orchestrating the protests, though they may have attempted to exploit the unrest online.

The protesters, who do not feel represented by Albania's democracy, are driven by domestic issues: corruption, the high cost of living and low wages. This is nothing new, but many factors have converged recently: a series of high-level corruption charges, including against ministers and two of Rama's former deputies, rising prices and the fact that Albania is being measured against EU standards by a new generation.

In certain areas, Rama has transformed Albania since taking office in 2013: the economy has grown significantly, as the government likes to point out, and tourism is booming. But the prosperity remains unevenly distributed. Critics accuse the government of channeling too much taxpayer money into a small number of well-connected companies through prestigious projects and public-private partnerships, and in some cases through corrupt business deals, at the expense of healthcare, education and better economic prospects for the wider population.

Rama has rejected the calls for his resignation and has invited protesters to engage in dialogue, which they have so far refused.

In 2008, Rama and Sali Berisha, the long-time chairman of the opposition Democratic Party, worked on a reform to Albania's constitution and electoral law, which was approved by parliament. Since then, it is party leaders who have determined who enters Parliament, which weakens ties between lawmakers and their constituents.

In addition, it has become significantly more difficult for new parties to win seats. In 2025, a new party received about 64,000 votes, but only one seat. The Socialists, by contrast, won about one seat for every 10,000 votes.

Rama has been the leader of the Socialists since 2005, while Berisha first became the chairman of the Democratic Party in 1991. For 21 years, Albania's prime minister has either been Rama or Berisha. Freedom House, a US nongovernmental organization that promotes democracy worldwide, classifies Albania as "partly free" and describes the country's political parties as being "often organized around leading personalities."

Protesters on the streets have said that they want both men to be jailed. Rama has argued that governments should be replaced at the ballot box, not on the streets.

Though the Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe (OSCE) acknowledged that parliamentary elections in 2025 were "competitive and professionally managed" it said that they had taken place "in a highly polarized environment and contestants did not enjoy a level playing field."

It added that there were "reports of intimidation, misuse of public resources and pressure on public employees, which undermined equal opportunities."

The Democratic Party also spoke of election fraud and intimidation. Turnout was below 45%.

Many protesters also allege that when it comes to business, what matters is the proximity to people in power. They criticize the public procurement, public auctions and concessions system, which has led to state-owned land, including coastal and protected areas, being sold to private investors.

Ordinary Albanians are feeling the financial brunt. According to the Albanian Institute of Statistics (INSTAT), half of all employees earn less than 60,000 lek (roughly €620 or $680) per month after taxes. But according to Eurostat, food prices are slightly above the EU average and real estate prices in Tirana have nearly doubled since 2018, with rents rising just as quickly.

For many years, young Albanians who were dissatisfied with the system opted to leave the country. The number of Albanian citizens in Germany more than doubled between 2018 and 2024, reaching almost 120,000.

Though it is still too early to say whether there are fewer Albanians leaving today, for many protesters staying has become a political statement.

Rama has argued that his resignation would jeopardize Albania's bid to join the European Union by 2030, but the vast majority of Albanians are pro-European, including those on the streets and in the opposition. From the protesters' perspective, it is the government headed by Rama that is the real obstacle on the path to the EU.

Though there is currently a wave of protests across Europe, the only obvious comparison is with Serbia, where a student-led movement recently forced a long-time ruler from office. Whether Albania will follow this example depends on whether young people, who have traditionally not been deeply engaged in politics, can turn their protests into votes.

What is certain is that for three decades, Albania's leaders have relied on their citizens' suitcases. Now, the country's students have burned that very luggage.

A press photo from 2021 - migrants in a boat from Marvin Reuter's firm (logo circled)

It is not exactly clear when police suspicions were aroused.
