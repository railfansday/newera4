#83. A clinic is reassessing people with ADHD and autism diagnoses - but critics fear it could deepen stigma

Johanna, 24, began questioning her ADHD diagnosis when she discovered it could act as a barrier to becoming a police officer

Freya looks nervous as she walks into a psychiatric clinic in Sweden for an ADHD assessment.

Countries around the world have seen a huge increase in ADHD diagnoses over recent decades. For many people this can be life changing, helping them understand themselves better and access support.

But Freya (not her real name) is not attending a long-awaited appointment to secure a diagnosis.

This government-funded clinic offers autism and ADHD diagnosis reassessments - its doctors believe it is the first in the world dedicated solely to this purpose.

At 12, Freya was struggling with anxiety at school and sought out professional help. "They thought I had ADHD," she says.

But as she grew up, she felt that she did not experience ADHD symptoms. "I don't think I had it in the first place," she now says.

Her doubts about the diagnosis became more pressing as a teenager, when she discovered that in Sweden, it could be a barrier to pursuing her dream career of joining the police.

"I want to have every option open," she tells BBC World Service.

If the doctors at the clinic decide Freya does not meet the ADHD criteria, her medical records will be updated, and it will be easier for her to apply for a job with the police.

The clinic, known as Mottagning Neuropsykiatri, has opened at a time when rates of ADHD and autism diagnoses are under the spotlight. Many say this is because of greater awareness of the symptoms, and that previously neglected groups - particularly women and girls - are finally getting the diagnoses they need, while others argue that the growing numbers stem from overdiagnosis.

The existence of the clinic is controversial. Clinical guidance from UK governing bodies and the World Health Organization describe autism as a lifelong condition and ADHD as a neurodevelopmental condition that can persist into adulthood. The medical consensus is that both diagnoses last a lifetime, even if symptoms fluctuate.

And some neurodiversity advocates warn that a dedicated reassessment service could reinforce the idea that being diagnosed with ADHD or autism is undesirable â and deepen the stigma surrounding both conditions.

Despite this, hundreds of people have approached the clinic about reassessment.

Like Freya, other young adults have been prompted to reconsider their diagnosis after they felt it had become a barrier.

This is because historically, Swedes with ADHD or autism were barred from certain jobs, including joining the military, and becoming police officers, train drivers and pilots. This is despite research suggesting some neurodivergent traits can be strengths in certain roles. No such bans exist in the UK, although rules differ globally.

Since 2022, people with ADHD have been able to join the Swedish military if they are medication-free. And since 2021, people with ADHD or autism have been able to apply to join the police, but must submit a medical certificate, which can cost around 15,000 SEK (Â£1,100) privately.

When Freya was referred to the clinic, she says she was relieved to find out she could get her diagnosis reassessed there for free.

Johanna, 24, was diagnosed with ADHD at 14 after what she describes as a period of bad behaviour at school. She found the diagnosis helpful at first and received additional support from teachers.

But as she grew into adulthood, she says she stopped feeling that she needed extra support. And after discovering it was a barrier to becoming a police officer, she began questioning whether it was even accurate.

Johanna says she has some traits of ADHD, such as a short attention span, "but not enough to have an ADHD diagnosis," she believes.

Johanna was reassessed aged 21 during an early pilot of the reassessment clinic and was found not to meet its diagnostic criteria for ADHD. She now works for her father's company, and does not plan on becoming a police officer.

Both ADHD and autism are neurodevelopmental conditions that affect how people think and interact with the world. They are diagnosed through detailed assessments, which can include questionnaires and interviews, rather than one single clinical test.

The doctors at Mottagning Neuropsykiatri say their approach is different from traditional assessments, as it focuses less on someone's history, and more on the symptoms they are experiencing in the present.

"This is not de-diagnosing, this is a renewed assessment of your functioning today," says Sebastian LundstrÃ¶m, the clinic's lead psychologist.

The doctors say they do not judge whether the original assessment was correct, so rather than wiping the original diagnosis from patients' medical records, they add a note of the new assessment.

LundstrÃ¶m says that some people who once had a diagnosis but are found to not meet the criteria at the clinic may have been misdiagnosed. But he also believes that for a small minority, ADHD and autism are not lifelong conditions and individuals may stop experiencing symptoms as they grow older.

But updating a person's diagnosis because they are not currently showing symptoms is a contested approach.

"We have to remember that these are lifelong conditions," says Navid Ghannad, chair of Autism SkÃ¥ne, a local Swedish non-profit for autistic people and their families.

"Because at a certain point in their adulthood during a clinical setting they have had their diagnosis removed, it doesn't necessarily mean that the ADHD or the autism has disappeared," he adds.

Symptoms could reappear later in life, he says. "The problem is once they resurface, that individual which doesn't have a diagnosis any longer, they will not be able to get the timely and correct support."

LundstrÃ¶m says he does not consider it a big risk that people could get reassessed and find themselves without necessary support.

During the pilot he encountered people who "really wanted" to get rid of their diagnosis but the psychologist said, "we still feel that you meet the criteria".

Vilda, 22, sought reassessment of her autism diagnosis as she feels she was misdiagnosed

Vilda, 22, sought reassessment of her autism diagnosis which was initially given after she experienced a trauma.

"I didn't recognise myself in the description of autism," she says. "I was stuck. I know that this is not me, but I can't do anything about it."

It was requests from people like Johanna and Vilda that led to the establishment of the clinic, LundstrÃ¶m explains.

"It gained momentum by itself and the word spread around. A lot of people contacted us and we felt that there was a justification for starting up," he says. It is only open one day a month.

Before opening the clinic, LundstrÃ¶m conducted a small peer-reviewed study - of the 72 individuals who took part, roughly 80% did not meet the criteria for ADHD or autism. All the participants already wanted to lose their diagnosis, rather than being a randomly selected group.

"It's important to stress that this patient population is not necessarily representative for all with autism and ADHD," he adds.

During the first year of the pilot some 400 to 500 people spoke to LundstrÃ¶m, who is also associate professor of child and adolescent psychiatric sciences at the University of Gothenburg, about getting a reassessment.

The project also has the backing of senior figures in Swedish psychiatry, including Sophia Eberhard, a senior consultant specialising in child and adolescent psychiatry.

"It's psychiatry who in the beginning made those diagnoses," she says. "I believe we have a responsibility to help these young adults on their way."

In September, the BBC was given permission to join Freya as she became the first patient to attend the newly opened clinic, which serves both men and women.

During her appointment, Freya takes part in questionnaires and verbal assessments to see if she meets the ADHD criteria. The process is the same for both ADHD and autism, although the questions differ.

Freya says she is hopeful that she will be able to "get rid" of her ADHD diagnosis after the appointment.

But the doctors are adamant that they do not just give attendees the outcome they want. They conduct other checks such as assessing body language and communication skills, speaking to younger patients' parents, and checking medical files for ongoing treatment.

The opening of the clinic has drawn criticism from those who fear it sends the wrong message about autism and ADHD.

"The clinic presents neurodiversity and autistic people as a bad thing, like we are a problem that needs to be fixed - we are not," says Jenny Hedin, from charity Organiserade Autister VÃ¤sternorrland, an advocacy organisation run by and for autistic adults.

Hedin, who is autistic and has ADHD, warns that people who can successfully "mask" or mimic neurotypical behaviour might feel under pressure to lose their diagnosis in order to be better accepted by society.

"That is especially a problem with women and girls, because girls are usually great at masking," she adds.

Others say the focus should be on changing the rules that make it difficult for people with ADHD or autism to pursue certain careers.

Eric Donell, the chairman of RiksfÃ¶rbundet Attention, a national Swedish charity, lobbies to remove employment restrictions affecting neurodivergent people, which he says are unfair.

He stresses that for many people, getting a diagnosis is extremely important. "Instead of asking people to get rid of their diagnosis, sometimes we should just ask whether the rules themselves are justified," he says.

Reassessments risk becoming "a way of adapting the individual to society instead of adapting society to human differences," he adds.

People from the UK, Denmark, Norway and Germany have also been in touch for reassessment, says LundstrÃ¶m, but they have been turned away, as the clinic only serves locals.

Georgina, 35, from the UK, contacted the clinic because she wants to have the autism diagnosis she received at 25 "removed".

"It felt like a magic spell, that I'd get this label and the world would be more understanding," she says.

Instead, she says, her colleagues belittled her.

Georgina has since been offered an NHS reassessment. The process is the same as the original assessment she went through, and can result in patients receiving more diagnoses, fewer diagnoses or no change.

The Swedish reassessment clinic says they generally would not apply new or additional diagnoses to people - something Georgina was attracted to.

"The situation where you can go in and then come out with other labels... that's really off-putting," she says. For now, she has decided not to undergo an NHS reassessment. Instead, she is trying to find out how often a diagnosis is reconsidered, before deciding whether to go ahead.

Back in Sweden, a few hours after Freya entered the clinic, she re-emerges from a consultation room. The doctors here have found that, right now, she does not meet the criteria for an ADHD diagnosis.

She says it is "confirmation" that she was right to question her diagnosis.

"Now, finally, I have the same opportunity as everyone else."

#82. X finally gets parental controls after 20 years

After 20 years of existence, X, the social network formerly known as Twitter, has finally added parental controls for teenagers, giving parents and guardians a way to manage what their kids see and interact with on the platform. But unlike most platforms’ implementation of such controls, X is putting its own spin on the system by giving teens a say in the matter.

The company said accounts belonging to 13-to-17-year-olds will be set to “protected mode” by default, meaning they won’t be able to see sensitive content, and won’t be served targeted ads. However, X is allowing teenagers to retain a measure of control over their accounts, letting them see what restrictions were made to their accounts. Parents and guardians will also not be able to read their teens’ private messages, see search or viewing history, or post as their teen.

The controls let parents see how much time their teen spends on X per week, set daily limits, require their approval for new followers, limit feed recommendations and videos to already followed accounts, filter specific words, turn off sensitive media, specify who can send their teen DMs, and see who they are messaging.

The controls arrive months after X came under fire for a lack of controls preventing the platform’s AI chatbot from being used to generate sexualized images of children and women. The social network has also long been rife with child sexual abuse material, misinformation, AI bots, and openly lets users post porn.

X has generally not been a platform aimed at teenagers, but that doesn’t mean they don’t use the platform. A 2024 study found that nearly a third of Gen Alpha kids have an X account. The platform itself requires users to be at least 13 years old, though that’s not a high bar given the kind of content on the platform.

“Parents want practical tools, and teens deserve room to grow. With Parental Controls on X, families can set the guardrails that fit their home, while teens keep ownership of their accounts and can see every change. Striking that balance is vital and what we set out to build.” Michael O’Herlihy, head of safety at X, wrote in a blog post.

#80. Behind China's spectacular rise, voices who defied it say they are being drowned out

China's economic might is drowning out our voices, say activists and dissidents

For Uyghur activist Dolkun Isa, who has lived in exile in Germany for nearly three decades, the thinning of the crowds is the clearest measure of how much his cause is slipping off the world's radar.

"Back in 2018, we could easily get thousands of people out in the streets to protest against China's human rights violations. Nowadays, we have trouble finding even 500."

It was in 2018 that reports emerged that China had detained up to a million Uyghurs and other Muslims in mass internment camps in its north-western province of Xinjiang. Beijing, which said the camps were for "re-education" to counter separatist violence, denied the allegations detailing alarming rights abuses.

But an international outcry, including protests, followed. Western governments rolled out trade bans and sanctions against senior Chinese officials, and froze their assets, and at least nine countries issued parliamentary resolutions condemning the detentions.

It was a time "when we felt the world was finally listening", says Dolkun, 59, who served as president of the Munich-based World Uyghur Congress from 2017-2024 .

But today, "we find ourselves competing against economic interests and geopolitical calculations to capture the world's attention. For me, this is one of the most painful aspects of our struggle".

Uyghur activist Dolkun Isa has lived in exile in Germany for nearly three decades

Few corners of the world remain untouched by China's rise. Its electric vehicles pack highways from Brazil to Belgium, made-in-China batteries, solar panels, AI models and microdramas fuel daily consumption in so many places, and Labubu dolls, Pop Mart's furry gap-toothed plush, dangle from backpacks almost everywhere.

China's immense economic clout now also has the flex of soft power, drowning out voices that say their fight against the Communist Party cannot compete with the country's gleaming skylines, advanced exports or futuristic ambitions.

Buoyed by its successes, Beijing is also swapping the "stick" for the "carrot", says Uyghur journalist Arslan Hidayat, who was born and raised in Australia, where he runs Kashgar Times.

Activists tell the BBC Beijing has been offering family reunions, money and the promise of safety - in return, they are asked for information about their networks and to promote pro-party narratives. The result, activists say, is a movement with fewer voices and less trust.

The Chinese embassy in the UK dismissed the activists' claims that they would be asked to spy on or disclose information about fellow exiles.

Shenzhen, a rich, buzzing city on the south-eastern coast, has emerged as one of China's star attractions for tech and tourism

China's "economic muscle" has earned it respect abroad and reshaped foreign policy, says Ian Johnson, a Pulitzer Prize-winning journalist who has long reported on Chinese civil society.

"At the same time, Western governments have become much more inwardly focused and populist, pushing human rights down their lists of priorities."

China's human rights record has not earned a mention in any of the three summits Xi Jinping and US President Donald Trump held this year, including the one in Shenzhen last week. As Beijing's leverage on the global stage has grown, so has the cost of confrontation or condemnation.

This shift in Beijing's image and tactics is deeply worrying, dissidents say, because it is weakening both their networks and the international pressure they hope to build.

In July Tibetan activist Lobga Rangzen died after he set himself on fire outside the UN headquarters in New York. He was protesting against China's sweeping new "ethnic unity law" which, among other things, requires minorities, including Tibetans, to learn Mandarin. The UN called it an "absolutely tragic" incident, but attention quickly faded.

His death was "an appeal to the international community", says Tenzin Dorjee, a researcher at the Tibet Action Institute, but he thinks much of the world is reluctant to criticise China because of the power it wields.

That same month Dolkun tried to rally supporters against the same law but he met with a muted response.

"Many people have dialled down on their activism over the years because they are thinking about their loved ones back in China," Dolkun says.

Donald Trump and Xi Jinping have held three summits so far this year

Arslan says he has interviewed at least a dozen Uyghur exiles who were "invited to go back to China on the condition that they would provide information to Chinese agents from time to time".

The questions seem "fairly innocuous", he adds: "Who lives around you? Which Uyghur restaurants do you frequent? We believe these are used to build out the profiles of dissidents overseas."

Beijing's embassy in the UK said in its response to the BBC that "facilitating visits to China by overseas Chinese to reunite with their families" is a "normal part" of consular services.

"China respects and protects the lawful rights and interests of people of all ethnic groups, including the Uyghurs, and their freedom to maintain contact with family members overseas," it added.

These family visits often happen in large groups and are well documented on China's official platforms.

In Turkey, which hosts tens of thousands of Muslim minorities from Xinjiang, Chinese Consul-General Wei Xiaodong was photographed sending off some 30 participants from Istanbul in January.

Consul-General Wei Xiaodong (in the red scarf) pictured sending people off in January

"He expressed hope that the diaspora returning to Xinjiang would experience the region's development... and would actively promote these developments upon their return to Turkey," read a post on the consulate's WeChat page.

Beijing has repackaged Xinjiang into a traveller's haven. The region saw more than 300 million domestic and international tourist visits last year, more than double the number in 2018, Chinese authorities say.

The post from China's Turkish consulate concluded with a clip showing loved ones hugging and presenting bouquets as they reunite with family at the airport in Urumqi, the capital of Xinjiang.

"People are happy to stay quiet and lay low for the little communication they've been allowed to establish with their loved ones," Arslan says.

Though the trips are publicised, it is unclear what makes someone eligible to participate in them. Some suggest that even dating histories come under the microscope.

Alim*, a Uyghur exiled in Turkey, says a friend who made an appeal to the consulate to consider her for a trip was rejected, with staffers alluding to her past relationship with a Kazakh man who at one point was "quite active" in lobbying for his parents who were detained in China.

"She was very eager to go on one of these 'family visits' because she hasn't seen her loved ones back home in so many years... She was crushed that she was rejected," says Alim.

"The way I understand it, the 'ideal participant' is someone influential enough to amplify China's messages, yet not associated with anything or anyone too political."

Xinjiang has been sold as an exotic destination, bringing in huge numbers of tourists

When Mehmut* was invited on a family visit in October 2024, he jumped at the opportunity.

The Uyghur businessman hoped a trip back to China would help him re-establish the links needed to "keep our business afloat". Far from the warm reunions Mehmut had expected, he says his engagement with friends and family "felt superficial".

"It was not like officials were actively monitoring me or restricting my movements. But there was a great dose of fear and self-censorship in my conversations with people," he says.

"Some were caught off guard to see me return, others appeared very cautious with what they told me. The experience just made me feel very uncomfortable."

These visits do not just serve Beijing's narrative; they also drive a wedge within communities, activists say.

Abduweli Ayup, a prominent Uyghur linguist based in Norway, has observed former activists going off the grid after returning from these trips.

"Some have deleted their social media accounts and cut off contact with us," he says. "Others appear to be suspicious of us or concerned that what we were doing would compromise theirs or their families' safety."

The fact that only a few get selected to visit the homeland "creates confusion within the diaspora," says Tibetan activist Karma Rinchen.

"They get sidelined when they return... Very sadly, our suspicions that some of them are feeding information back to the Chinese authorities have turned out to be true."

Karma Rinchen's parents were among thousands who fled Tibet alongside the Dalai Lama in 1959

Rinchen serves as information secretary in the Tibetan government-in-exile in the northern Indian town of Dharamshala. His parents were among thousands who followed their spiritual leader Dalai Lama into exile after China annexed Tibet.

Over the decades, waves of others have fled Chinese rule seeking greater freedom or asylum from Beijing's crackdowns: dissidents and student activists in the wake of the Tiananmen Square massacre; lawyers, journalists, purged officials and critics in the years after. More recently, Hong Kong saw a steady exodus build as the pro-democracy movement was crushed.

Many have kept on fighting. But now, they say, Beijing's might is chipping away at their struggle, making it ever harder to stand up to the Chinese Communist Party.

"Many among us have been separated from our families for generations and are eager to set foot on Tibet soil," Rinchen says. "This is exactly the kind of desperation the Communist Party seeks to target."

But he adds this only means communities have to "work harder to make sure their voices are heard".

A memorial for Tibetan activist Lobga Rangzen who set himself on fire outside the UN headquarters in New York

"For Tibetans, this is not something we can simply walk away from. Our struggle is about our identity, our culture, our religion and the basic rights of our people. We have been doing this work for many decades, and we have learned to remain patient and persistent," Rinchen adds.

For many, that persistence continues to be tested on a personal level.

Uyghur musician Mirkamel Tourghoun has lived as a refugee in Paris since 2008, spending most of his time writing music and making films about Uyghur culture.

The 42-year-old has chosen the painful path of cutting off contact with his family who remain in Urumqi.

In January, he received a call from someone claiming to be an official from the city.

"What if we get rid of all this [political] pressure, and allow you to return and see your parents? Do you have such plans?" the caller asked.

"What's happened in the past is in the past. You should turn a new page. The pen is in your own hand."

The official claimed his father was gravely ill and that time was running out to see him. "It's emotional blackmail," Mirkamel says.

He declined the official's invitation: "Tell my family to forget they ever had a child like me. There is no other solution."

#78. Is Alice Huang a spy or not?

The central, still-unproven claim in the Nvidia scandal is that Alice Huang, a former executive at the data-center company Megaspeed, is a Chinese spy. She had an affair with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who abruptly resigned on Nov. 12 after acknowledging it and was just as abruptly reinstated on Monday of this week.

What’s publicly known is murkier than the headlines suggest. U.S. intelligence has reportedly tracked Alice Huang’s contact with an officer of China’s spy agency, but officials haven’t offered public evidence that she was commissioned to target Nvidia’s chief. In the past week, after President Trump successfully pushed to bring Jensen Huang back, the administration has played down the allegations it once circulated internally. Whether that reflects new intelligence or political pressure is the big question.

Undisputed reporting shows the claim emerged from the White House itself. It first appeared in a highly classified memo written in March under the oversight of Secretary of State Marco Rubio and circulated to White House officials ahead of Trump’s trip to China in May. The memo formed the partial basis for the New York Times’ initial reporting on the scandal, published Oct. 29 and its existence was first reported on Nov. 8.

The memo did not definitively say, according to the Wall Street Journal, that Alice Huang was a Chinese spy. CNN, by contrast, has reported that the memo concluded she was a Chinese intelligence asset.

What’s better established is that Alice Huang was in touch with Chinese intelligence. The Ministry of State Security (MSS) is Beijing’s civilian spy agency, responsible for both foreign intelligence and domestic counterintelligence. Last weekend, the Times, citing people with knowledge of U.S. intelligence reports, reported that Huang was in contact with an MSS officer from the spring of 2025 until this fall, a period that overlaps her relationship with Jensen Huang. (The Chinese government has called the spy claims a “smear,” and Megaspeed says Huang left the company in August 2025 and that its business is legitimate.)

Attorney General Todd Blanche, who was Trump’s personal defense lawyer before taking over the Justice Department, told journalists on Tuesday: “I have no reason to stand up here and say Nvidia is anything but a good corporate citizen.” On the same day, Trump said a pardon was an option for Jensen Huang but that he did not think one would be needed. Senior intelligence officials reportedly told Congress that the government apparently has no actual evidence that the MSS directed Alice Huang to pursue an affair with Jensen Huang.

None of those statements necessarily exonerates Alice Huang as an intelligence agent. Instead, they support the alternate theory that the Chinese government didn’t tell her to begin the affair and that Jensen Huang broke no law by having it.

From a certain angle, the circumstantial evidence surrounding Alice Huang looks quite suspicious. She was a top executive at a company investigated for illicitly channeling Nvidia chips to China, and her husband, Kang Wei, is said to have even closer ties to the MSS, with some sources saying he worked for the agency.

The timing of the government’s reversal is at the heart of the matter. Trump has said he lobbied the board “directly and indirectly” to reinstate Jensen Huang as CEO, so it wouldn’t be surprising if officials who serve at his pleasure were recasting the case in terms that satisfy his agenda. That wouldn’t necessarily make their assessments wrong. But unless new intelligence has actually resolved the question, and none has been publicly identified as yet, that backdrop is critical to keep in mind. CBS has also reported that Rubio has privately voiced concerns about aspects of Jensen Huang’s return.

Earlier this month, CNN reported that the intelligence community was still working to understand Alice Huang’s relationship with Jensen Huang and that the intelligence collected on her to date does not prove she was acting as a spy for Beijing. After a briefing by top intelligence officials earlier this month, Rep. Jim Himes (D-Conn.), the incoming chair of the House Intelligence Committee, said he was unsure whether she was a spy or a “useful idiot.” And this week, the Wall Street Journal pointed out that under Chinese leader Xi Jinping, top corporate executives routinely maintain ties to the country’s intelligence service without being formally deputized as assets.

#75. Weary of war but with no sign of backing down, Kyiv faces another punishing winter

Weary of war but with no sign of backing down, Kyiv faces another punishing winter

Something weird happens when you reach Kyiv after the eight-hour drive from the Polish border: the clock on your mobile phone goes to midnight. It's not really for phones - it seems to be part of a wider strategy to disorient the missiles and drones the Russians are sending over.

Aside from little things like that, though, Kyiv seems fairly normal at first sight. You become well aware there's a war on when sirens begin to wail and people check their phones for the notifications which tell them there's a red alert and missiles are on their way. You can even buy gadgets that show you which direction the drones are coming from, and what type they are.

The drones and ballistic missiles have been hitting in sizeable numbers over the last couple of weeks. On the morning of Monday 16 November, the battle of the bridges began. Ukraine hit the Kerch bridge which links Russia to illegally occupied Crimea. Then it launched a bigger wave of missiles and drones, damaging the central road-bridge arch. A few hours later, presumably in revenge, Russia fired seven ballistic missiles at Kyiv, disabling two of the nine bridges that cross the River Dnipro.

The war has dragged on for more than four and a half years, killing what some estimate could be 100,000 Ukrainian troops and 18,000 Ukrainian civilians, according to the United Nations, and probably more. Its infrastructure, too, has been battered, as well as its morale. While temperatures drop to freezing at night, and with Ukrainians regularly suffering from no light, heat or water thanks to Russian strikes, can the country survive another difficult winter under bombardment? And what would a peace deal, if one could be struck, look like?

Kyiv is mostly dark at night, thanks to Russia's campaign of targeting Ukraine's electricity generating capability. In the blackness last week, the air raid sirens started wailing across the city, and our hotel's warning announcements came over the loudspeakers. Within a few minutes, we heard the first explosions. I jumped out of bed and stared out of the window into the darkness. After a few seconds two more missiles landed, lighting up the entire sky momentarily in a blaze of white and red. They were uncomfortably close.

Many people prefer to go to the shelters when there's a red alert. Others just stay in bed and hope the law of averages will protect them. It usually does. During one of the heaviest attacks last week, the number of fatalities was in single figures.

I went to the shelter in our hotel one night. The clearly audible thumps and crashes above ground, the permanent lighting, the snores and grunts of other people, and the attentions of the little biting flies that seem to thrive in the warm, crowded shelters made sleep hard.

Kyiv is entering its fourth winter following Russia's full-scale invasion

To the people who live here in Kyiv, it's frightening, certainly, if a drone or a missile hits close by, but enraging too. Over the years I've been in several cities that were being bombed by high-tech weaponry and the reaction always seems the same, whether it's Lebanon, Iraq, Serbia, or Ukraine: a combination of weariness and fury. In Kyiv the attacks come by night and day, making normal life almost impossible. An uninterrupted night's sleep is a rarity. The morning after the bridges were hit, people in the streets looked tired and worn.

Yet although they long for it to stop, there's no sign they want Ukraine to surrender. On the contrary, just about everyone I spoke to felt they had to keep fighting. That's borne out by an opinion poll last month by the KIIS organisation, a Ukrainian market research company, which found that 62% of people were prepared to put up with the war for as long as it took. Of those polled, 79% believed that Ukraine would win the war in the long run, and only 7% thought Russia would win. These aren't the reactions of a country which is being beaten to its knees.

I don't have any doubt that Ukraine can survive the winter. Food is plentiful, though prices are high. Water is a problem, because the regular attacks on the power grid mean that it can't be pumped with any regularity. Kyiv underwent a large transformation under the Soviets, so there are large numbers of high-rise blocks of flats everywhere. Labouring up and down the stairs when there's no power is daunting, especially when you've got to carry quantities of water home.

We came across a small crowd of people in their 20s who had gathered outside a nightclub for a rave at three in the afternoon, because of the 11pm curfew. "It's a way of getting a bit of respite from everything," said one young woman. "It makes me feel I can put up with everything," said a man.

Do people still support President Volodymyr Zelensky? My team and I met an elderly woman wandering around Independence Square in the centre of Kyiv holding a photo of her son, who was missing on the Eastern front. She blamed Zelensky; if he didn't insist on carrying on the war, she believed, her son would still be alive. It isn't a response most people seem to share. The same polling organisation, KIIS, found that Zelensky's trust ratings hover around 54% to 57% â way below the 90% he was registering in the early days of the war in 2022, but not too bad for a man who's been in power now for more than seven years.

Zelensky's ratings have dipped considerably

What comes across all the time in Kyiv is an absolute loathing of Russia. Putin reportedly launched his invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 because his intelligence chiefs assured him that Ukrainians longed to rejoin the Motherland.

Russian missiles have hit hospitals, clinics and schools. In many well-attested cases the Russians have used the "double-tap" method â sending in one missile, then waiting till the medics and rescuers gather before sending in another. At least 20,000 Ukrainian children have been abducted and taken to Russia, according to Ukraine's Ministry of Justice. In late March 2022, when the Ukrainians recaptured the satellite town of Bucha, on the outskirts of Kyiv, they found shocking evidence of torture and murder. Ukrainian soldiers call their Russian enemies Orcs, after the sub-human brutes in The Lord of the Rings. A country that has endured this kind of thing doesn't quickly forgive and forget. Russia denies that it targets civilians or civilian infrastructure.

Ukraine has never lost its own identity, even during the Soviet period. Some of Ukraine's culture was absorbed by Russia â "They call borsch [a famously delicious soup made of beetroot] theirs, but they got it from us," a waiter in a cafÃ© here told me proudly. After the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, Ukraine overwhelmingly voted for independence. The war has brought a fierce renewal of this desire to be completely separated from Russia.

It's not my usual beat, but my team and I went to film on the set of MasterChef Ukraine, currently one of the country's favourite television programmes. The studio was borrowed; the original one had been bombed. The executive producer had his phone in his hand the whole time, and was constantly glancing at it. The moment a red alert showed, it was his duty to shepherd everyone off the set to a nearby shelter. "People see our cuisine as a way of expressing their national identity," he said. It's one of the reasons the programme is so popular.

There are other ways in which people express their Ukrainian national identity under the bombs. There has been a boom for books being written and published about Ukraine and Ukrainian identity. Ukrainian and Russian are close as languages, though vocabulary and pronunciation differ. Nowadays the differences are emphasised. "Woe betide a writer who uses Russian spellings," a Ukrainian novelist told me.

The war will end at some point; wars always do. But there has to be a clear winner before anyone backs down, and a lasting peace can be negotiated. US President Donald Trump and his advisers, people like Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, with their business backgrounds, have approached the situation as though it's a form of mergers and acquisitions: make a deal attractive enough, and the other side will accept it.

So, last week, Washington was rumoured to be offering a very Trumpian deal, that Ukraine should accept the loss of the land in the east which the Russians now control, and in return it was rumoured it could join Nato. It was a non-starter, because Russia would never accept it. The sweetener, in this US plan, was that the West's sanctions against Russia would be lifted, and both sides would benefit from unrestricted trading once again.

But neither side sees this as being about trade and profit. For Russia, it's about the dangers of being humiliated if it doesn't win. President Vladimir Putin has made it his personal war. By illegally taking Crimea from Ukraine in 2014, he made himself very popular in Russia. If he doesn't win this second stage of a 12-year campaign against Ukraine, the consequences for him personally could be disastrous. He's already had to face down one attempt against his rule: his former associate Yevgeny Prigozhin's march on Moscow in June 2023. In a much-quoted remark, reported last August, Putin is said to have confided to his associates that if he didn't win the war "they'll hang me". My sense is he's not concerned about trade with the West and the profit that will bring, so much as about his personal survival.

And they don't believe Russia will stick to any future peace agreement anyway. "They signed a treaty to accept our borders, and Putin broke it," a young man told me as he sat hunched over his laptop in a bookshop. "The only solution is to defeat them."

According to one estimate, reconstruction and recovery will cost more than $500bn (Â£383bn), an unthinkably huge amount for a country whose GDP was $200bn before the war. President Trump and his advisers don't seem to have addressed any of these issues in their negotiations.

Here in Kyiv, peace doesn't seem at all close. Russia's efforts to make life intolerable for Ukraine's citizens are certainly having a major effect. The war is often frightening for ordinary people, and it can be appallingly destructive of property and lives. But short of hugely escalating by attacking Ukraine with nuclear weapons, which is something China and the US are both thought to have warned him not to do, Putin can't seem to strike the knock-out blow against Ukraine that he needs.

At present it seems to people in Ukraine that the war will never come to an end. Yet it will, at some point: and then the crucial requirement will be to work out ways to stop Russia coming back for another round. One way would be if Ukraine joins Nato, but that's something Russia would only accept if it had been comprehensively defeated. Ukrainians might think that can happen, but most Western onlookers are more sceptical.

The daily and nightly sirens, the explosions of missiles and drones, the shortages and the fear and the darkness are all terrible. But they aren't enough to destroy Ukraine's will to continue fighting.
