#22. Falkland Islanders face ‘devastating consequences’ if Argentina blocks oil project, says UK

Countries clash over Sea Lion project as Hamburg tribunal risks deepening sovereignty dispute

The people of the Falkland Islands face “devastating consequences” if Argentina is successful in blocking development of its offshore oilfield, the attorney general for England and Wales has told an international tribunal.

Ellie Reeves, representing the British government at a hearing of the international tribunal for the law of the sea (Itlos), said billions of pounds vital to the future of the British overseas territory would be lost.

Reeves accused Argentina of surreptitiously seeking to pursue its longstanding sovereignty claims over the south Atlantic islands through an application to the tribunal, which is sitting over two days in Hamburg.

Argentina is seeking a provisional ruling suspending the drilling for oil before further arbitration over the future of the project, which it claims poses an environmental risk.

Reeves told the tribunal: “In reality, what Argentina opposes is hydrocarbon activities for the economic benefit of the Falkland Islanders. What it wants is to take control of those resources for itself.”

Argentina claims Britain’s approval of the Sea Lion project is in breach of a 1976 UN resolution calling on both parties to desist from unilateral action on the islands until the sovereignty dispute is settled.

Buenos Aires wants to force a commitment from the UK that it will “stop the drilling”, in order to “preserve the present situation” before a settlement over the oilfield.

After raising Argentina’s “armed invasion” of the islands in 1982, which she told the tribunal led to “loss of life and military occupation”, Reeves said the Falkland Islanders had a right to exploit their own natural resources, adding that a tribunal that ruled over the law of the seas had no place in settling a sovereignty dispute.

Reeves said that suspension of the drilling, which is due to start in March next year, would kill off the Sea Lion project.

“The provisional measures sought by Argentina would prevent the people of the Falkland Islands from developing their natural resources as they are entitled to do so, pursuant to their fundamental right to self-determination,” she said.

“The measures sought would have devastating consequences for the Falklands people. They risk losing billions of pounds in money, which is essential for the islands’ economic development.”

Sir Michael Wood KC, who also spoke on behalf of the UK, said the tribunal could lose member states should it overreach by issuing “provisional” measures that should be used only in exceptional circumstances.

Asked whether the UK would abide by the tribunal’s ruling, Reeves told the Guardian that she respected the institution but that she would not “get ahead” of the decisions.

Speaking earlier on Tuesday, Mario Oyarzábal, chief legal adviser to Argentina’s ministry of foreign affairs, had told the tribunal that the 3,600 inhabitants of the Falkland Islands, had no right to the resources around the south Atlantic archipelago.

Oyarzábal said: “[Argentina] respects the interests of the inhabitants of the islands and their way of life as enshrined in the Argentine constitution … What these British citizens do not have is a right to decide to which state the islands belong …

“These resources belong to the Argentine people. Some 46 million of them including the 1,247 inhabitants born on the islands whose interests will be duly taken into account.”

He said Argentina was ready to negotiate “all aspects of the question” of the Falkland Islands, which are known as the Malvinas in Argentina, “including the present aggravation of the dispute caused by the UK”.

He accused the UK of refusing to enter talks over the islands while pushing forward on the exploitation of its natural resources in a way that would entrench the status quo.

Oyarzábal claimed that the oil money from the drilling would “finance the very military base and presence that sustains the UK’s de facto control of the territory”.

Argentina’s case is that provisional measures to suspend the drilling are urgent. The Sea Lion project in the North Falkland basin, led by the Israeli firm Navitas and its British partner, Rockhopper, will start drilling in March and oil will be extracted from 2028 and continue for 35 years. It has the potential to double the islands’ GDP per capita to a cumulative uplift of £8bn over the lifetime of the project. The UK government asked why an application to Itlos had only been made in recent months amid increasingly bellicose rhetoric.

Argentina further claims that it has rights as the “coastal state” to the Falklands. The UK’s lawyers said this claim highlighted that Argentina’s application was “at heart” about sovereignty.

The Sea Lion project has been condemned as unlawful by Argentina’s president, Javier Milei. The tribunal was told by Argentinian lawyers that the permissions given by the UK government for drilling was the “unilateral conduct” of a “colonial power”.

Oyarzábal added: “In the event of a blowout or spill, it is Argentina that would bear its consequences and be called upon to respond, not a state that is 12,700km away.”

UK lawyers responded that the risk of such an incident was low and that the companies involved had mitigated against it.

Argentina invaded the Falklands in 1982 at the cost of the lives of 649 Argentinian military personnel, 255 British military personnel and three Falkland Islanders. The hearing is the first time that substantive matters relating to the islands have been litigated at an international tribunal. In a show of solidarity, Princess Anne arrived in Port Stanley on Monday. The hearing continues on Wednesday.

#18. ANC faces bruising defeat in South African local elections with 80% of ballots counted

Former liberation movement’s share of national vote almost 14 percentage points below 2021 result

The African National Congress is on course to suffer a bruising defeat in South Africa’s local elections, with the once dominant party of Nelson Mandela falling to under a third of the vote nationally with more than 80% of ballots counted.

Wednesday’s fragmented vote marks another stage in the decline of the ANC, which has led national governments since the end of white minority apartheid rule in 1994. High unemployment, crime, corruption and poor local service delivery have all contributed to voters turning away from the party.

The former liberation movement is projected to lose its status as the biggest party in the two most populous provinces, Gauteng and KwaZulu-Natal, as parties began coalition negotiations behind closed doors. The final results are expected to be announced on Sunday.

The ANC was on 31.8% of the vote nationally, almost 14 percentage points below its 2021 local election result. The centre-right, white-led Democratic Alliance (DA) had 24.1% of the vote, while the uMkhonto weSizwe (MK) party of the former ANC president Jacob Zuma was on 9.3%.

Turnout also rose in Wednesday’s poll. The election commission estimated that 54.5% of registered voters cast their ballot, up from 46% five years ago.

The final shape of coalitions in some of the country’s biggest cities remained unclear with the final votes still being counted. Senior figures in key parties said they would talk with almost anyone, while keeping negotiating strategies under wraps.

In Johannesburg, South Africa’s commercial capital and biggest city, with 4.8 million people, voters have had to contend with regular water and power cuts, uncollected rubbish and potholes. The municipality, which has seen 10 changes of mayor in the last decade amid unstable coalitions, has admitted to a 220bn rand (£9.9bn) infrastructure spending backlog.

With just over half of votes counted in Johannesburg, the DA was leading with 40%, having run an energetic campaign under Helen Zille, a 75-year-old former Cape Town mayor. The ANC, which leads the current coalition, was in second place on 17.5%. ActionSA, a rightwing party led by Herman Mashaba, was on 15.5%. Mashaba has pledged never to work with Zille, whom he calls “racist”, something she vehemently denies.

Many of the ANC’s township strongholds have yet to report full results. The Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR), a respected thinktank, has projected the DA will finish with 34%, the ANC 20% and ActionSA 16.8%.

Coalitions in Gauteng’s three city municipalities are often negotiated together. The DA is leading in all three.

Founded in 2023, it mixes socially conservative Zulu nationalism with pledges to redistribute land and wealth from white to black South Africans.

With half of votes counted in eThekwini municipality, which contains Durban, the MK had 41%, meaning other parties could form a coalition to keep it out of power. Meanwhile, the DA is projected to maintain its majority in Cape Town, despite growing middle-class anger over rising housing costs.

#16. US House blocks bid to impeach Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth, for now

Watch: Republicans vote to block Hegseth impeachment from reaching full vote

An effort to impeach US Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth, accusing him of waging war on Iran without congressional authorisation, has failed on Capitol Hill.

A majority of the House of Representatives agreed to refer impeachment articles to a committee - essentially killing the effort introduced by dissident Republican congressman Thomas Massie of Kentucky - for now.

In his first news briefing from the Pentagon in six months, Hegseth called the impeachment effort a "farce" and "sabotage".

The bid to find the cabinet secretary guilty of misconduct and ultimately remove him from office was seen as a long shot as it could have required a number of other Republicans to defy President Donald Trump.

Massie was the only Republican to vote against moving the resolution to the Republican-led House Judiciary Committee on Tuesday.

His effort failed in a 211-204 vote, with more than a dozen members, including 11 Democrats, missing the vote.

The move to send the measure to the panel effectively kills the effort, as there is only a short period of time left in this Congress before Democrats take control in January.

President Trump celebrated the vote in a post on Truth Social, saying the effort "has miserably FAILED".

"Again, Victories in Venezuela and Iran, the Best Recruitment Numbers in the History of the Military (Long waiting lists to get in!), and Spirit like rarely seen before, led to the EXONERATION of a Great American Patriot," Trump said, referring to Hegseth.

After the vote, Massie said he "wasn't surprised" but "was disappointed", according to BBC's US media partner CBS.

"My diagnosis of Congress is these people know what to do," he added. "They just can't bring themselves to do it, even when it would benefit them politically."

Massie had previously acknowledged his impeachment bid may not succeed immediately, but argued it could help push Trump to replace Hegseth before Democrats can launch their own impeachment process.

Hegseth: Impeachment effort is "farce" and "stunt"

The Kentucky congressman also said that removing Hegseth now would give the Senate time to approve a replacement while Republicans were still in power.

Impeachment resolutions can be carried past the end of a congressional term, meaning Democrats would be able to take it up next year.

The Democrats will take control of both chambers of Congress in January following a successful midterm election that allowed them control of both chambers.

House Speaker Mike Johnson, a Republican, had predicted earlier in the day that the impeachment bid would fail.

In the eight articles of impeachment formally introduced in the House on Monday, Massie said Hegseth was "breaking the law and must be held accountable" for fighting a war in Iran for more than 90 days without lawmakers' approval.

The impeachment articles also accused Hegseth of overseeing "extrajudicial killings" in the US campaign against boats alleged to be carrying drugs in the Pacific Ocean and Caribbean Sea.

At a news conference on Tuesday, Hegseth called efforts to impeach him a political stunt by a Trump administration opponent who was "disgruntled and defeated".

Hegseth also argued Massie's resolution endangered national security, because it communicated to US adversaries "that the United States will actually punish its own warriors and its own civilian leadership for successfully executing lawful military operations".

"This is not patriotism. This is sabotage," he said.

He echoed some statements Trump had made during a surprise appearance at the White House briefing room on Monday, including touting a rise in military recruitment levels.

But Hegseth said Massie was motivated by policy differences and "not high crimes, not misdemeanours, not treason, not bribery, not the kinds of offences the founders intended when they wrote the impeachment clause".

Massie, who lost re-election earlier this year to a Trump-backed opponent, has been critical of Trump and the war in Iran since it began in February.

He had already incurred the president's ire for demanding the Trump administration release files on deceased sex offender Jeffrey Epstein.

While Hegseth has survived politically for now, many lawmakers - including some Republicans - have questioned how long he can last in the administration.

Texas Senator Ted Cruz, a Republican, said on Tuesday he expected Hegseth to resign or be fired by Trump.

#14. US Army soldier suspected of spying for Russia arrested in UK

The BBC understands the man was based at Imjin Barracks in Gloucestershire

A soldier in the US Army has been arrested on suspicion of spying for Russia, British counter-terror police have said.

The 41-year-old senior serviceman was arrested near Gloucester on Tuesday.

Counter Terrorism Policing London said the man had been arrested under section one and section three of the National Security Act 2023.

Section one of the Act relates to obtaining or disclosing protected information, while section three relates to assisting a foreign intelligence service.

As part of the investigation, officers are carrying out searches at two locations in Gloucestershire.

The BBC understands the soldier was based at Imjin Barracks in Innsworth, Gloucestershire. The barracks serve as the headquarters of Nato's Allied Rapid Reaction Corps (ARRC), a strategic operational base for the alliance.

Counter Terrorism Policing said it was not linked to any other ongoing investigations.

FBI director Kash Patel said in a statement: "This important arrest involves allegations of espionage, which is of the utmost concern to the United States and our allies."

He added: "While this investigation is continuing, I want to send a clear message â this FBI has no tolerance for anyone who breaks their oath to the United States and whose actions endanger our national security and that of our partners."

Deputy Assistant Commissioner Vicki Evans, from Counter Terrorism Policing London, described the arrest as "significant".

"The allegations are serious and our investigation is very much ongoing," she said.

The unnamed soldier is currently in custody at a London police station.

#13. Kenyan doomsday sect leader jailed for 200 years for murder of 190 children

Paul Mackenzie convicted over deaths of children and one adult after followers were ordered to starve themselves and their families

The leader of a Kenyan doomsday sect has been sentenced to 200 years in prison for the murders of 190 children and one adult in one of the world’s worst cult disasters in recent history.

The sentence, comprising 100 years for murder by starvation and 100 years by direct homicide, came two weeks after the court in Kenya’s coastal city of Mombasa found the cult leader guilty of the crimes.

Prosecutors said Paul Mackenzie and his Good News International church ran ​a Christian doomsday sect whose members were ordered to starve themselves and their children to death to go to heaven before the world ended.

His accomplices threatened and beat victims to ensure they followed his orders. The emaciated bodies were found scattered in shallow graves and dumped in latrines. Some had been buried alive.

Mackenzie was arrested in 2023 after police received a tipoff, and weeks later authorities began uncovering bodies in shallow graves in the Shakahola forest near the coast in south-eastern Kenya.

In total, more than 400 bodies have been recovered. Many were severely decomposed and only 26 could be identified.

Mackenzie denied murder charges, saying he did not force the victims to starve, but the court rejected that argument.

The Shakahola tragedy has gripped Kenya since the discovery of mass graves in the forest more than three years ago.

It raised questions about how Mackenzie was able to continue operating despite being on the radar of the authorities since 2017, when police searched his premises.

In 2019, authorities shut down Mackenzie’s church and he relocated ‌to the Shakahola forest, police said.

The case has also led to calls for tougher oversight of churches and other religious organisations in a country where religious faith plays a prominent role in public life.

Mackenzie, a former taxi driver, forbade cult members from sending their children to school and from going to hospital when they were ill, describing such institutions as satanic.

Cult members lived in makeshift homes of polythene sheeting and thatch that Mackenzie divided into areas with biblical names such as Jerusalem and Judea.

The plight of his followers began ​to emerge in March 2023, when a man told ​police his brother and wife had starved their children ​to death in the forest on Mackenzie’s orders.

Mackenzie was arrested but released on bail, after which relatives said he brought forward the date ​on which he told his followers the ‌world would end. Police rearrested him and ​started combing through the forest more systematically and began exhuming mass graves.

Last month’s judgment covered 191 deaths while investigations into more than 200 other deaths are continuing.

Justice Diana Kavedza-Mochache said in a hearing lasting more than eight hours that in some cases children were isolated and denied food while their parents watched.

Mackenzie faces other charges over the forest deaths, including those of terrorism, which he has denied.

Prosecutors said that while he was in detention he also directed the deaths of 52 people in Kwa Binzaro village, about 18 miles from Shakahola. Mackenzie has pleaded not guilty in that case.

#9. Three Iran-backed suspects plotted attacks including RAF Fairford incident, US says

The US removed all its bombers from the UK's RAF Fairford base last month after a major incident in September that police described as "suspicious"

The US Department of Justice (DOJ) has announced charges against three Iran-backed operatives that it alleges were involved in a string of plots in the US and Europe.

The DOJ said Iranian agents most recently "orchestrated a terrorist plot at an air force base in the United Kingdom", saying they were involved in the major incident in September near RAF Fairford in the UK, where US armed forces were stationed.

One man, a dual Iranian-Iraqi national, is accused of communicating with contacts in the UK from a prison in New York where he is awaiting trial on previous charges. The other two are accused of doing the same from Iran.

Iran has strongly denied any involvement in the RAF Fairford incident, which came to light when suspicious vans were reported near the base. Seven men have been arrested in the UK over the incident.

Iranian Mohsen Mohajeri, Iraqi national Mohammad Ali al-Hassani and dual Iranian-Iraqi citizen Mohammad Baqer Saad Dawood al-Saadi were charged in an indictment unsealed on Tuesday, the DOJ said.

It accuses the trio of planning attacks in Europe that were carried out under the name of a purportedly new group with the pseudonym Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamiya (The Islamic Movement of the People of the Right Hand - HAYI).

"Of the 18 terrorist attacks in the spring of 2026 for which HAYI claimed responsibility in Europe, seven took place in the UK," the DOJ statement said. It added that the attacks involved "arsons, explosives, and in one instance, a near-fatal stabbing in London of two Jewish men, including a dual US-British citizen".

Two Jewish men were stabbed in Golders Green, north London, in April. A suspect is due to stand trial next year.

"This eight-count indictment shows the determination of Iran to commit terrorist attacks in Europe and the US," FBI Director Kash Patel said.

He added that one of the three "allegedly sent coded messages to assist in future acts of violence" from detention.

Patel said the accused were involved in nearly 20 attempted attacks in support of Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) and the Iran-backed Iraqi militia Kataib Hezbollah.

Armed police officers were posted outside the RAF Fairford base following the incident in September

A major incident was declared on 27 September near the UK's RAF Fairford base, in the Cotswolds in Gloucestershire, after three suspicious vans were seen near the airbase.

No explosive materials were found in the vans, but a quantity of petrol was seized.

The DOJ said that in August, al-Hassani "allegedly directed and paid associates" in the UK to "conduct preparatory steps and arrange logistics for the RAF Fairford terrorist plot".

On 22 August, al-Hassani paid one individual to "perform pre-operational surveillance of RAF Fairford's perimeter", the department said.

In September, al-Hassani paid several people to help him prepare for the plot, the DOJ also alleged.

According to its statement, this included "procuring vans and barrels of fuel, researching the purchase of chemical accelerants, including large quantities of potassium chloride, and purchasing metallic objects that could be used as explosive shrapnel".

The suspects then gave the vans an appearance of being used by a fuel delivery business and a swimming pool supply business, the DOJ stated.

#7. How African philosophies inspired a new approach to staging film festivals in Manchester

See My World, an event highlighting the pan-African thought via film, poetry and dance, builds on momentum sparked by local activists and creatives

African philosophies including exploring how lessons from the past can shape the present and near future have inspired a film festival coming to Manchester.

See My World, a day dedicated to independent creators from across the African diaspora in the north of England, will take place at the Contact theatre on 21 November.

The festival is part of See My World’s year-round programme showcasing pan-African arts, culture and events.

It reflects the determination among creatives and activists in Manchester to build on the legacy of the 1945 Pan-African Congress in Manchester – which connected luminaries of postwar independence movements with a shared vision of liberation – after last year’s 80th anniversary commemorations.

See My World’s CEO and artistic director, Tunde Adekoya, said this month’s film festival was taking inspiration from across the spectrum of pan-African thought, including the works of Kenyan writers John Mbiti, an Anglican theologian who died in 2019, and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o, the author of the groundbreaking 1986 book Decolonising the Mind who died last year.

Both writers rejected the colonial conceit that African thought was inferior, stressed the importance of community and saw reclaiming African ways of knowing as essential to a people’s self-understanding.

While Mbiti sought common ground between African religions and Christianity, Ngũgĩ was a Marxist. He argued colonialism was motivated by cultural and economic conquest, and that European powers used language as their sharpest instrument. Ngũgĩ famously abandoned English to write in Gikuyu, a Bantu language mainly spoken in Kenya and criticised the role missionary Christianity played in colonial rule.

Adekoya said: “It’s a day dedicated to pan-African and global majority films from the north of England with exclusive premieres of documentaries we’ve been working on, which weave together expressions of liberation through art forms like poetry and dance.

Starting with the Guardian’s own history, Guardian journalists explore the legacies of enslavement and reparative justice around the world

“The whole idea is that it’s like a playground in search of strategies of liberation, using the festival as a way to explore different philosophies.

“John Mbiti explores the philosophy and spiritual understandings of time through the concept of Sasa na Zamani – in which the past informs everything that happens moving forward. That reflects how we constantly go back to pan-African histories to inform how we progress.”

Film-makers have been invited to submit films lasting up to 30 minutes from across genres, with no screening fees charged.

As well as submissions, the festival will feature documentaries produced by See My World. They include Homage, a “cinematic love letter to dance” that traces a culture through present-day portraits illustrating dance artists and archival material from Manchester, telling a story of joy, loss, migration, rhythm and resistance. Meanwhile, Poetic Justice explores how pan-African thought and Manchester’s poetry scene speak across decades.

Adekoya added: “The idea is that we would go against the norm of how film festivals are, because when we think about decolonisation, we think about how structures have been created to alienate and exclude communities.

“So we thought our film festival would look more at how we make film accessible and how stories that deserve to be seen get seen. Throughout the day we will have different activities, from exhibitions to explorations of our performances, discussions, Q&As, insights into directors’ processes, and workshops.

“We’ve been looking for literature and concepts that inform us and give us an understanding of who we truly are. And as works from thinkers like John Mbiti have come into our sphere, they have really emboldened us to think differently about our place in the world and what we do.”

See My World film festival runs from 2pm to 11pm on Saturday 21 November at Contact theatre, Oxford Road, Manchester.

Ayman Gamal, a resident of Kafr Girza in al-Ayyat where the crash occurred, said people rushed to the scene, pulled out survivors and called ambulances.
