#124. AI datacentres plan on-site power stations on huge scale despite climate risks, research reveals

181GW of gas-fired capacity proposed for sites in US and Europe because of delays in connecting to national grids, says report

Developers of AI datacentres plan to build on-site climate-destroying gas power stations on a huge scale to meet their energy demands, research reveals.

Datacentres planned across the US, the UK and mainland Europe have proposals to build fossil fuel plants on-site to create 181GW of gas-fired capacity because of delays to connect to national grids, according to the findings. This is almost four times the total peak electricity demand of Great Britain.

The research by BloombergNEF says this “speed to power” will – if the planned rollout happens – emit about 345m metric tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent a year in the US, where 90% of on-site gas-fired power stations are planned. This is equivalent to annual greenhouse gas emissions from about 72.8m fossil fuel cars.

The report was published on Wednesday as global leaders gathered in Turkey for the 31st UN climate summit, where increasing electrification – ideally from clean sources – is a central part of discussions. However, a number of key European leaders, including from France and Germany, will not be at Cop31, instead heading to Dublin where EU leaders will hold their first dedicated AI summit on Friday.

Mark Daly, the head of datacentres and emerging tech at BloombergNEF, said: “The extraordinary economics around AI are making speed to power one of the most important priorities for defining constraints on datacentre developers.

“The climate outcome will depend not only on how much electricity these facilities use, but on how quickly grids can connect them and whether power systems add cleaner generation alongside that demand.”

By 2035, the electricity demand of AI datacentres was predicted to almost quadruple to 2,354 terawatt-hours – equivalent to 7% of global electricity demand – the report said.

AI datacentres use a massive amount of electricity to power servers and cool equipment, and to run facility systems.

Daly said while the developers of the future datacentres claimed their gas-fired power supplies would be temporary while they waited for grid connections, once they were online, the economics could encourage them to continue generating energy, creating a longer-term fossil-fuel lock-in risk.

The report said ensuring new datacentres were connected to the grid was the more sustainable option. Faster grid connections were crucial to limiting emissions impacts and locking in fossil fuel risk, it said.

UK planned datacentres made more than 100 inquiries in 2024-5 to connect to gas as a result of delays in securing access to the electricity grid, according to Future Energy Networks, the representative body for the UK gas networks.

#121. Hundreds of millions at risk from Chinese shopping app malware

It is one of China’s most popular shopping apps, selling clothing, groceries and just about everything else under the sun to more than 750 million users a month.

But according to cybersecurity researchers, it can also bypass users’ cell phone security to monitor activities on other apps, check notifications, read private messages and change settings.

And once installed, it’s tough to remove.

In a detailed investigation, CNN spoke to half a dozen cybersecurity teams from Asia, Europe and the United States — as well as multiple former and current Pinduoduo employees — after receiving a tipoff.

Multiple experts identified the presence of malware on the Pinduoduo app that exploited vulnerabilities in Android operating systems. Company insiders said the exploits were utilized to spy on users and competitors, allegedly to boost sales.

“We haven’t seen a mainstream app like this trying to escalate their privileges to gain access to things that they’re not supposed to gain access to,” said Mikko Hyppönen, chief research officer at WithSecure, a Finnish cybersecurity firm.

“This is highly unusual, and it is pretty damning for Pinduoduo.”

Malware, short for malicious software, refers to any software developed to steal data or interfere with computer systems and mobile devices.

Evidence of sophisticated malware in the Pinduoduo app comes amid intense scrutiny of Chinese-developed apps like TikTok over concerns about data security.

Some American lawmakers are pushing for a national ban on the popular short-video app, whose CEO Shou Chew was grilled by Congress for five hours last week about its relations with the Chinese government.

The revelations are also likely to draw more attention to Pinduoduo’s international sister app, Temu, which is topping US download charts and fast expanding in other Western markets. Both are owned by Nasdaq-listed PDD, a multinational company with roots in China.

While Temu has not been implicated, Pinduoduo’s alleged actions risk casting a shadow over its sister app’s global expansion.

There is no evidence that Pinduoduo has handed data to the Chinese government. But as Beijing enjoys significant leverage over businesses under its jurisdiction, there are concerns from US lawmakers that any company operating in China could be forced to cooperate with a broad range of security activities.

The findings follow Google’s suspension of Pinduoduo from its Play Store in March, citing malware identified in versions of the app.

An ensuing report from Bloomberg said a Russian cybersecurity firm had also identified potential malware in the app.

Pinduoduo has previously rejected “the speculation and accusation that Pinduoduo app is malicious.”

CNN has contacted PDD multiple times over email and phone for comment, but has not received a response.

Pinduoduo, which boasts a user base that accounts for three quarters of China’s online population and a market value three times that of eBay (EBAY), wasn’t always an online shopping behemoth.

Founded in 2015 in Shanghai by Colin Huang, a former Google employee, the startup was fighting to establish itself in a market long dominated by e-commerce stalwarts Alibaba (BABA) and JD.com (JD).

It succeeded by offering steep discounts on friends-and-family group buying orders and focusing on lower-income rural areas.

Pinduoduo posted triple digit growth in monthly users until the end of 2018, the year it listed in New York. By the middle of 2020, though, the increase in monthly users had slowed to around 50% and would continue to decline, according to its earnings reports.

It was in 2020, according to a current Pinduoduo employee, that the company set up a team of about 100 engineers and product managers to dig for vulnerabilities in Android phones, develop ways to exploit them — and turn that into profit.

According to the source, who requested anonymity for fear of reprisals, the company only targeted users in rural areas and smaller towns initially, while avoiding users in megacities such as Beijing and Shanghai.

“The goal was to reduce the risk of being exposed,” they said.

By collecting expansive data on user activities, the company was able to create a comprehensive portrait of users’ habits, interests and preferences, according to the source.

This allowed it to improve its machine learning model to offer more personalized push notifications and ads, attracting users to open the app and place orders, they said.

The team was disbanded in early March, the source added, after questions about their activities came to light.

PDD didn’t reply to CNN’s repeated requests for comment on the team.

Approached by CNN, researchers from Tel Aviv-based cyber firm Check Point Research, Delaware-based app security startup Oversecured and Hyppönen’s WithSecure conducted independent analysis of the 6.49.0 version of the app, released on Chinese app stores in late February.

Google Play is not available in China, and Android users in the country download their apps from local stores. In March, when Google suspended Pinduoduo, it said it had found malware in off-Play versions of the app.

The researchers found code designed to achieve “privilege escalation”: a type of cyberattack that exploits a vulnerable operating system to gain a higher level of access to data than it’s supposed to have, according to experts.

“Our team has reverse engineered that code and we can confirm that it tries to escalate rights, tries to gain access to things normal apps wouldn’t be able to do on Android phones,” said Hyppönen.

The app was able to continue running in the background and prevent itself from being uninstalled, which allowed it to boost its monthly active user rates, Hyppönen said. It also had the ability to spy on competitors by tracking activity on other shopping apps and getting information from them, he added.

Check Point Research additionally identified ways in which the app was able to evade scrutiny.

The app deployed a method that allowed it to push updates without an app store review process meant to detect malicious applications, the researchers said.

They also identified in some plug-ins the intent to obscure potentially malicious components by hiding them under legitimate file names, such as Google’s.

“Such a technique is widely used by malware developers that inject malicious code into applications that have legitimate functionality,” they said.

In China, about three quarters of smartphone users are on the Android system. Apple (AAPL)’s iPhone has 25% market share, according to Daniel Ives of Wedbush Securities.

Sergey Toshin, the founder of Oversecured, said Pinduoduo’s malware specifically targeted different Android-based operating systems, including those used by Samsung, Huawei, Xiaomi and Oppo.

CNN has reached out to these companies for comment.

Toshin described Pinduoduo as “the most dangerous malware” ever found among mainstream apps.

“I’ve never seen anything like this before. It’s like, super expansive,” he said.

Most phone manufacturers globally customize the core Android software, the Android Open Source Project (AOSP), to add unique features and applications to their own devices.

Toshin found Pinduoduo to have exploited about 50 Android system vulnerabilities. Most of the exploits were tailor made for customized parts known as the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) code, which tends to be audited less often than AOSP and is therefore more prone to vulnerabilities, he said.

Pinduoduo also exploited a number of AOSP vulnerabilities, including one which was flagged by Toshin to Google in February 2022. Google fixed the bug this March, he said.

According to Toshin, the exploits allowed Pinduoduo access to users’ locations, contacts, calendars, notifications and photo albums without their consent. They were also able to change system settings and access users’ social network accounts and chats, he said.

Of the six teams CNN spoke to for this story, three did not conduct full examinations. But their primary reviews showed that Pinduoduo asked for a large number of permissions beyond the normal functions of a shopping app.

They included “potentially invasive permissions” such as “set wallpaper” and “download without notification,” said René Mayrhofer, head of the Institute of Networks and Security at the Johannes Kepler University Linz in Austria.

Suspicions about malware in Pinduoduo’s app were first raised in late February in a report by a Chinese cybersecurity firm called Dark Navy. Even though the analysis didn’t directly name the shopping giant, the report spread quickly among other researchers, who did name the company. Some of the analysts followed up with their own reports confirming the original findings.

Soon after, on March 5, Pinduoduo issued a new update of its app, version 6.50.0, which removed the exploits, according to two experts who CNN spoke to.

Two days after the update, Pinduoduo disbanded the team of engineers and product managers who had developed the exploits, according to the Pinduoduo source.

The next day, team members found themselves locked out of Pinduoduo’s bespoke workplace communication app, Knock, and lost access to files on the company’s internal network. Engineers also found their access to big data, data sheets and the log system revoked, the source said.

Most of the team were transferred to work at Temu. They were assigned to different departments at the subsidiary, with some working on marketing or developing push notifications, according to the source.

A core group of about 20 cybersecurity engineers who specialize in finding and exploiting vulnerabilities remain at Pinduoduo, they said.

Toshin of Oversecured, who looked into the update, said although the exploits were removed, the underlying code was still there and could be reactivated to carry out attacks.

Pinduoduo has been able to grow its user base against a backdrop of the Chinese government’s regulatory clampdown on Big Tech that began in late 2020.

That year, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology launched a sweeping crackdown on apps that illegally collect and use personal data.

The Personal Information Protection Law stipulates that no party should illegally collect, process or transmit personal information. They’re also banned from exploiting internet-related security vulnerabilities or engaging in actions that endanger cybersecurity.

Pinduoduo’s apparent malware would be a violation of those laws, tech policy experts say, and should have been detected by the regulator.

“This would be embarrassing for the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, because this is their job,” said Kendra Schaefer, a tech policy expert at Trivium China, a consultancy. “They’re supposed to check Pinduoduo, and the fact that they didn’t find (anything) is embarrassing for the regulator.”

#119. Belgian plan for ICE-style immigration raids on homes criticised as ‘law of fear’

Rights groups oppose law that will let police forcibly enter homes to remove refused asylum seekers

Belgium could see ICE-style home raids that echo the US crackdown on immigration, rights campaigners have warned, after MPs approved a law that allows police to forcibly enter properties to remove undocumented people.

For months, intense debate has swirled across Belgium over measures introduced by the ruling right-leaning coalition to ramp up expulsions of people who have been denied the right to stay in the country.

In the run-up to the parliamentary vote last Friday, more than 60 protests were held across the country, adding to criticism voiced by NGOs, judges and lawyers’ associations over the measures. Some have drawn parallels with the US, where arrests by Immigration and Customs Enforcement have soared under Donald Trump’s administration.

The law, which is set to take effect next spring, gives the Belgian immigration office the power to ask an investigating judge to authorise entry into residences that may be housing anyone without papers, including homes where migrants are being hosted by friends or family.

Once this warrant has been obtained, police will be able to enter people’s homes without consent between 5am and 9pm, potentially in the presence of children. Once inside, they can detain people and carry out searches for identity documents.

“It’s a law of fear,” said Carine Thibaut, the director of Amnesty International’s French-speaking Belgian section, citing the impact the raids could have on children and on people who had opened their homes to migrants. “It really disseminates fear.”

Officials have said the measures will be targeted at people who have been denied asylum in Belgium, who have ignored orders to leave and who are seen as a threat to public order or national security.

A broad range of critics have said that the law is vague on what constitutes a public threat, citing cases where these terms were used for people who had carried out undeclared work, run a red light or shoplifted, meaning it will be up to individual judges to determine whether private homes can be turned into sites of immigration enforcement.

According to Amnesty International, the judges would not have full investigative powers but instead would be limited on who they could ask for additional information, while the legislation fails to provide a means for people to appeal or challenge any decisions that are made.

“They always say it’s for criminals,” Thibaut said. “We always answer that they already have all the tools to address this problem, because they can do that through the normal justice system.”

Her organisation is among the 20 rights groups that have warned that the law perpetuates the conflation of lack of legal status with delinquency. “The home is inviolable. This constitutional principle should never depend on the administrative status of those who live there,” they said.

The view was echoed at the Brussels-based Platform for International Cooperation on Undocumented Migrants, known as Picum. “This really has nothing to do with people who have committed crimes,” said Chiara Catelli, an advocacy officer. “It’s really about wanting to return people, deporting people, which is an administrative procedure.”

She added: “We’ve all seen the devastating consequences of home raids in the US, where they fuel fear and violence and tear families apart. It’s deeply alarming that an EU member state is now moving in the same direction.”

Catelli noted that similar, hardline measures were already in place in countries such as the Netherlands and Germany. The EU’s recently approved returns regulation, aimed at ramping up deportations across the bloc, includes similar provisions, meaning other member states could soon adopt comparable measures, she said. “We’re really afraid that this is the beginning of something that will become more widespread.”

Thibaut said that measures such as those in Belgium would gradually chip away at the rights of everyone. “It’s the beginning of reducing the state of law and the respect for human rights,” she said.

“This time it is for people without papers; after that, it might be people who are using drugs. That’s the way you slowly, slowly dismantle a society based on human rights, on respect for justice and on separation of powers”.

#112. Nissan reduces petrol car production as EV sales boom in China

Manufacturer says ‘shift to new energy vehicles is happening faster than expected’ and market is harsh

The Japanese carmaker Nissan has said it has been caught out by accelerating sales of electric and hybrid cars in China amid a brutal price war in the world’s biggest automotive market.

The manufacturer slashed its sales forecasts for China and said it would cut back on petrol car manufacturing while increasing production of battery electric vehicles and plug-in hybrids, which combine a smaller battery with a petrol engine.

Nissan said it expected to sell between 2.97m and 3m cars this year, down from a previous forecast of 3.15m, mainly because of struggles in China.

“China remains our biggest challenge,” said Ivan Espinosa, Nissan’s chief executive. “Demand is weaker, competition is intensifying, and the shift to new energy vehicles is happening faster than expected. The market is being very, very harsh.”

The company reported increased sales in its home market as well as in North America, regions in which the shift to electric cars has been much slower because of policy decisions, including Donald Trump’s tearing up of incentives for battery car makers.

That divergence between different parts of the world illustrates the challenge for global carmakers, many of whom are trying to continue selling their more profitable petrol and diesel cars for as long as possible while risking being left behind by newer Chinese rivals.

China’s government gave weighty subsidies to its electric car industry, spotting an opportunity to overtake other countries such as Germany, Japan and the US, which had dominated sales of internal combustion engine cars.

That led to a huge number of Chinese EV competitors, which are now chasing a shrinking market. Nissan said sales across China fell by 22% in the first half of the year. That pressure “intensified from July through October” and left the carmaker’s sales down 32% in the first nine months of the year, Nissan said.

Espinosa said Nissan’s product lineup in China, led by the Sylphy family of saloon cars, was too heavily associated with petrol engines, so the company was cutting local production, exporting some products, while trying to ramp up its N series of models, which are electric or plug-in hybrids.

Yet at the same time as pushing for increased electric car production in China, Nissan has been among the manufacturers slowing the shift to batteries in Europe. In the UK the carmaker has lobbied to weaken the zero-emission vehicle mandate, a target for increasing EV sales, and to continue to sell petrol cars after 2035.

Her mother-in-law, Indira Gandhi, India's prime minister, had just left for her office in an adjoining building. Moments later, she would be assassinated by two of her Sikh bodyguards, in retaliation for the Indian army's storming of the Golden Temple in Amritsar, Sikhism's holiest shrine, earlier that year.

The sound was like firecrackers, but "different". The children's nanny came pounding on the bathroom door, hysterical, pointing towards the garden.

Sonia ran towards the path. A white Ambassador car was moving slowly towards her. Inside, Indira lay covered in blood, her sari stained, her eyes open, her face wearing what Sonia remembered as a look of surprise.

"Mummy, Mummy," she tried to say. No words came. At the hospital, she waited for hours before a doctor's shake of the head told her what she feared.

The ordeal did not end there. Rajiv Gandhi, her husband and the elder son of Indira Gandhi, summoned from a rally in West Bengal, arrived to tell her that party leaders wanted him to become prime minister.

Sonia's response was fury and terror. "They will kill you; they will kill you, too!" she pleaded. Rajiv replied: "They will kill me anyway." That evening, as Indira's body lay at home, Rajiv was sworn in.

It is one of the most searing passages in Belonging: A Journey of Love, Sonia Gandhi's new memoir: private devastation unfolding alongside the pressures of public life.

The woman who had largely stayed away from politics was now married to India's prime minister, and would eventually find herself at the head of the Congress party, leading it through two general election victories and becoming one of the country's most consequential political figures.

The passage also foreshadows another devastating loss. In 1991, Rajiv himself was assassinated.

Indira Gandhi was asssassinated by her Sikh bodyguards in October 1984

For Sonia, the years that followed were marked by retreat.

At 19, a young woman from a small Italian town, she had met Rajiv, an Indian student, in England. Their relationship took her into a new country and family, and a close bond with Indira followed. India became home through her husband and her mother-in-law.

After Rajiv's killing, even the public appearances required by the memorial trusts she headed became an ordeal. Before speeches, she feared tears would overwhelm her.

When the Dalai Lama asked whether she felt vengeful towards her husband's killers, she answered, "No, I don't," and burst into sobs. Slowly, she writes, Rajiv became "a presence within me rather than a piercing absence".

But grief returned at family milestones. At her son Rahul's Cambridge graduation in 1995, she and her children were overcome by the thought of how proud Rajiv would have been.

Politics, meanwhile, was closing in. The Congress party, the political vehicle of her husband's family for generations, was losing cohesion and electoral ground. Senior leaders were defecting, and the calls for Sonia to step in were growing louder.

She initially refused. She writes she had seen politics through Rajiv: its achievements, but also its "ambition and greed, hypocrisy and treachery". "I'd always disliked politics," she writes. It had taken first her mother-in-law, then "the love of my life, my children's father".

Yet the party's decline began to feel personal. Walking past photographs of Indira and Rajiv, she was seized by "a mixture of pain and guilt". She feared that the forces they had fought to "protect India's pluralistic ethos" were gaining ground: a rising Hindu nationalism that, in her view, threatened the "secular and pluralistic vision" of India they had sought to defend.

Congress leaders argued that without her, the party could splinter, leaving little to resist communal politics. The family name, she believed, carried an appeal beyond religion, caste, language and region. This, she says, was "the most compelling argument".

Rajiv Gandhi took oath as India's youngest prime minister hours after his mother's death

Her doubts were also about her own credentials. How could an outsider occupy a position held by two formidable figures? At home, Rahul and Priyanka feared losing their mother as they had lost their grandmother and father. Before a New Year holiday, they extracted a promise: "Don't you do anything crazy while we're away."

She writes that its central message â to perform one's dharma, or duty, without attachment to the fruits of one's actions â eventually resolved her inner conflict.

"I had the answer to my own doubts and questions. I had to step out and go where my duty called. And I did."

Sonia goes with Manmohan Singh to meet India's president at the presidential palace in Delhi and formally stake the Congress-led alliance's claim to form a government. Everyone assumes she will become prime minister. She says nothing.

At home, she tells a senior aide she intends to refuse. Party workers gather at the gates; senior colleagues demand a meeting.

"You fought the election, you led us," says one. "People believed in you. How can you let them down like this? Our workers will revolt. You think they'll accept another leader?"

Alliance leaders arrive to persuade her. They surround her, she recalls, and one insists: "We will not take no for an answer." Overwhelmed, she murmurs "Theek hai" - all right. They take it as consent and begin congratulating her.

Her children are appalled, she writes. Rahul storms in: "You said yes?" Her daughter Priyanka urges her not to be forced into a decision.

Sonia writes she spends a long night arguing with herself, friends and family. The risk is not merely personal. She is at pains to defend the political inheritance of her husband and mother-in-law, even as the party they built has suffered a prolonged decline. "At this moment it seems to me that, although I tried my best, I didn't succeed in beating back the forces I'd set out to fight."

#89. The four astronauts NASA picked for the first crewed moon mission in 50 years

Astronauts who will helm the first crewed moon mission in five decades were revealed on Monday, queuing up the quartet to begin training for the historic Artemis II lunar flyby that is set to take off in November 2024.

The astronauts are NASA’s Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch, and Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency.

Wiseman is a 47-year-old decorated naval aviator and test pilot who was first selected to be a NASA astronaut in 2009. A native of Baltimore, Maryland, he’s completed one prior spaceflight, a 165-day trip to the International Space Station that had launched aboard a Russian Soyuz rocket in 2014. Most recently, Wiseman served as chief of the astronaut office before stepping down in November 2022, making him eligible for a flight assignment.

Wiseman will serve as commander of the Artemis II mission.

Hansen, 47, is a fighter pilot who was selected by the Canadian Space Agency for astronaut training in 2009. From London, Ontario, Hansen is one of only four active Canadian astronauts, and he recently became the first Canadian to be put in charge of training for a new class of NASA astronauts.

He will be the first Canadian ever to travel to deep space.

Glover is a 46-year-old naval aviator who returned to Earth from his first spaceflight in 2021 after piloting the second crewed flight of SpaceX’s Crew Dragon spacecraft and spending nearly six months aboard the International Space Station.

“It’s so much more than the four names that have been announced,” Glover said during the Monday announcement at NASA Johnson Space Center in Houston. “We need to celebrate this moment in human history. … It is the next step in the journey that will get humanity to Mars.”

Glover, born in Pomona, California, served in several military squadrons in the United States and Japan in the 2000s, and he completed test pilot training with the US Air Force. When he was selected for the NASA astronaut corps in 2013, he was working in the US Senate as a legislative fellow. All told, Glover logged 3,000 flight hours in more than 40 aircraft, over 400 carrier arrested landings and 24 combat missions.

Glover’s first mission to space was as part of the SpaceX Crew-1 team, which launched to the International Space Station in November 2020 for a six-month stay on the orbiting laboratory.

Koch, 44, is a veteran of six spacewalks — including the first all-female spacewalk in 2019. She holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman, with a total of 328 days in space. Koch is also an an electrical engineer who helped develop scientific instruments for multiple NASA mission. Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

NASA is targeting a 2025 launch date for Artemis III, though the space agency’s inspector general has already said delays will likely push the mission to 2026 or later.

The space agency has been seeking to return people to the moon for more than a decade. The Artemis program was designed to pave the way to establishing a permanent lunar outpost, allowing astronauts to live and work deeper into space long term as NASA and its partners map a path to sending the first humans to Mars.

Vanessa Wyche, the director of NASA’s Johnson Space Center, declined to provide details to CNN about the selection process. But she emphasized the diversity of the Artemis II crew, which includes men and women rather than only a staff of White male test pilots as has been the case for historic missions of the past.

“I can tell you, they still all have the right stuff,” Wyche said. “We have requirements different than we did (when we) just had test pilots” on inaugural missions.

Koch said in an interview with CNN’s Ed Lavandera that the group found out they were selected a few weeks ago.

“We were all sent to a meeting that was on our calendars under a different pretext that didn’t sound as lofty as the one it was going to be,” Koch said. “And accidentally two of us were very late to that meeting.”

She said the offer rendered her “speechless.”

“It truly is an honor,” she added. “It’s an honor — not to get myself in the space — but because it’s amazing to be a part of this team that’s going back to the moon and on to Mars.”

An interview with the four astronauts will air on “CNN This Morning” on Tuesday, which starts at 6 am ET.

#27. Falkland Islanders face ‘devastating consequences’ if Argentina blocks oil project, says UK

The people of the Falkland Islands face “devastating consequences” if Argentina is successful in blocking development of its offshore oilfield, the attorney general for England and Wales has told an international tribunal.

Ellie Reeves, representing the British government at a hearing of the international tribunal for the law of the sea (Itlos), said billions of pounds vital to the future of the British overseas territory would be lost.

Reeves accused Argentina of surreptitiously seeking to pursue its longstanding sovereignty claims over the south Atlantic islands through an application to the tribunal, which is sitting over two days in Hamburg.

Argentina is seeking a provisional ruling suspending the drilling for oil before further arbitration over the future of the project, which it claims poses an environmental risk.

Reeves told the tribunal: “In reality, what Argentina opposes is hydrocarbon activities for the economic benefit of the Falkland Islanders. What it wants is to take control of those resources for itself.”

Argentina claims Britain’s approval of the Sea Lion project is in breach of a 1976 UN resolution calling on both parties to desist from unilateral action on the islands until the sovereignty dispute is settled.

Buenos Aires wants to force a commitment from the UK that it will “stop the drilling”, in order to “preserve the present situation” before a settlement over the oilfield.

After raising Argentina’s “armed invasion” of the islands in 1982, which she told the tribunal led to “loss of life and military occupation”, Reeves said the Falkland Islanders had a right to exploit their own natural resources, adding that a tribunal that ruled over the law of the seas had no place in settling a sovereignty dispute.

Reeves said that suspension of the drilling, which is due to start in March next year, would kill off the Sea Lion project.

“The provisional measures sought by Argentina would prevent the people of the Falkland Islands from developing their natural resources as they are entitled to do so, pursuant to their fundamental right to self-determination,” she said.

“The measures sought would have devastating consequences for the Falklands people. They risk losing billions of pounds in money, which is essential for the islands’ economic development.”

Sir Michael Wood KC, who also spoke on behalf of the UK, said the tribunal could lose member states should it overreach by issuing “provisional” measures that should be used only in exceptional circumstances.

Asked whether the UK would abide by the tribunal’s ruling, Reeves told the Guardian that she respected the institution but that she would not “get ahead” of the decisions.

Speaking earlier on Tuesday, Mario Oyarzábal, chief legal adviser to Argentina’s ministry of foreign affairs, had told the tribunal that the 3,600 inhabitants of the Falkland Islands, had no right to the resources around the south Atlantic archipelago.

Oyarzábal said: “[Argentina] respects the interests of the inhabitants of the islands and their way of life as enshrined in the Argentine constitution … What these British citizens do not have is a right to decide to which state the islands belong …

“These resources belong to the Argentine people. Some 46 million of them including the 1,247 inhabitants born on the islands whose interests will be duly taken into account.”

He said Argentina was ready to negotiate “all aspects of the question” of the Falkland Islands, which are known as the Malvinas in Argentina, “including the present aggravation of the dispute caused by the UK”.

He accused the UK of refusing to enter talks over the islands while pushing forward on the exploitation of its natural resources in a way that would entrench the status quo.

Oyarzábal claimed that the oil money from the drilling would “finance the very military base and presence that sustains the UK’s de facto control of the territory”.

Argentina’s case is that provisional measures to suspend the drilling are urgent. The Sea Lion project in the North Falkland basin, led by the Israeli firm Navitas and its British partner, Rockhopper, will start drilling in March and oil will be extracted from 2028 and continue for 35 years. It has the potential to double the islands’ GDP per capita to a cumulative uplift of £8bn over the lifetime of the project. The UK government asked why an application to Itlos had only been made in recent months amid increasingly bellicose rhetoric.

Argentina further claims that it has rights as the “coastal state” to the Falklands. The UK’s lawyers said this claim highlighted that Argentina’s application was “at heart” about sovereignty.

The Sea Lion project has been condemned as unlawful by Argentina’s president, Javier Milei. The tribunal was told by Argentinian lawyers that the permissions given by the UK government for drilling was the “unilateral conduct” of a “colonial power”.

Oyarzábal added: “In the event of a blowout or spill, it is Argentina that would bear its consequences and be called upon to respond, not a state that is 12,700km away.”

UK lawyers responded that the risk of such an incident was low and that the companies involved had mitigated against it.

Argentina invaded the Falklands in 1982 at the cost of the lives of 649 Argentinian military personnel, 255 British military personnel and three Falkland Islanders. The hearing is the first time that substantive matters relating to the islands have been litigated at an international tribunal.
