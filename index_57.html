#19. UK was ‘vehement’ slavery reparations should not be on Commonwealth summit agenda

British prime minister has now agreed to structured dialogue on reparatory justice but issue remains off formal Chogm agenda

British officials reportedly insisted that discussions of reparations for slavery should not be on the agenda of the Commonwealth leaders’ summit in Antigua and Barbuda – and even warned that Andy Burnham would not attend the meeting if the subject was formally included, according to a leading Caribbean political figure.

Ralph Gonsalves, a former prime minister of St Vincent and the Grenadines and a senior figure in the reparatory justice movement Repair Campaign, welcomed Britain’s decision to enter structured dialogue on reparations, but said the UK had come “kicking and screaming” to the conversation.

Currently, slavery reparations is not on the official agenda of the Commonwealth Heads of Government (Chogm) summit, which begins on Sunday.

Trade, climate resilience and finance, sustainable debt, democracy and investment in opportunities for young people are among the stated priorities for the 56-nation bloc spanning Africa, Asia, the Caribbean and the Americas, the Pacific and Europe.

“My understanding from informed circles is that Britain was vehement not to have the matter on the agenda. The British officials – and in that regard they were joined by the Canadian officials – were even raising the prospect that if it is on the agenda the prime minister of Britain wouldn’t go,” Gonsalves told the Guardian.

“They also started to circulate this untruth that India didn’t want it on the agenda, but that was not so at all. India was very supportive at the UN general assembly resolution on March 25th, 2026. So they were spreading this false word about India.”

Gonsalves said that the relentless campaign by Caribbean nations, amplified by a growing regional and global reparations movement, has propelled reparatory justice to the heart of the political conversation at the meeting.

“Because of what happened at the UN, because of what happened in Africa, what happened in the Caribbean, and all the work which is taking place in Britain itself too, inside of the British parliament, inside of the British Labour party, both in the Commons and the House of Lords, it is an issue which you can’t duck any more,” he said.

He added: “The danger of not putting it formally on the agenda is what we see happening now, that everybody who could have foreseen, whether or not it’s formally on the agenda, even the nature of how the discussions take place at the Commonwealth, and certainly in the retreats, it would have been raised.”

Gonsalves said while the actual structured dialoguewas unlikely to happen at Chogm, it was expected that when the leaders met in their private retreat, the format and details of the dialogue would be discussed.

He said: “The British government, they’re coming, kicking and screaming to the table of structured dialogue. But at least they’re coming.”

An FCDO spokesperson said: “We categorically reject this completely untrue accusation. The UK has approached this conversation in good faith, and we have at no point told any other Chogm member what should not be on an agenda.

“Our position on reparations is clear but this government respects the right of others to raise matters as they wish.”

Prof Sir Hilary Beckles, chair of the Caribbean Community (Caricom) Reparations Commission, had described the meeting as “the Chogm of reparations”, adding that reparatory justice was “the number one item” for the Caribbean as Commonwealth leaders gather in the region.

On Friday there was a historic shift in Britain’s position when the British prime minister went further than any of his predecessors by agreeing to structured dialogue and to consider an apology.

Though Caribbean governments and campaigners welcomed Burnham’s announcement, made in an op-ed for the Guardian, it was clear that agreeing on forms of reparations was still a priority for the region.

Guyana’s president, Irfaan Ali, welcomed Burnham’s statement and called for a Commonwealth subcommittee to work out the details of the dialogue over the next six to 12 months.

“I’m happy that finally the discourse has moved to a position where it should have been, where the British government is involved, because that’s the state we are dealing with,” he said.

Ali said the talks should move beyond attempting to put a price on the indignity of slavery and towards addressing its developmental consequences.

“How do we identify a system of support where the UK government, with its financial institutions, can sit with countries … and work on a development pathway, identify development gaps, whether in health, education, social services, infrastructure?”

Barbados’s prime minister, Mia Mottley, thanked Burnham for “breaking new ground”, while making clear that the two sides remained divided. “On reparations in particular, our views differ, and we will say so plainly and respectfully,” she said.

Mottley identified an apology, investment in health and education, tackling systemic poverty and inequality, and fairer debt terms among areas where agreement could be reached.

The Bahamas prime minister, Philip Davis, said the talks were not simply about “historical remembrance” but “the enduring consequences of slavery and the importance of justice, acknowledgment and meaningful redress”.

As the meeting kicks off on Sunday, the 45th anniversary of Antigua and Barbuda’s independence from British colonial rule, King Charles is indicating he will not follow Burnham and offer a full apology, there is still a sense that the reparations fight is far from over.

King Charles and Burnham will be greeted with protests as they travel through Antigua’s capital, St John’s, to attend Chogm events.

On billboards, placards and T-shirts, in letters and at a candlelight vigil for the thousands of Africans trafficked to Antigua and Barbuda during the transatlantic slave trade, the message from the Caribbean will be clear: there will be calls for an apology and reparations.

On Sunday Kemi Badenoch told broadcasters that an apology for Britain’s role in the transatlantic slave trade would be a “navel-gazing distraction”, describing slavery as an evil “in which people from every continent and region participated”.

“I would really like to sit down with my dear sister Kemi and explain to her so that she could get an understanding of what we are talking about,” Gonsalves said, adding that he would like to discuss how reparatory justice could help heal some of the fractures arising from Britain’s multicultural and ethnic tensions.

#4. Sudanese diaspora and human rights groups denounce UAE nominee for UN top job

Critics cite Lana Nusseibeh’s denial of accusations the UAE is fuelling Sudan war by supporting paramilitary RSF

Members of the Sudanese diaspora and international human rights monitors have denounced the candidature of an Emirati diplomat for the top job at the UN, citing her denial of accusations that the United Arab Emirates is fuelling the war in Sudan by supporting the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces.

The UAE nominated Lana Nusseibeh last week to replace the UN secretary general, António Guterres, as he finishes his second five-year term ⁠this year, upending a race that had become gridlocked.

Rights groups and others have for years documented evidence of UAE support for the RSF, which has been accused of atrocities during the course of its war against the Sudanese army, including an attack on the city of El Fasher in October 2025 that the UN said had “hallmarks of genocide”.

Nusseibeh, the UAE’s representative to the UN until 2024, has repeatedly reflected the UAE’s blanket denials of the accusations.

The Sudanese pro-democracy and women’s rights activist Hala al-Karib, the regional director of the Strategic Initiative for Women in the Horn of Africa, said Nusseibeh had “no track record of acting independently of her country’s role in Sudan” and that endorsement by the security council would amount “essentially [to] a denial of the genocidal acts committed in El Fasher by the RSF”.

There was also strong criticism of Nusseibeh’s candidature at a virtual briefing by the Avaaz human rights advocacy group on Tuesday.

Ibrahim Alduma, who grew up in El Fasher and is the co-founder of the human rights organisation Sudan Action Hub, told the briefing that success for Nusseibeh would result in people losing faith in the UN.

“If this candidate [succeeds], how would we tell people on the ground that the United Nations is not an obstruction of the [rights of] the people of Sudan?” said Alduma. “How could the people – not only in Sudan, but other victims of wars and international criminals – trust that the UN will achieve some kind of justice?”

Husam Mahjoub, a Sudanese advocate and co-founder of independent media Sudan Bukra TV, said: “Electing [the UAE’s] senior diplomat as secretary general would hand leadership of (UN) institutions to that government’s chief defender, at the very moment Sudanese civilians most need the ‌UN to hold powerful states accountable.”

Kenneth Roth, a former executive director of Human Rights Watch, told the briefing: “She has been asked about the UAE’s role in arming and supporting the RSF, and she just blatantly denies it without regard to the facts. That is not what we need as UN secretary-general.”

On Wednesday, Bruno Stagno Ugarte, the chief advocacy officer at Human Rights Watch, said Nusseibeh’s dismissal of UN reports documenting the UAE’s links to the RSF had “seriously undermined justice and accountability for the victims”.

In a statement on Tuesday Nusseibeh said that if she got the top UN job she would support the work of UN fact-finding missions for accountability and transitional justice over the war in Sudan, and described the RSF’s crimes as “atrocious”.

She added that she had taken a leave of absence from her ministerial post in the UAE government to be “mindful of the obligations of independence” the UN role would entail, adding that “the horrific conflict in Sudan will be the litmus test of the success of an incoming secretary general”.

To become the next UN security general, a candidate must secure a minimum of nine affirmative votes out of the 15 security council members, and avoid a veto from the US, the UK, China, France or Russia, its permanent members.

He added: “It amazes me that she’s being put forward. This is a completely unsuitable candidate.”

#3. Shop freezers in seven European countries hit by cyber-attack

Freezers have had to be emptied at several ICA grocery stores in Sweden after the cyber-attack

Dozens of supermarkets across seven European countries have been hit by a cyber-attack affecting their refrigerators and freezers.

Around 40 Delhaize supermarkets in Belgium and Luxembourg were affected, while a smaller number of shops were hit in Sweden, Ireland, the Netherlands, Estonia and Finland.

Sabcobel, a Belgian company that manufactures some of the affected refrigerators, said the units' evaporators were switched to defrost mode, causing them to warm up, in what is believed to be the same method used in last month's incident that hit hundreds of French supermarkets.

Millions of euros worth of food had to be thrown away in October, local media reported at the time.

Stephan Moelans, the CEO of Sabcobel, which operates Delhaize's systems, on Monday told the Belga news agency that the incident was a cyber-attack and that the company had been "working all night and morning to disconnect the affected units from the network".

He said since the French attack in early October, the company had been working to "protect our customers fridges against this vulnerability" but that the units needed physical modifications, not just software updates.

Most supermarkets across Europe are linked to the internet, and maintenance and troubleshooting can be done remotely.

"The simplest solution is to manage the fridges over a secure internet connection. Most of our customers switched to that after the attack in France," Moelans added.

The temperature of freezers in several shops across Europe reportedly rose to as high as 50C (122F).

It is unclear who is behind the latest apparent attack.

In the US, authorities are investigating what caused refrigeration malfunctions at a number of American military bases in August and working together with French officials to examine whether the incidents could be linked.

Both Belgium and Sweden's national cyber-security agencies said they were monitoring the incident.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre said it was working with international partners and industry to "fully understand details of an incident". No incidents have been reported in the UK.

#1. Sinkholes, scarred land and scapegoats: the scourge of illegal gold mining in Johannesburg

There is no end in sight for the drivers of illegal mining, while police action appears ineffective

Cora Bailey has been encountering illegal miners ever since she first started treating injured animals in the townships around Roodepoort, west of Johannesburg, in 1991. But as the price of gold has soared, so too has activity in the abandoned mine shafts that mark the area.

In late September, dozens of zama zamas – the name given to illegal miners in South Africa, meaning roughly “to take a chance” in Zulu – arrived to dig for gold across the dirt road from her charity, Claw (Community Led Animal Welfare).

Soon, they were taking water for their panning and processing from the organisation’s two 25,000-litre water tanks, which are filled irregularly by the city government for the 100 or so rescued and injured animals on site.

“We’ve asked them really nicely and haven’t ever refused them water to drink … But, for the first time ever, we closed the gate at 12 [midday], because we literally had about two buckets of water left,” Bailey said in mid-October.

Police told her that the scale of the illegal mining was too big for them to do anything about, she said at the time. About a week later, officers sheared away the grass that had partly hidden the zama zamas. But within a few days a smaller group had returned, although Bailey said they were now taking Claw’s water only for drinking.

South Africa’s police regularly trumpet their success in tackling illegal mining. On 19 October, they said they had arrested 71 suspected zama zamas across the country in the previous week.

However, researchers put the number of illegal miners in the tens of thousands. In 2019, Marcena Hunter, a researcher at the Global Initiative against Transnational Organised Crime, estimated that a tenth of South Africa’s gold production, worth about $1bn, was being exported illegally.

Police operations have also led to tragedy. In 2025, 93 bodies were pulled from a mine shaft in Stilfontein, after officers blocked food and water from being delivered underground.

Meanwhile, there is no end in sight to the dynamics driving illegal mining, including the high price of gold, poverty, unemployment, corrupt politicians and police profiting, and the failure of mining companies to fulfil their legal obligation to seal mining shafts when they cease operating.

South Africa’s modern history is tied to mining. In 1884, gold was found on a farm, sparking a gold rush that led to the creation of Johannesburg. Gold production peaked in the 1970s, but South Africa was the world’s largest producer, a title it held for more than a century, until 2007.

In 2009, Morris Viljoen, a geology professor at the University of the Witwatersrand, estimated that the reef that gave the university its name had produced more than a third of the gold ever mined globally. Claw is housed in the old offices of the Durban Deep mine, which shut at the turn of the century, having once lent its name to an Elton John song.

The city government claims there is still 40,000 tonnes of gold underground in the region. But, with the remainder lying ever deeper, the costs of recovering it have risen.

Mining companies are supposed to seal up old mine shafts and rehabilitate the environment around them. However, in 2025 there were “6,100 derelict and ownerless mines and 1,170 unsafe mine openings”, the mining minister, Gwede Mantashe, said in response to a parliamentary question.

Zama zamas have stepped into those voids left in the earth. While there is no comprehensive data on them, experts agree that a majority are from neighbouring countries, principally Lesotho, Mozambique and Zimbabwe.

The skyline south of Johannesburg is punctuated by the pale yellow ridges of mine dumps. Increasingly, the ground is also caving open.

For politicians seeking votes in local elections on 4 November, the culprit is clear: illegal miners. In May, Kenny Kunene, the Johannesburg council member responsible for transport, took journalists on a tour of sinkholes that he claimed were the fault of zama zamas, including a section of Wemmer Pan Road that had completely collapsed.

The road was once the main artery linking the city’s central business district to the south-eastern suburbs. In one section it has completely disappeared, now raw earth pitted with the holes where illegal miners have been digging.

However, when it comes to sinkholes, experts say zama zamas are being scapegoated.

Rosalind Morris, an anthropology professor at Columbia University, who has worked extensively with illegal miners, noted that the porous dolomitic rock to Johannesburg’s south and west becomes unstable when water is removed.

“The biggest culprit of sinkholes in South Africa is formal industrial mining, which dewaters at a scale of hundreds of millions of gallons of water removed from underground every day,” she said.

“There are areas where there is a lot of residual gold, which are being very intensely mined by zama zamas, and some of that definitely causes structural collapse, there’s no doubt about it. But the idea that this was invented or caused for the first time by zama zamas is completely fallacious.”

Zama zamas are also frequently blamed for violence in mining areas, including a September mass shooting in Wedela township, west of Johannesburg. However, David van Wyk, the lead researcher at Bench Marks Foundation, said culpability laid with the often-feuding gangs that controlled access to the shafts.

“The guys working in the mine are actually super-exploited cheap labour … The guys with the guns are the guys who take the gold,” he said. “They don’t go underground.”

In Khutsong, about 50 miles (80km) west of Johannesburg, illegal miners, local and foreign, kept the township afloat, said Vusi Cele, the youth secretary for the local branch of Mining Affected Communities United in Action, a campaign group that advocates for “artisanal miners” to be granted mining licences. “Without those rents, it’s a ghost town,” he said.

While Cele, 33, currently hustles above ground selling mobile phone covers and screen protectors, he said he too would mine if he could get a licence. “On the surface there’s peanuts,” he said. “Underground there’s money.”
