#150. How a US extradition bid over Hamas allegations put a wealthy activist before a Spanish court

James ‘Fergie’ Chambers, a Cox Enterprises heir, faced a two-hour hearing about alleged support for Hamas

A two-hour hearing in Spain’s high court Friday on the US extradition request of wealthy leftwing activist and philanthropist James “Fergie” Chambers turned out to be a study in contrasts.

Chambers’s defense took most of the session to query the US citizen and detail irregularities in the Trump administration’s extradition request – several of which the Guardian has reported on – as well as the figures of Spanish law that would lead the three-judge panel to deny the request; while the prosecutor sped through his case, head down, asking Chambers no questions at all.

Notably, neither the judges or the prosecutor asked Chambers to address the insinuation in the extradition request that the wealthy activist had supported Hamas. Chambers’s case is the first known instance of the US seeking to extradite a citizen over alleged support for Hamas, an accusation apparently tied to an international money laundering charge and donations to organizations helping Palestinians in Gaza.

But the defense’s queries did allow him to speak for the first time since his 10 July arrest in Ibiza about events at the center of the case: 2023 protests against Israeli weapons manufacturer Elbit in Massachusets and New Hampshire and 2024 transfers of $7.2m to Tunisia, where he lived at the time. The latter was buttressed with testimony from a financial expert.

On the whole, Chambers painted a picture different from the depiction contained in the extradition request, that of a violent backer of Hamas on the run from the US. At one point, he noted that his family’s next door neighbor in Tunis was the US ambassador.

The court will decide next week on extraditing Chambers. If the decision is affirmative, Spain’s council of ministers can reverse the court’s decision. This means Chambers’s extradition may be among the last decisions made under Pedro Sánchez, the socialist prime minister, before national elections on 29 November.

Friday’s hearing began with Chambers’s defense asking about his wealth. A self-described communist and anti-imperialist, Chambers sold stock back to his family’s business, Atlanta-based Cox Enterprises, worth about $250m in mid-2023.

Then he started a foundation, he told the court. He and the foundation’s three-member board decide on funding and ensure that no projects seeking support are under US sanctions, Chambers said. The foundation has funded an estimated 100 progressive and humanitarian projects in 20 countries, according to his partner, activist and actor Stella Schnabel – including, Chambers said Friday, organizations in Gaza working on issues such as “medical treatment for children, transportation out of dangerous areas and clean water and food”.

Two of the request’s three charges – riot and conspiracy to riot – are related to the late-2023 protests against Elbit.

Chambers detailed his participation in the protests: locking himself to a door, marching in the street and – in New Hampshire – standing in the road, speaking to the media and paying bail for four protesters who were eventually found guilty of misdemeanor charges and served 30 days in jail.

As for the $7.2m, the defense and a financial expert serving as a witness laid out how ten bank transfers from the US to Tunisia mostly went to sponsor the football club Club Africain – described by Chambers as “one of the largest football clubs in Africa” – and a sporting business Chambers launched while living in Tunisia.

“Is there any indication that the money went to support terrorist activities?” asked defense attorney Aitor Martínez. “None,” said financial expert Albert Deulofeu Schilt.

Then, in about 15 minutes of rapid-fire monotone, prosecutor Juan Hernández Villalba laid out a wholly different picture of Chambers as backing “an organized structure … aimed at repeatedly committing illegal acts … together with funds destined for terrorist organizations”.

He accused the defense of laying out the facts of the case “in a fragmented way … when what’s most relevant is an evaluation of the whole picture”.

“The extradition petition isn’t based on ideology or political statements, but on actions [Chambers] organized, financed and carried out, causing material damage,” Hernández Villalba asserted.

The actions against Elbit “were presented as a simple protest”, he said. “Nevertheless, the actions described by the US were planned for days … with objectively violent content.”

Given all this, the prosecutor said, “there are more than enough indications to consider … that [Chambers’s] funding was aimed at terrorism”.

Among the 100-plus people in varying time zones following Friday’s hearing on the court’s YouTube channel was Irish film-maker and journalist Sinéad O’Shea, whose documentary on Chambers called All About the Money makes its New York premiere this weekend. It covers the time period cited by the FBI affidavit underpinning the US request. O’Shea said that extradition case “builds a false narrative which begins with the protest in New Hampshire – a piece of vandalism at worst and for which people ended up serving 30 days. I saw myself the football club in which he invested in Tunisia and his donations to Palestinian and other charities are a matter of public record. It is impossible not to see this as a politically motivated case.”

Chambers’s views have been extensively profiled in US media. A week after the 7 October 2023 Hamas attacks on Israel, he posted on X about Israel’s response: “Stop calling it ‘collective punishment’/There’s nothing to be ‘punished’ for/No faction of the Palestinian resistance, Hamas or other, has done *anything* wrong/It’s simply genocide/Like it has been.”

Dressed in dark jeans, a grey-blue shirt, a black jacket, with a close-cropped haircut and mustache, Chambers got the last word during the hearing.

“To this day I cannot understand why I have been in prison since July for what amounts to speaking emotionally and committing minor offenses of public disturbance,” he said.

“I’ve never had any contact with any terrorist organization. I’ve certainly never financed any terrorist organization … and for this my family has been torn apart. I missed my grandmother’s funeral last week. My five-year-old son asks where I am every day. And my wife will give birth, God willing, in March – and I don’t know if I will be by her side.”

“[All] because I have spoken my mind, and I had a lot more money than others who have spoken their mind, in my country. And that’s all it amounts to.”

#149. Hundreds of millions at risk from Chinese shopping app malware

It is one of China’s most popular shopping apps, selling clothing, groceries and just about everything else under the sun to more than 750 million users a month.

But according to cybersecurity researchers, it can also bypass users’ cell phone security to monitor activities on other apps, check notifications, read private messages and change settings.

And once installed, it’s tough to remove.

In a detailed investigation, CNN spoke to half a dozen cybersecurity teams from Asia, Europe and the United States — as well as multiple former and current Pinduoduo employees — after receiving a tipoff.

Multiple experts identified the presence of malware on the Pinduoduo app that exploited vulnerabilities in Android operating systems. Company insiders said the exploits were utilized to spy on users and competitors, allegedly to boost sales.

“We haven’t seen a mainstream app like this trying to escalate their privileges to gain access to things that they’re not supposed to gain access to,” said Mikko Hyppönen, chief research officer at WithSecure, a Finnish cybersecurity firm.

“This is highly unusual, and it is pretty damning for Pinduoduo.”

Malware, short for malicious software, refers to any software developed to steal data or interfere with computer systems and mobile devices.

Evidence of sophisticated malware in the Pinduoduo app comes amid intense scrutiny of Chinese-developed apps like TikTok over concerns about data security.

Some American lawmakers are pushing for a national ban on the popular short-video app, whose CEO Shou Chew was grilled by Congress for five hours last week about its relations with the Chinese government.

The revelations are also likely to draw more attention to Pinduoduo’s international sister app, Temu, which is topping US download charts and fast expanding in other Western markets. Both are owned by Nasdaq-listed PDD, a multinational company with roots in China.

While Temu has not been implicated, Pinduoduo’s alleged actions risk casting a shadow over its sister app’s global expansion.

There is no evidence that Pinduoduo has handed data to the Chinese government. But as Beijing enjoys significant leverage over businesses under its jurisdiction, there are concerns from US lawmakers that any company operating in China could be forced to cooperate with a broad range of security activities.

The findings follow Google’s suspension of Pinduoduo from its Play Store in March, citing malware identified in versions of the app.

An ensuing report from Bloomberg said a Russian cybersecurity firm had also identified potential malware in the app.

Pinduoduo has previously rejected “the speculation and accusation that Pinduoduo app is malicious.”

CNN has contacted PDD multiple times over email and phone for comment, but has not received a response.

Pinduoduo, which boasts a user base that accounts for three quarters of China’s online population and a market value three times that of eBay (EBAY), wasn’t always an online shopping behemoth.

Founded in 2015 in Shanghai by Colin Huang, a former Google employee, the startup was fighting to establish itself in a market long dominated by e-commerce stalwarts Alibaba (BABA) and JD.com (JD).

It succeeded by offering steep discounts on friends-and-family group buying orders and focusing on lower-income rural areas.

Pinduoduo posted triple digit growth in monthly users until the end of 2018, the year it listed in New York. By the middle of 2020, though, the increase in monthly users had slowed to around 50% and would continue to decline, according to its earnings reports.

It was in 2020, according to a current Pinduoduo employee, that the company set up a team of about 100 engineers and product managers to dig for vulnerabilities in Android phones, develop ways to exploit them — and turn that into profit.

According to the source, who requested anonymity for fear of reprisals, the company only targeted users in rural areas and smaller towns initially, while avoiding users in megacities such as Beijing and Shanghai.

“The goal was to reduce the risk of being exposed,” they said.

By collecting expansive data on user activities, the company was able to create a comprehensive portrait of users’ habits, interests and preferences, according to the source.

This allowed it to improve its machine learning model to offer more personalized push notifications and ads, attracting users to open the app and place orders, they said.

The team was disbanded in early March, the source added, after questions about their activities came to light.

PDD didn’t reply to CNN’s repeated requests for comment on the team.

Approached by CNN, researchers from Tel Aviv-based cyber firm Check Point Research, Delaware-based app security startup Oversecured and Hyppönen’s WithSecure conducted independent analysis of the 6.49.0 version of the app, released on Chinese app stores in late February.

Google Play is not available in China, and Android users in the country download their apps from local stores. In March, when Google suspended Pinduoduo, it said it had found malware in off-Play versions of the app.

The researchers found code designed to achieve “privilege escalation”: a type of cyberattack that exploits a vulnerable operating system to gain a higher level of access to data than it’s supposed to have, according to experts.

“Our team has reverse engineered that code and we can confirm that it tries to escalate rights, tries to gain access to things normal apps wouldn’t be able to do on Android phones,” said Hyppönen.

The app was able to continue running in the background and prevent itself from being uninstalled, which allowed it to boost its monthly active user rates, Hyppönen said. It also had the ability to spy on competitors by tracking activity on other shopping apps and getting information from them, he added.

Check Point Research additionally identified ways in which the app was able to evade scrutiny.

The app deployed a method that allowed it to push updates without an app store review process meant to detect malicious applications, the researchers said.

They also identified in some plug-ins the intent to obscure potentially malicious components by hiding them under legitimate file names, such as Google’s.

“Such a technique is widely used by malware developers that inject malicious code into applications that have legitimate functionality,” they said.

In China, about three quarters of smartphone users are on the Android system. Apple (AAPL)’s iPhone has 25% market share, according to Daniel Ives of Wedbush Securities.

Sergey Toshin, the founder of Oversecured, said Pinduoduo’s malware specifically targeted different Android-based operating systems, including those used by Samsung, Huawei, Xiaomi and Oppo.

CNN has reached out to these companies for comment.

Toshin described Pinduoduo as “the most dangerous malware” ever found among mainstream apps.

“I’ve never seen anything like this before. It’s like, super expansive,” he said.

Most phone manufacturers globally customize the core Android software, the Android Open Source Project (AOSP), to add unique features and applications to their own devices.

Toshin found Pinduoduo to have exploited about 50 Android system vulnerabilities. Most of the exploits were tailor made for customized parts known as the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) code, which tends to be audited less often than AOSP and is therefore more prone to vulnerabilities, he said.

Pinduoduo also exploited a number of AOSP vulnerabilities, including one which was flagged by Toshin to Google in February 2022. Google fixed the bug this March, he said.

According to Toshin, the exploits allowed Pinduoduo access to users’ locations, contacts, calendars, notifications and photo albums without their consent. They were also able to change system settings and access users’ social network accounts and chats, he said.

Of the six teams CNN spoke to for this story, three did not conduct full examinations. But their primary reviews showed that Pinduoduo asked for a large number of permissions beyond the normal functions of a shopping app.

They included “potentially invasive permissions” such as “set wallpaper” and “download without notification,” said René Mayrhofer, head of the Institute of Networks and Security at the Johannes Kepler University Linz in Austria.

Suspicions about malware in Pinduoduo’s app were first raised in late February in a report by a Chinese cybersecurity firm called Dark Navy. Even though the analysis didn’t directly name the shopping giant, the report spread quickly among other researchers, who did name the company. Some of the analysts followed up with their own reports confirming the original findings.

Soon after, on March 5, Pinduoduo issued a new update of its app, version 6.50.0, which removed the exploits, according to two experts who CNN spoke to.

Two days after the update, Pinduoduo disbanded the team of engineers and product managers who had developed the exploits, according to the Pinduoduo source.

The next day, team members found themselves locked out of Pinduoduo’s bespoke workplace communication app, Knock, and lost access to files on the company’s internal network. Engineers also found their access to big data, data sheets and the log system revoked, the source said.

Most of the team were transferred to work at Temu. They were assigned to different departments at the subsidiary, with some working on marketing or developing push notifications, according to the source.

A core group of about 20 cybersecurity engineers who specialize in finding and exploiting vulnerabilities remain at Pinduoduo, they said.

Toshin of Oversecured, who looked into the update, said although the exploits were removed, the underlying code was still there and could be reactivated to carry out attacks.

Pinduoduo has been able to grow its user base against a backdrop of the Chinese government’s regulatory clampdown on Big Tech that began in late 2020.

That year, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology launched a sweeping crackdown on apps that illegally collect and use personal data.

The Personal Information Protection Law stipulates that no party should illegally collect, process or transmit personal information. They’re also banned from exploiting internet-related security vulnerabilities or engaging in actions that endanger cybersecurity.

Pinduoduo’s apparent malware would be a violation of those laws, tech policy experts say, and should have been detected by the regulator.

“This would be embarrassing for the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, because this is their job,” said Kendra Schaefer, a tech policy expert at Trivium China, a consultancy. “They’re supposed to check Pinduoduo, and the fact that they didn’t find (anything) is embarrassing for the regulator.”

“Probably none of our regulators can understand coding and programming, nor do they understand technology. You can’t even understand the malicious code when it’s shoved right in front of your face,” a cybersecurity expert with 1.8 million followers wrote last week in a viral post on Weibo, a Twitter-like platform.

Hundreds of thousands of Czechs have rallied in Prague to protest against a perceived government attempt to stifle media freedom.

The gathering on Sunday to save the mandatory licence fees that fund Czechia’s public broadcasters drew more than 350,000 people, making it one of the biggest in the country’s history, organisers said.

Many people wore triangular paper hats similar to an animated TV character called Večerníček, a children’s bedtime story mascot who has become a symbol of the protest. Others carried banners saying: “We won’t give media away.”

The event was held at the capital’s Letná Plain, the cradle of the 1989 Velvet Revolution which ended more than 40 years of communist rule in the country.

It marked the latest challenge to the prime minister, Andrej Babiš, and his coalition of populist, hard-right nationalist and Eurosceptic parties, and follows weeks of demonstrations in towns and cities and a strike by staff at Czech TV and Czech Radio.

Protesters say the plan to scrap licence fees and fund the broadcasters from the state budget would give ministers more leverage over newsrooms and follows the example of Hungary, Poland, Serbia and Slovakia, where current and previous rightwing populist governments eroded media independence.

A series of protests has been held since late October, when the first reading of the bill to scrap the licence fees was pushed through in the middle of the night.

The Million Moments for Democracy group, which organised the demonstration, said the authorities would now grasp the level of opposition. “We do witness here something extraordinary today,” said Mikuláš Minář, the lead organiser. “It’s just a beginning.”

In a statement, the group demanded that the government withdraw its plan and expressed concern that Babiš and his coalition partners posed a threat to democracy. “Babiš and his government obviously think they will get away with anything … that they can ignore experts, silence critics and treat democratic institutions like their property,” the group said.

The proposed changes, which would cut state television and radio budgets by about 15%, have drawn fire from international media organisations.

The European Broadcasting Union, the European Federation of Journalists, Reporters Without Borders and the International Press Institute have also criticised the government’s media plan, likening it to controversial moves by Slovakia’s prime minister, Robert Fico, and Hungary’s former premier Viktor Orbán.

Babiš, a billionaire who returned to power last year, said protesters were riling up the public with unfounded claims about threats to media freedom.

Last week, he published an op-ed on what he called the “seven lies” being peddled by the opposition over the licence fees, which together add up to 205 koruna (£7) a month.

“Anyone who talks about ‘nationalisation’ [of public media] is deliberately lying,” he wrote, describing the criticism as “hysterical outbursts”. “Under no circumstances will we interfere in editorial work.”

Babiš heads a coalition that includes his ANO, or YES, movement, the rightwing Motorists for Themselves and the anti-migrant and pro-Russian Freedom and Direct Democracy party. Despite the protests, ANO still enjoys the backing of about 30% in opinion polls.

#130. Estonia to close all border crossings with Russia after attack on drone maker

Estonia will close all its border crossings with Russia from 1 December after a suspected sabotage attack on a drone maker in the Baltic state over the weekend, which authorities said was ordered by Russia’s GRU military intelligence service.

On Saturday morning, eight people were detained during an attempted attack on Threod Systems, a defence company that manufactures long-range attack and reconnaissance drones. No injuries or damage were reported after the company’s security team responded to the incident.

Estonia’s internal security service, Kapo, said on Sunday the attack was carried out “with the aim of causing an explosion and setting the building on fire”. Authorities have requested a court order for five of those detained to remain in custody, Estonia’s public broadcaster ERR reported.

The Estonian prime minister, Kristen Michal, said the border closure was needed to reduce the risk of further attacks and “send a clear message that attacks on Estonia’s defence industry and defence capabilities” were unacceptable.

He linked the attacks with Estonia’s continuing support for Ukraine. “Russia’s aim is to make us afraid, make us hesitate and weaken our support for Ukraine,” Michal said.

“Estonia’s response is the opposite. We will strengthen our security, work even more closely with businesses and our allies, increase pressure on Russia and continue supporting Ukraine.”

The Estonian foreign minister, Margus Tsahkna, echoed the warning, saying: “Russia must understand that hostile actions against Estonia come with consequences. Attempts to intimidate, destabilise or undermine our security will not go unanswered.”

Estonia operates three border crossings with Russia – Narva in the north, and Koidula and Luhamaa in the south. The authorities did not specify how long the closure would be.

It is the second attempted attack on an Estonian defence company that authorities have attributed to Russia, after an arson attack at Milrem Robotics in August.

In September, Kapo said it was confident that the incident was “a deliberate and pre-planned attack carried out on behalf of Russian special services”.

The agency also said it fitted a pattern of Russian attacks targeting defence and drone companies, particularly those with links to Ukraine.

#66. Russia's mega-rich are already imagining a future after Putin

Russia's mega-rich are already imagining a future after Putin

Hours after launching his full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022, Vladimir Putin gathered Russia's richest men in the Kremlin's cavernous Yekaterininsky Hall.

More than three dozen of them sat huddled along a wall, wearing face masks because of the Covid pandemic. One Russian newspaper reporter described them as "pale and sleep-deprived". On entering, Putin sat down at the opposite side of the hall, creating a vast space between him and the businessmen. As usual, he was wearing no mask.

Using his typically coded language, he said he expected them to support the war.

"I would like to call on you to show understanding of what is going on," he said, "and work together with the government to find the tools which would support production, the economy, jobs."

In the Kremlin-published video of the meeting, none of the invited billionaires said a word.

That silence has become a feature of the Russian super-rich. Over the decades of Putin's rule, they have learnt that the price of piping up could be high. In 2003, oil magnate Mikhail Khodorkovsky, Russia's richest man at the time, who was funding a number of opposition groups, accused Putin of presiding over rampant corruption. He spent the next 10 years behind bars, convicted of tax evasion, fraud and embezzlement - a warning to other would-be critics. He denied the charges.

The silence of Russian businessmen at a Kremlin summit with Putin in 2022 perhaps demonstrated their fear of the president

And even if any of the tycoons objected to the war, they have tended to keep their thoughts to themselves.

But even in that silence, there are murmurings: some members of Russia's once-mighty business elite seem to be imagining a Russia after Putin. They are wondering if, once the president has left office or died, the country might look more like it did in the 1990s, when the oligarchs enjoyed the fruits of power - or even if a healthier politics may take root.

The journalist Arkady Ostrovsky, a prominent Kremlin-watcher at The Economist newspaper who recently spent hours interviewing one of Russia's richest men, says he feels that a "historic, political shift is happening" in terms of how the ultra-rich view the future of the country.

The collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991 gave birth to a new, powerful force on the domestic scene: people who took advantage of the end of communism and became extraordinarily rich, and then wanted to control politicians for their own benefit. They were dubbed "the oligarchs", from Ancient Greek for the "few who rule".â¯ They had money, which back then meant power.

This era of the "wild 1990s" was when the so-called "new Russians" burst onto the scene. Huge state companies came up for grabs, and men with the right connections became millionaires - even billionaires - almost overnight. Corruption was rife, cronyism rampant, and contract killings frequent.

But almost immediately after Putin was first elected as president in 2000, he subjugated the oligarchs. He had little interest in sharing power with anyone else. He could afford to stand up to the billionaires because Russia's economy was getting back on its feet, and the central government was getting stronger.

Some oligarchs developed a distinctive dress sense in the 1990s, as later satirised by this St Petersburg artist

Bill Browder, a financier who used to be the biggest foreign investor in Russia, and was banned from the country in 2005 after campaigning against corruption, says the Russian tycoons cowered and wilted in front of Putin - for example at the Davos economic forum, in the pre-war years when they were still welcomed there.

"I can remember seeing oligarchs walking around like they own the place, the most arrogant and self-confident individuals. And then Putin showed up, and it was just remarkable to see how these supposedly alpha-male-on-steroids individuals turned into absolutely subordinate, almost desperate schoolchildren," he tells the BBC.

Since the outbreak of war in 2022, the Kremlin has kept Russia's rich businessmen in line with a ruthless policy of carrot and stick. Critics of the war have seen their assets seized, while loyalists have been rewarded with lavish government war contracts: today there are more Russian billionaires than ever (155 in total, according to Forbes magazine). â¯Their collective wealth has reached a new high of nearly $700bn (Â£525bn).

But even for those tycoons who have stayed loyal and been rewarded, all of that money doesn't seem to be making them happy. Some are growing restless.

Their billions do not go as far as they used to: domestically, they cannot buy much political influence.

"They have no cards, they have no game," exiled politician and analyst Dmitry Nekrasov says. "They're rich because they've got something to do with Putin, not the other way round."

And outside of Russia, they are ostracised because of the Western sanctions imposed on Russia after its full-scale invasion.

"They're treated like low-level criminals. They can't fly their private jets. Their yachts can't park in all the best harbours in Europe. It must be very awful and humiliating for them," says Browder, the avowed Putin critic.

And so, quietly, some of their minds are turning to what happens after Putin leaves the scene. That's what I'm told by well-placed analysts, who are immersed in the world of these tycoons.

There's no immediate prospect of the president's departure, and there's no reason to think he's in bad health. But Putin, who turned 74 last month, has served in the Kremlin for a quarter of a century; people in Moscow know that his exit will come one day. When asked by Reuters last year if he intends to serve until 2036, when his constitutional term technically ends, Putin replied: "Only God knows if we have enough health."

One prominent oligarch of the 1990s was Boris Berezovsky, who fell out with the Kremlin and lived his later years in the UK

If Putin resigned or died in office, the prime minister, Mikhail Mishustin, would take over. But Mishustin has not exhibited any presidential ambitions (that's probably a key reason he was appointed). That would create something of a power vacuum.

In that scenario, Russia-watchers I have spoken with envisage a few potential scenarios.

Some in the West, and no doubt within Russia, hope that democracy might flourish in a post-Putin Russia, with government led by the people, rather than by a new cadre of oligarchs or a single autocrat. But this seems like the least likely path, analysts say. Key democratic institutions have been eroded or destroyed over Putin's rule. "Russia is so used to violence, it has grown unaccustomed to standard democratic procedures," says Alexandra Prokopenko, an exiled Russian journalist and analyst.

Another path is more autocracy. A different figure could replace Putin and continue his culture of repression. But Prokopenko thinks this wouldn't be another strongman dictatorship - at least not right away. These things take time, she says: at first, Putin's successor would be weaker than him, with fewer resources.

And then there's the third option: tycoons re-assert their power and Russia returns to something resembling a 1990s-style system of oligarchy. Dmitry Nekrasov, a former Kremlin adviser and opposition politician who now lives in exile, says under this scenario, the country would be governed by a handful of powerful elite clans in alliance with business interests.

This last option, of course, is the one that many tycoons want. "They'd like to return to a time when they were listened to, when they were not stripped of assets and when they understood the rules of the game," says Prokopenko, who has spent a lot of time speaking to Russia's richest people.

Though their power has been seriously curtailed since the 1990s, it's still worth listening to the tycoons: given their immense wealth, it seems likely that sooner or later they will try to re-assert their political influence.

Already, some are "trying to be friends with the right sort of security officials or ministers", says Nekrasov, the exiled politician. But he says any further steps are "too dangerous to take".

He says the behaviour of the tycoons in coming years will depend in part on their finances. Those who have lots of liquid cash will be more confident to "play political games" and jockey for position, preparing for a post-Putin country. But those whose wealth is dependent on state contracts or government goodwill will feel too afraid, in case they lose everything, he says.

Prokopenko says there's already been a shift in how the businessmen talk about Putin. They used to nickname him "Nachalnik" (which translates as "boss"), as well as "Perviy" ("first"), or by his initials "VV" - all nicknames that convey a degree of respect. Now they call him "Ded", with what Prokopenko calls "a pinch of contempt". "Ded" translates as "oldie" or "granddad" - a much less reverent name.

Some rich Russians are pining after a "return of politics into people's lives", she says, with less repression, more open speech, and healthier competition between parties and politicians.

But at the moment, she says, they are too afraid to make that happen due to Russia's stifling atmosphere. "I thought that it's an absolutely Stalinistic type of fear. It's absolutely irrational," she says.

An interesting clue for what tycoons might want from a post-Putin future came from Andrey Melnichenko, who is known as the world's fertiliser king and is currently Russia's eighth richest man.

Earlier this year, he surprised observers by giving an interview to The Economist - a rare thing for a rich Russian to do, especially to a publication from a country the Kremlin considers unfriendly. They also published an essay written by him.

In parts of the interview, Melnichenko repeated Kremlin talking points. He said that Ukraine is a battlefield between Russia and the West, and he thinks the West ignored Russia's security concerns and threatens its sovereignty.

But then it got more interesting. Melnichenko did not call for Putin to go, but - and it's a big but - he argued for a change that would bring about an end to one-man rule in Russia. He said Russia needs to engage more with the world.

Prokopenko thinks the tycoon was trying to stir a public conversation. It was a "call on rich people like him to start talking", she says.

The journalist who interviewed him, Arkady Ostrovsky, tells me the pair had "tense and heated discussions" in "hotels and cafes, mostly drinking coffee, lots of coffee, sometimes smoking". He says the tycoon seemed to be "genuinely thinking" about his answers, rather than giving politician-style spin.

Nothing in Russia is simple, and most observers think the interview probably happened with some form of approval - implicit or explicit - from the Kremlin. We don't know why authorities would have been happy to authorise the unusual call for change; it's possible they were trying to show that some degree of pluralism still exists in the country.

No tycoons came out to support him. Still, the journalist feels that Melnichenko was not speaking for himself alone, but also for his fellow businessmen.

Some tycoons feel ostracised because of Western sanctions, unable to fly private jets and visit Western resorts like Saint-Jean-Cap-Ferrat in southern France, which used to be popular with rich Russians

Some of them are hoping that after Putin's departure, the country might become an "oligarchic republic", as journalist Ostrovsky puts it - something like how it looked in the 1990s, with the business elites in charge.

Based on his years of reporting on Russia, Ostrovsky says that many of the country's mega-rich currently feel a "sense of complete dead end, which this war has led Russia toâ¦ [an] absence of the future".

But even if the tycoons are thinking about the future, there's a slim prospect of them acting any time soon, it seems.

Browder, the Putin critic, says that although many of them are unhappy, they're unlikely to conspire against Putin while he is still alive. "They understand that if they were to do anything that would challenge Putin, he would kill them. And Putin is a very paranoid little man."

Mike Calvey, an American financier who used to be a key investor in Russia, agrees. "What can they do besides just go straight to the prison and lose their assets? Russia's richest men used to be able to pull strings at the highest levels of power. Once Putin is gone, there is no doubt they will try to claw back their past influence. The question is: will remnants of the current regime let them?

See My World, a day dedicated to independent creators from across the African diaspora in the north of England, will take place at the Contact theatre on 21 November.

The festival is part of See My World’s year-round programme showcasing pan-African arts, culture and events.

It reflects the determination among creatives and activists in Manchester to build on the legacy of the 1945 Pan-African Congress in Manchester – which connected luminaries of postwar independence movements with a shared vision of liberation – after last year’s 80th anniversary commemorations.

See My World’s CEO and artistic director, Tunde Adekoya, said this month’s film festival was taking inspiration from across the spectrum of pan-African thought, including the works of Kenyan writers John Mbiti, an Anglican theologian who died in 2019, and Ngũgĩ wa Thiong’o, the author of the groundbreaking 1986 book Decolonising the Mind who died last year.

Both writers rejected the colonial conceit that African thought was inferior, stressed the importance of community and saw reclaiming African ways of knowing as essential to a people’s self-understanding.

While Mbiti sought common ground between African religions and Christianity, Ngũgĩ was a Marxist. He argued colonialism was motivated by cultural and economic conquest, and that European powers used language as their sharpest instrument. Ngũgĩ famously abandoned English to write in Gikuyu, a Bantu language mainly spoken in Kenya and criticised the role missionary Christianity played in colonial rule.

Adekoya said: “It’s a day dedicated to pan-African and global majority films from the north of England with exclusive premieres of documentaries we’ve been working on, which weave together expressions of liberation through art forms like poetry and dance.

Starting with the Guardian’s own history, Guardian journalists explore the legacies of enslavement and reparative justice around the world

“The whole idea is that it’s like a playground in search of strategies of liberation, using the festival as a way to explore different philosophies.

Film-makers have been invited to submit films lasting up to 30 minutes from across genres, with no screening fees charged.

As well as submissions, the festival will feature documentaries produced by See My World. Adekoya added: “The idea is that we would go against the norm of how film festivals are, because when we think about decolonisation, we think about how structures have been created to alienate and exclude communities.

“So we thought our film festival would look more at how we make film accessible and how stories that deserve to be seen get seen. Throughout the day we will have different activities, from exhibitions to explorations of our performances, discussions, Q&As, insights into directors’ processes, and workshops.

“We’ve been looking for literature and concepts that inform us and give us an understanding of who we truly are. And as works from thinkers like John Mbiti have come into our sphere, they have really emboldened us to think differently about our place in the world and what we do.”

#2. Zimbabwean author Simbarashe Kundizeza cleared of using AI to write prize-winning story

After a month-long investigation it was concluded that Kundizeza’s story The Blue Fighter, which won the £10,000 Caine prize, was ‘human-written’

The Zimbabwean author Simbarashe Kundizeza has been cleared of using AI to write a prize-winning short story, after the prestigious Caine prize for African writers concluded that his entry was “human-written” after a month-long investigation.

Kundizeza’s story The Blue Fighter, which charts the declining fortunes of a family after the father invests in fish tanks, was announced as the winner of the £10,000 prize on 6 October.

Five days later, the Atlantic published an article titled “Yet another literary prize winner seems to be AI”, in which the author and journalist Vauhini Vara said the AI-detection software Pangram, which itself uses AI, had deemed the story to be almost completely artificially generated.

The Caine prize chair, Ellah Wakatama, said in a statement: “From our detailed investigation, we feel the evidence is clear: The Blue Fighter was human-written. We now look forward to awarding and celebrating Simbarashe Steyn Kundizeza … before the end of the year.”

She said: “When concerns were raised, we have a duty as an organisation to look at them carefully and fairly,” adding that the prize had taken a “human-centred” approach to its investigation.

“Our review included interviews with the author, the 2026 judging panel and The Shallow Tales Review, where the story was first published,” Wakatama said. “It also included independent and expert-led metadata analysis of drafts written by the author.”

Kundizeza, who had strongly denied using generative AI to write the story, said he was “relieved” that the allegations had been dismissed after the investigation. “It was not necessary in the first place, I am happy to put all this behind me and continue with my writing journey,” he said.

Vara’s article prompted outrage online, with many claiming that Black writers, and Africans in particular, were being unfairly singled out by allegations of the use of AI.
