#79. Suspected Chinese spy balloon was able to transmit information back to Beijing

The Chinese spy balloon that transited the US earlier this year was able to capture imagery and collect some signals intelligence from US military sites, a source familiar with the matter tells CNN.

The balloon was able to transmit information back to Beijing in real time, the source said, and the US government still does not know for sure whether the Chinese government could wipe the balloon’s data as it received it. That raises questions about whether there is intelligence the balloon was able to gather that the US still doesn’t know about.

Still, the intelligence community has not been overly concerned about the information the balloon was able to gather, the person said, because it is not much more sophisticated than what Chinese satellites are able to glean as they orbit over similar locations.

A US intelligence official told CNN on Monday that “although analysis of the wreckage of the High Altitude Balloon remains ongoing, thus far, its flight over the United States does not appear to have provided critical new insights to the People’s Republic of China.”

The US also knew what the balloon’s path would be and was able to protect sensitive sites and censor some signals before the balloon was able to pick them up, officials have said.

As CNN has reported, the US intelligence community last year developed a method of tracking what it says is a fleet of these Chinese balloons operating across the globe, controlled by the Chinese military.

The FBI is still examining the balloon, but so far officials have been able to glean additional information about how the device worked, including the algorithms used for the balloon’s software and how it is powered and designed.

CNN has reached out to the National Security Council at the White House and the Pentagon for comment. NBC was first to report on the new intelligence.

The balloon first crossed into US airspace over Alaska in late January before passing through Canada and down into Montana, where it hovered for a few days, leading the US to believe it was trying to surveil sensitive military sites, like Malmstrom Air Force Base in Montana. It was eventually shot down by the US off the East Coast on February 4, and the incident further escalated tensions between Washington and Beijing, including the postponement of a diplomatic visit by US Secretary of State Antony Blinken to China.

A senior State Department official said in February that, as the balloon floated across the US, it “was capable of conducting signals intelligence collection operations.”

Gen. Glen VanHerck, the commander of US Northern Command and NORAD, said at the time that the US “did not assess” that the balloon “presented a significant collection hazard beyond what already exists in actionable technical means from the Chinese.”

The surveillance program, which includes a number of similar balloons, is in part run out of the small Chinese province of Hainan, officials told CNN. The US does not know the precise size of the fleet of Chinese surveillance balloons, but sources tell CNN that the program has conducted at least two dozen missions over at least five continents in recent years.

Roughly half a dozen of those flights have been within US airspace, although not necessarily over US territory, according to one official familiar with the intelligence.

China has maintained that the balloon was actually just a weather balloon thrown off course, and the US has been assessing the possibility that it was not deliberately maneuvered into the continental US by the Chinese government, CNN has reported.

Still, China did maintain some ability to maneuver the balloon, officials believe. And once the balloon was over Montana, China appeared to take advantage of its position to loiter over sensitive sites and try to collect intelligence.

#73. Beijing Queer film festival cancelled after harassment from authorities

Forums for discussion of feminist and LGBTQ+ views face growing intolerance from Chinese state, say experts

Beijing’s annual independent queer film festival has been cancelled after what is understood to have been a campaign of sustained harassment from authorities.

Beijing Queer film festival, which was due to begin on Friday in China’s capital, is one of the country’s oldest independent film festivals.

In a statement posted on Instagram late on Thursday, the organisers said: “We regret to inform you that the Love Queer Film Week … has been cancelled. We sincerely apologise for any inconvenience and disappointment that the cancellation of this event may cause to those who planned to participate or had been looking forward to the film week.”

Police had increased pressure on the festival through repeated phone calls in recent weeks, it is understood.

Founded in 2001 by a group of Peking University students, the festival grew to become the only film festival in mainland China to offer a public forum for films and discussions exploring LGBTQ+ issues.

The Chinese authorities have grown increasingly intolerant of open discussion of feminist or LGBTQ+ issues. Film festivals in particular attract particular scrutiny because they provide an opportunity to platform narratives that sit outside the Communist party’s worldview.

Beijing Independent film festival, China’s most prominent independent film festival, was closed down in 2014 after the authorities detained the founders and shut off the electricity supply to the venue. Last year, an independent Chinese film festival that was due to be held in New York was cancelled after harassment from the Chinese authorities, an incident which raised concern about transnational repression.

Maya Wang, the deputy Asia director for Human Rights Watch, said: “The Chinese government under Xi has been relentlessly tightening social and ideological control, and so any activities outside of those sanctioned by the party-state are subjected to intensifying scrutiny and often also official harassment.

“The authorities have put more pressure on what little is left of China’s civil society, as well as the LGBT communities and independent film-makers. A queer film festival basically faces all of these overlapping pressures.”

Hongwei Bao, a professor of Chinese studies at the University of St Andrews who focuses on LGBT issues, said that the festival was “a beacon for LGBT resilience” in China.

“The shutdown of the Love Queer Cinema Week has a chilling affect on the Chinese queer community,” Bao said.

In June this year, screenings of films with LGBTQ+ themes that were scheduled to be held in Beijing during Pride month at the Institut Français, a French cultural centre, were cancelled because of police harassment.

Despite the increased pressure, Beijing Queer film festival remains a popular event. Several international film-makers whose work had been selected for the festival had travelled to China to participate. Last year, the festival received 200 volunteer applications, four times the number received in 2024.

Beijing’s public security bureau could not be reached for comment.

#68. How crypto hacking funds isolated North Korea’s nuclear programme

Cryptocurrency theft is estimated to generate half of the country’s foreign currency income

North Korea plays by its own rules. The isolated, secretive and autocratic country is largely cut off from the world’s economic system – but when it comes to generating foreign earnings it relies on some of the world’s most sophisticated hacking.

Experts, such as TRM Labs, quickly attributed the extraordinary $6.3bn (£4.7bn) Binance cryptocurrency theft to TraderTraitor, an elite North Korean hacking unit that has also been known as the Lazarus Group. Though the link may not be proven yet, the pattern of behaviour is familiar.

North Korea has a history of cryptocurrency theft dating back to 2017, focusing on the sector’s relatively lax security controls as a way of stealing money and generating revenues for its military, including its nuclear weapons programme.

Kim Jong-un, its dictatorial ruler, controls a stockpile of about 50 nuclear warheads, and has been supplying troops and weapons to help Russia in its war in Ukraine. But North Korea is also extremely poor: 40% of the population are undernourished according to the UN and nearly one in five of its children are stunted.

A UN panel of experts, reporting to the security council, said in the spring of 2024 that it was reviewing “58 suspected cyber-attacks” by North Korea “on cryptocurrency-related companies between 2017 and 2023” worth $3bn.

The panel said the cash was estimated to generate half of North Korea’s foreign currency income. One unnamed country went further with its estimate: the stolen billions fund “40% of the weapons of mass destruction programmes”.

The suspected crypto hacks amounted to as many as 17 in 2023 alone: thefts valued at $750m, and disturbingly, the sums involved are only increasing. The world’s largest previous crypto hack took place in February 2025, when $1.5bn was stolen from the Bybit exchange – the work, the FBI said, of TraderTraitor.

Ciaran Martin, a former head of the UK’s National Cyber Security Centre, argues that North Korea appears to have targeted crypto because traditional banks have developed sophisticated defences that the fast-growing and lightly regulated sector has yet to match.

“Over the last decade, hackers have walked off with nine- or 10-figure sums from crypto hacks on more than 30 occasions. The number of traditional banks who have suffered a loss of more than $100m as a result of a cyber-attack in the same period is zero or one,” Martin said.

North Korea was behind one of the last large cyber thefts on a traditional bank – an attack on Bangladesh Bank in February 2016, where North Korean hackers tried to illicitly move $951m from an account the bank had with the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, taking advantage of time differences between the two.

A piece of luck prevented most of the money from being sent to an intermediate account in the Philippines. The corresponding bank was based on Jupiter Street, which shares its first word with the name of a sanctioned Iranian ship, so most of the transfers were held up. In the end, $81m was stolen.

The isolated state has long been accused of engaging in criminal activities to survive, including the forgery of high-quality $100 bills, known as “supernotes” in the 1990s, and the production and trafficking of crystal meth for export in the 2000s.

The UK has been rarely targeted. In 2019, North Korean hackers did try to take control of cash machines linked to 56 financial institutions to make them pay out sums well in excess of normal. British officials spotted what was afoot, alerted the banks and the hack was quietly suppressed with no media coverage.

The irony is that the internet in North Korea is severely restricted, with access denied to the general population. Maths and science are pushed at school, however, and at university some of the best students are recruited into hacker groups, such as Lazarus, operating under the auspices of the country’s Reconnaissance General Bureau spy agency.

Hacking is closely tied to the all-powerful state. In December 2014, it emerged, to the embarrassment of Sony Pictures, that film stars Jennifer Lawrence and Amy Adams were paid less than their male counterparts in American Hustle. It was one of thousands of embarrassing emails leaked from the Hollywood studio through a hack.

The attack, by North Korea’s Lazarus Group, was conducted because Sony had funded a film, The Interview: a Seth Rogen comedy that depicted the assassination of Kim Jong-un. Amid the chaos of the revelations, Sony tried to cancel the movie, then had to U-turn on its retreat, releasing it online and to a handful of cinemas, after being criticised for caving in to North Korean pressure.

#57. The US-China trade war seems to have subsided – but the sense of calm is misleading

A truce has cooled the trade conflict, but neither country has done anything to limit the outbreak of new hostilities

Donald Trump’s trade wars have largely fallen off the headlines. Fears of new tariffs flare up from time to time when the US president goes off the rails – for instance, threatening to end US trade with countries with which it has a deficit unless the Federal Reserve cuts interest rates. But the sense that the world’s two largest economies are plowing ahead into an overt economic conflagration has taken a back seat to other, more salient fears, about Trump ending American democracy, AI killing us all and stuff like that.

The sense of calm is misleading. Under the radar, the world is marching toward an economic war of potentially epic proportions, one that, history suggests, could well spill over into the real thing.

The truce reached by Washington and Beijing last year in their escalating trade conflict, which has been extended until next January, lowered the heat between the two – putting an end to the escalation of tit-for-tat tariffs and opening a door to some as yet undefined future trade deal.

But neither China nor the US has done anything to establish the mutual assurances that could limit the outbreak of new hostilities. Rather, they are both focused on gaining the upper hand in what seems like an inevitable next round, in a standoff that both sides have defined as a national security priority, essential to ensure economic, technological and military supremacy.

The new arms race is different from those in the past. Rather than building weapons to blow each other up, China and the US are focusing on ensuring the resilience of the international supply chains on which they rely and figuring out how to constrain – and if push comes to shove, throttle – the supply chains essential to the economic and technological progress of their rival.

The paired strategies go back at least to the Covid pandemic, when China’s Xi Jinping called on the nation to “tighten international production chains’ dependence on China, forming a powerful countermeasure and deterrent capability against foreigners who would artificially cut off supply [to China]”.

Early in the Biden administration, national security adviser Jake Sullivan articulated the American goals: to limit China’s access to a narrow set of advanced technologies with military applications without upending the global economic order. He described the strategy as building a “small yard” of forbidden tech surrounded by a “high fence” of trade and investment controls.

By now, the conflict has breached the bounds of these initial rationales. Restrictions on American tech exports, investments and technology transfers to China have proliferated over the last half decade. And they are hitting not only China’s military capabilities but slowing its technological progress writ large. And Trump’s wanton deployment of tariffs is not helping.

China, on the other hand, is becoming increasingly assertive, leveraging its overwhelming dominance over the supply of critical minerals that are essential to hi-tech industries for civilian and military uses. Last year, it stunned the Trump administration by throttling exports of rare earths and magnets made from them to retaliate against US tariffs and efforts to restrict China’s access to high-end semiconductors and the lithography machines needed to make them.

It has relaxed some of these controls, but it maintains many other restrictions. While they have largely been imposed to retaliate against American actions, they have also been used to hit other countries for a variety of policies it dislikes. The stakes are high. The International Energy Agency has estimated that if China were to block the US’s and Europe’s access to rare earth magnets, it could cost their economies about $1.5tn per year.

Where does the supply chain war go from here? The Trump administration is pouring hundreds of millions into projects to mine and process rare earths and other critical minerals, hoping to close China’s lead. It is also doubling down on efforts to limit China’s access to frontier semiconductors and to bar it from buying the machinery needed to make them.

China, on the other hand, has announced a range of hi-tech export restrictions, covering things such as artificial intelligence chips and solar power technology. Moreover, it has threatened to punish countries that work to reduce their dependence on its mineral inputs.

One hopes there is a path to a peaceful entente. This would probably require some shared understanding of what constitutes a legitimate national security risk that may warrant actions to block supply chains. Defining the “yard” is essential. Frustrations of standard commerce should not be dealt with on a wartime footing.

China will probably dominate renewable energy technologies, especially the solar power industry, into the foreseeable future. It may also dominate the electric vehicle industry. This might hurt in Brussels and Washington, but it’s not worth a war. China, on the other hand, will have to accept that its mercantilistic economic strategy is not tenable for the rest of the world. Its combination of massive export growth with zero import growth is not sustainable.

This might seem doable. And yet the odds that the world economy will remain at peace seem slim. One might recall the Axis powers’ efforts to seize territories as the League of Nations curtailed their access to raw materials in the early stages of the second world war. Or FDR’s blocking of sales of American scrap steel and oil to Japan, which probably contributed to Tokyo’s decision to attack Pearl Harbor. Today, American fighter jets, submarines and missiles use rare earth magnets. What would happen if China blocked access to these materials?

The moment is strange. It’s not that long ago that trade was proposed as perhaps the most powerful tool to deliver world peace, building positive-sum interdependence among nations. That’s over. Interdependence has become a vulnerability. As China and the United States fight over who will control the next generation of technology, trade has become a theater of war.

#41. Fossilised red blood cells found in 115m-year-old dinosaur’s bone, say scientists

Chemical tests on Aratasaurus bone identify presence of heme, the organic compound that allows haemoglobin in blood to carry oxygen around body

It could be the opening of a Jurassic Park rip-off: scientists examining ancient dinosaur bones find what appear to be fossilised red blood cells, and traces of the molecule that gives the beast’s blood its distinctive crimson hue.

But that is what researchers are claiming to have discovered after analysing the hind leg bones of a small, meat-eating dinosaur called an Aratasaurus that lived and died in what is now north-eastern Brazil 115m years ago.

Electron microscope images of the dinosaur bones revealed small, round structures that resemble red blood cells in fine channels where blood vessels once ran, while chemical tests identified the presence of heme, the organic compound that allows haemoglobin in blood to carry oxygen around the body.

Such claims are controversial because biological tissues and molecules can break down fast, leaving little or no trace in ancient fossils. But if confirmed, it would open up entirely new avenues for scientists, allowing them to investigate dinosaur blood, metabolism and even their activity levels.

“We are seeing a mineral and molecular echo of the dinosaur’s original biology,” said Prof Kliti Grice at Curtin University in Western Australia who led the research team. “Fossilised red blood cells with distinct chemistry related to heme have never been found in situ in any fossil, let alone a dinosaur. This is a world first.”

The fossilised remnants of the Aratasaurus were discovered in a mine in 2008 and have since been held in the National Museum of Brazil, where researchers continue to study them. While analysing the dinosaur’s hind leg bones, Grice spotted the structures that resembled red blood cells.

The scientists’ suspicions that they were looking at red blood cells were bolstered when chemical tests came back positive for heme and its breakdown products. Tests also found traces of the amino acid building blocks for collagen, a key protein in connective and other tissues.

If the structures really are fossilised red blood cells, as the researchers contend, it raises the question of how they were preserved. According to Grice, a manganese compound called hausmannite, which formed in and around the blood cells, may have lent a helping hand by stabilising them prior to fossilisation. The same process might have helped preserve the amino acids, she added. The study appears in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

The work is already stoking debate. Prof Mary Schweitzer at North Carolina State University in the US, who has discovered remnants of blood vessels and soft tissues in dinosaur fossils, is not convinced Grice and her colleagues have found red blood cells. One reason for her doubt is that the dinosaur’s red blood cells should have a “nuclear bulge” making them appear dome-like on both sides. The structures Grice found are concave, suggesting they might be something else.

Schweitzer was more persuaded by the discovery of heme and collagen amino acids in the bone and said there was so much more to learn from exceptionally well-preserved fossils. Schweitzer added: “The conventional wisdom in palaeontology that all endogenous organics would disappear in a million years or less is being routinely challenged, and will continue to be as technological approaches to palaeontological specimens are more routinely applied.”

Another question the research raises is what other biological material might lurk in fossils collections around the world. On this, Grice urges extreme caution, adding that her work does not imply that large biological molecules such as proteins or DNA live on in ancient fossils. “Any extraordinary claim needs extraordinary analytical evidence,” she said. “The aim isn’t to find Jurassic Park DNA, it’s to determine how much genuine molecular information deep time can preserve.”

“The really exciting implication is that extraordinary molecular information may already be sitting in museum collections around the world, waiting for us to ask new questions of old fossils,” she added.

In cyber-security, a "honeypot" is a digital decoy - such as a fake computer network or file - deliberately set up to look like a vulnerable target to lure and trap hackers.

By monitoring these traps, security experts can safely study an attacker's methods without putting real systems at risk.

According to activity gathered after the first wave of cyber-attacks, the second wave which hit several European countries was highly targeted.

"The fact that all these fridge freezers were hit simultaneously suggests planning and coordination," says Ryan Dewhurst from Spanish cyber-security company Previdian.

The entry point to the affected freezer software products was not difficult to exploit as they were poorly protected, with known attack methods already floating around online.

But experts like Dewhurst say the hacker or hackers had to gather the information about the devices' public IP addresses to create a target list then take time to plan which methods to use and when to execute them.

"Given the reported timing of when the devices had their temperatures increased, which seems to have been overnight for maximum damage - this is further evidence that the attack was planned and co-ordinated," he said.

Another cyber firm which has deployed similar honeypot tech to lure the hackers into revealing their tactics is Strathclyde-based Lupovis.

It had honeypots which appeared to be located around the world, and started seeing activity 10 days before the second wave of cyber-attacks.

Intriguingly, the team saw hackers scanning and probing their fake freezer sites only in Europe.

"We saw none of our honeypots in places like Africa or South America targeted at all," said Lupovis founder Dr Xavier Bellekens.

A third company - French cyber-defence firm Sekoia - also noticed scanning activity prior to the second wave of cyber-attacks, but said it could have been automatic scanning without malicious intent.

In a report to its customers, it said evidence suggested the nature of the attack aligned with activity of Russian hacktivists or the Russian government, which is being accused of carrying out so-called "hybrid warfare" cyber-attacks across Europe.

But Sekoia's research also points to Iranian regime hackers or hacktivists too.

"Iranian-aligned actors have an established record of targeting internet-exposed industrial control systems and previous incidents involved the same equipment in Israel and the US - both adversaries of Iran," the report said.

"Furthermore, as NoName057 is not known to effectively conduct operations beyond DDoS attacks and has a history of making false attributions, we remain sceptical of their alleged claim of responsibility."

Distributed denial of service (DDoS) attacks work by flooding a website or online service with internet traffic in an attempt to throw it offline, or otherwise make it inaccessible.

Sekoia also said the possibility remains that all three waves could have been carried out by different and distinct groups or copycats.

#13. Sinkholes, scarred land and scapegoats: the scourge of illegal gold mining in Johannesburg

There is no end in sight for the drivers of illegal mining, while police action appears ineffective

Cora Bailey has been encountering illegal miners ever since she first started treating injured animals in the townships around Roodepoort, west of Johannesburg, in 1991. But as the price of gold has soared, so too has activity in the abandoned mine shafts that mark the area.

In late September, dozens of zama zamas – the name given to illegal miners in South Africa, meaning roughly “to take a chance” in Zulu – arrived to dig for gold across the dirt road from her charity, Claw (Community Led Animal Welfare).

Soon, they were taking water for their panning and processing from the organisation’s two 25,000-litre water tanks, which are filled irregularly by the city government for the 100 or so rescued and injured animals on site.

“We’ve asked them really nicely and haven’t ever refused them water to drink … But, for the first time ever, we closed the gate at 12 [midday], because we literally had about two buckets of water left,” Bailey said in mid-October.

Police told her that the scale of the illegal mining was too big for them to do anything about, she said at the time. About a week later, officers sheared away the grass that had partly hidden the zama zamas. But within a few days a smaller group had returned, although Bailey said they were now taking Claw’s water only for drinking.

South Africa’s police regularly trumpet their success in tackling illegal mining. On 19 October, they said they had arrested 71 suspected zama zamas across the country in the previous week.

However, researchers put the number of illegal miners in the tens of thousands. In 2019, Marcena Hunter, a researcher at the Global Initiative against Transnational Organised Crime, estimated that a tenth of South Africa’s gold production, worth about $1bn, was being exported illegally.

Police operations have also led to tragedy. In 2025, 93 bodies were pulled from a mine shaft in Stilfontein, after officers blocked food and water from being delivered underground.

Meanwhile, there is no end in sight to the dynamics driving illegal mining, including the high price of gold, poverty, unemployment, corrupt politicians and police profiting, and the failure of mining companies to fulfil their legal obligation to seal mining shafts when they cease operating.

South Africa’s modern history is tied to mining. In 1884, gold was found on a farm, sparking a gold rush that led to the creation of Johannesburg. Gold production peaked in the 1970s, but South Africa was the world’s largest producer, a title it held for more than a century, until 2007.

In 2009, Morris Viljoen, a geology professor at the University of the Witwatersrand, estimated that the reef that gave the university its name had produced more than a third of the gold ever mined globally. Claw is housed in the old offices of the Durban Deep mine, which shut at the turn of the century, having once lent its name to an Elton John song.

The city government claims there is still 40,000 tonnes of gold underground in the region. But, with the remainder lying ever deeper, the costs of recovering it have risen.

Mining companies are supposed to seal up old mine shafts and rehabilitate the environment around them. However, in 2025 there were “6,100 derelict and ownerless mines and 1,170 unsafe mine openings”, the mining minister, Gwede Mantashe, said in response to a parliamentary question.

Zama zamas have stepped into those voids left in the earth. While there is no comprehensive data on them, experts agree that a majority are from neighbouring countries, principally Lesotho, Mozambique and Zimbabwe.

The skyline south of Johannesburg is punctuated by the pale yellow ridges of mine dumps. Increasingly, the ground is also caving open.

For politicians seeking votes in local elections on 4 November, the culprit is clear: illegal miners. In May, Kenny Kunene, the Johannesburg council member responsible for transport, took journalists on a tour of sinkholes that he claimed were the fault of zama zamas, including a section of Wemmer Pan Road that had completely collapsed.

The road was once the main artery linking the city’s central business district to the south-eastern suburbs. In one section it has completely disappeared, now raw earth pitted with the holes where illegal miners have been digging.

However, when it comes to sinkholes, experts say zama zamas are being scapegoated.

Rosalind Morris, an anthropology professor at Columbia University, who has worked extensively with illegal miners, noted that the porous dolomitic rock to Johannesburg’s south and west becomes unstable when water is removed.

“The biggest culprit of sinkholes in South Africa is formal industrial mining, which dewaters at a scale of hundreds of millions of gallons of water removed from underground every day,” she said.

“There are areas where there is a lot of residual gold, which are being very intensely mined by zama zamas, and some of that definitely causes structural collapse, there’s no doubt about it. But the idea that this was invented or caused for the first time by zama zamas is completely fallacious.”

Zama zamas are also frequently blamed for violence in mining areas, including a September mass shooting in Wedela township, west of Johannesburg. However, David van Wyk, the lead researcher at Bench Marks Foundation, said culpability laid with the often-feuding gangs that controlled access to the shafts.

“The guys working in the mine are actually super-exploited cheap labour … The guys with the guns are the guys who take the gold,” he said. “They don’t go underground.”

In Khutsong, about 50 miles (80km) west of Johannesburg, illegal miners, local and foreign, kept the township afloat, said Vusi Cele, the youth secretary for the local branch of Mining Affected Communities United in Action, a campaign group that advocates for “artisanal miners” to be granted mining licences. “Without those rents, it’s a ghost town,” he said.

The African National Congress is on course for a bruising result in South Africa’s local elections on Wednesday as voters go to the polls amid deep discontent over water outages, power cuts and crumbling infrastructure.

The former liberation movement, which Nelson Mandela led to power in 1994 at the end of apartheid as South Africa’s first black president, is still likely to be the largest party. However, polls showed it getting less than a third of the vote, down from 45.6% at the last municipal elections in 2021 and 40.2% in 2024’s national elections.

In Kensington, a diverse Johannesburg suburb that has sustained repeated water and power outages in recent weeks, voters said they wanted to see change. The city is currently led by Dada Morero, the ANC mayor, in coalition with the far-left Economic Freedom Fighters (EFF) and the rightwing populist Patriotic Alliance (PA).

“Service delivery is bad, it’s horrible,” said Tshepiso Makotse, 26, a call centre worker. “Water, electricity – everything, basically.”

Makotse said she had split her two votes, casting one for the centre-right Democratic Alliance (DA) local councillor and her party vote for the leftwing uMkhonto weSizwe (MK) party of the former ANC president Jacob Zuma. “I just want change,” she said.

Terrence Pieters, a retired boilermaker, said: “The infrastructure in Johannesburg is shot.” He declined to say which party he had voted for, other than it was “one of the offshoots of the DA”.

“I would like to see things get better and great people in charge,” said the 72-year-old. “But … there’s no one party that’s going to take over here. It’s going to be a circus, it’s going to be a coalition.”

Vote fragmentation means the ANC could still end up leading many city coalition governments while losing control of others.

Unstable coalitions are already a feature of many of South Africa’s 257 municipalities, and analysts point to their high turnover as a major reason for poor service delivery.

Johannesburg, a city of 4.8 million people that has faced water shortages for more than a week, has had 10 mayors in as many years. The neighbouring municipality of Ekurhuleni has had three mayors since 2021. To the north, in Tshwane, which contains the capital, Pretoria, the mayoralty has rotated between parties four times since 2020. In October, more than 800 people contracted a gastrointestinal illness from contaminated water in Bronkhorstspruit, in east Tshwane.

The ANC’s national leadership is open to working with the DA in Gauteng, according to South African media, mirroring the government of national unity, where the two parties are the largest in the national coalition. However, local ANC leaders are reportedly not in favour of a DA tie-up. Currently, the ANC governs in various coalitions across the province’s three metros, including under an ActionSA mayor in Tshwane.

In Johannesburg, the DA’s Helen Zille has led a spirited campaign, with viral stunts including the 75-year-old snorkelling in a pothole and kayaking along a flooded street. However, the former Cape Town mayor is still viewed with suspicion by many, especially black voters, due to past comments appearing to excuse colonialism. Some voters also point to soaring housing costs in Cape Town, which has been run by the DA since 2006, as a reason not to vote for Zille.

The former Johannesburg DA mayor Herman Mashaba, who now leads ActionSA, which is polling around 10%, has ruled out working with Zille in Johannesburg. Mashaba has called Zille a “racist”, an allegation she has always fervently denied. The ANC, for its part, has three possible mayoral candidates for the city, amid factional infighting.

In eThekwini metro, which contains the port city of Durban, the MK party was polling above 40%. Zuma, its 84-year-old leader, who has weathered and denied numerous corruption allegations, is set to take many of the votes of his former party, which won 42% in eThekwini in 2021 and is now polling around 10%.

The DA looks set to retain its majority in Cape Town, although probably with less than the 58% of the vote it won in 2021, as unaffordable housing turns more voters against the party.

South Africa’s electoral commission will have a week to announce the final results of Wednesday’s vote, which chooses councillors and parties, with mayors not directly elected. Parties will then have two weeks from the announcement to form councils and elect mayors.

He said in later interviews with the New York Times and Fox News that he would accept the election results. Trump told Fox he was a "little bit surprised" by the election results, but noted that the president's party typically fared poorly in midterm elections.

Republican Senator Jon Husted at an election event in Ohio, where the Senate seat flipped in favour of Democrat Sherrod Brown

Since 1934, the party in the White House has only gained seats in three midterm elections. While the Democratic gains in 2026 did not set a record, the party did win dozens of House races, state governor contests and Senate seats, including in states where the party had not been competitive for decades.

Before Tuesday, Democrats had not won a Senate seat in Kansas since 1932, and had not won a statewide race in Texas in more than three decades. The party also notched surprise upsets in other reliably red states like South Carolina that were thought to be safe seats for Republicans just weeks before Election Day.

Trump and Vice-President JD Vance held dozens of campaign rallies in the run-up to the midterms. The principal Trump-aligned Super PAC, MAGA Inc., spent hundreds of millions backing Republican candidates.

Trump allies said it was unfair to hold him responsible for the party's election losses. They pointed to the history of midterm elections, and said the party was saddled with weak Republican candidates in battleground states like Georgia, Michigan and Texas.

Ken Paxton, the Republican Senate nominee in Texas, ran "the worst Senate campaign in the country from either side of the aisle," said another Texas Republican operative, who spoke on the condition of anonymity. "If Ken's campaign knocked on 100,000 doors I'd be shocked," the source said. The person added that Paxton's victorious Democratic opponent, James Talarico, ran "a really good and smart campaign."

Democrats 'got their wish': Voters react to midterm results

Others defended Trump's autumn campaign schedule, noting that he had to balance his day job of running the country with midterm travel.

"He's the president. He's not going to spend his time campaigning 24/7," said Krish Mathrani, the Michigan Republican Party's youth chair. Mathrani said Trump's campaign rallies helped drive up enthusiasm among Republican voters. "President Trump is a turnout machine," he said.

That's true in presidential elections. But Trump's vaunted turnout operation has failed to deliver in elections when he's not on the ballot, and 2026 proved no different.

Voters rejected Trump's argument that the economy was better on his watch and that he was achieving his aims in the war with Iran. The effort by Trump to portray Democrats as extreme socialists also fell flat, as voters handed victories to moderates like Roy Cooper in North Carolina.

"That just didn't work with Cooper," said a Cooper campaign official. "Trying to tag him as some socialist liberal, that wasn't going to fly."

Republican supporters at a watch party in Iowa, where Democrat candidate Josh Turek won a Senate seat

Trump often seemed disinterested in the midterms over the summer, the period of time when Republicans in Congress shifted their focus to the election.

Trump was consumed by the war with Iran abroad and his construction overhaul of Washington at home. The projects included the under-construction ballroom and a helipad on the South Lawn of the White House, renovations to a local golf course, a proposed memorial arch near Arlington Cemetery and renovations to the Kennedy Center.

Events like a UFC match at the White House on Trump's 80th birthday in June created the impression that the president was more interested in feting himself than winning the midterms, several sources said. Trump also repeatedly downplayed the rising costs of gas and often cast the affordability issue as a Democratic hoax.

Trump turned his full attention to the election in September at the Republican Party's midterm convention. The event was a gamble: Republicans hadn't held a midterm convention in decades, and it was an open question whether voters would tune in.

Trump dominated the convention in Dallas, frustrating Republicans who told the BBC they wanted to highlight the party's midterm candidates.

In his rambling keynote speech, Trump pledged to give $5,000 (Â£3,700) to Americans if Republicans retained control of Congress and raised eyebrows with an endorsement of Paxton which included a lengthy criticism of the candidate's lack of charisma and physical appearance.

"Obviously, that's never helpful," Robin Armstrong, Texas' Republican National Committeeman, said of Trump's critique of Paxton. "You're not going to stop what President Trump says. We just have to kind of live with that."

The convention failed to attract a large television audience, and Democrats were quick to call it a failure. "It backfired for Republicans because nobody paid any attention to it," said Terri Burke, the Texas Democratic Party's executive director.

Trump's penchant for straying off-message continued when he hit the campaign trail in the final weeks before Election Day. He made headlines for praising Ohio Republican Senator John Husted's physique, and drew criticism for calling Abdul El-Sayed a "jihadist" in the Michigan Senate race.

Mike Rogers in Michigan was among the other losing candidates - having been beaten to a Senate seat by Democrat Abdul El-Sayed

As Trump criss-crossed the country stumping for Republican candidates, the White House contended with controversies in Washington. Katie Zacharia was ousted after just five days as White House press secretary, and Kimberly Guilfoyle resigned as the US ambassador to Greece after reports surfaced that she asked a donor to pay her credit card debt.

Trump also faced a backlash in October for striking a deal to buy Russian diesel. The agreement, which blindsided members of Congress and drew condemnation from some Republicans, came just weeks after Trump signed a sanctions bill aimed at cutting off Russian oil and gas revenue.

Democrats used the deal and other scandals swirling around the White House to make the case for retaking control of Congress. The messaging resonated in the end, as Democrats flipped both chambers of Congress in convincing fashion.

A second White House ally said that the president's political aides projected optimism until the final days of the election, even as it became clear the party was headed to defeat.

Now, as Republicans take stock of the loss, several said they were ready to look ahead to the 2028 election.

"Whether that's JD Vance, or Marco Rubio, or Ted Cruz, we've got a pretty big bench," said Scheffler, the state's RNC member. "It'll be a wide open race."

Peppin, the Republican strategist, said the midterms results demonstrated the party needed a new direction. "I sincerely hope that we've turned the page from the Trump era, and that we'll have new players, new issues and a new focus moving forward."

#2. British healthcare worker with Ebola evacuated from DR Congo to UK

Patient is ⁠in ​stable condition and will be transferred to a specialist ‘high consequence’ infectious disease unit

A British healthcare worker with Ebola is being evacuated from the Democratic Republic of the Congo to the UK for medical treatment, it has been confirmed.

“A British citizen currently in the DR Congo has tested positive for Ebola,” a spokesperson for the UK government said in a statement on Wednesday. “The UK has well-established procedures for dealing with cases like this, and those processes have been activated … The risk to the UK public from Ebola remains low.”

The patient, whose identity is unknown, is in a stable condition and is expected to arrive in the UK on Wednesday or Thursday before being transferred in a specialist ambulance to a “high consequence” infectious disease unit, according to an official document seen by Reuters.

A humanitarian organisation sent the worker to DR Congo to help in the response to the outbreak, which is the second largest on record. It is the first case involving a British national contracting Ebola in the current outbreak.

The Royal Free hospital in London has a high-level isolation unit for infectious diseases such as Ebola; other centres are in Newcastle and Liverpool.

Ebola is a highly contagious, severe and often fatal illness. It is spread through direct contact with body fluids such as the blood or vomit of infected people, or dead bodies.

The current outbreak, declared on 15 May by DR Congo’s health ministry, has surpassed 10,000 cases, and 5,000 deaths. It is the 17th in DR Congo since Ebola was identified, was caused by the Bundibugyo virus, and has become the largest and deadliest in the country’s history. The Bundibugyo virus has no approved treatment, though vaccine trials are under way.

Since the outbreak started, it has spread to Kenya and Uganda in east Africa, while last month Nigeria confirmed a case in the capital, Abuja, after a woman who arrived from Uganda tested positive for the virus. She, along with a close contact, were evacuated to Switzerland for treatment.

In an interview with the Guardian last month, Dr Jean Kaseya, the director general of the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), said the “unprecedented” spread of the Ebola outbreak meant it could reach the UK and other countries.

“We want all countries to know that they are not safe if they don’t take appropriate measures,” he said. “Each country, not only in the African region, but all continents, must now increase the preparedness level and the possibility for treatment.

“It is the first time three African regions have been affected [by Ebola]. The geographic extension of this outbreak is unprecedented.”

Other patients who have been medically evacuated for specialised care since the start of the outbreak include two sent to Germany and one to the Netherlands. Another case was reported in France in July.

British health authorities say the risk ⁠to the wider public from imported Ebola cases remains low and the NHS has procedures in place to manage suspected and confirmed infections.
