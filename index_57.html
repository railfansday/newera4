#21. Caribbean nations fear economic impact of shuttering citizenship program after EU threats

St Kitts and Nevis to wind down citizenship-by-investment program after EU warns of end to visa-free access

The decision by St Kitts and Nevis to end a lucrative programme offering citizenship to wealthy foreigners in exchange for investment threatens its economy and ability to support people in need, the premier of the island of Nevis has warned.

On Friday, Terrance Drew, the prime minister of the twin-island Caribbean nation, announced he would wind down the citizenship-by-investment (CBI) programme, after the European Union threatened to end visa-free access for St Kitts and Nevis.

Mark Brantley, the premier of Nevis, which has autonomous self-governance in some aspects, said he understood that the “country was given very little choice”, but did not “support the winding down of the program”.

The 42-year-old CBI programme, which St Kitts and Nevis pioneered, allows wealthy foreigners to obtain citizenship and ultimately a passport by investing in infrastructure and other development projects. It has brought billions of dollars into the country and in some years accounted for the largest share of government revenue.

In June, the European Commission wrote to the five Caribbean nations that currently operate CBI programmes: St Kitts and Nevis, Dominica, Antigua and Barbuda, Grenada and St Lucia. It warned that countries may lose visa-free access to its 29-country Schengen area if they did not phase out CBI.

In Friday’s statement, Drew blamed concerns over the programme on mismanagement by previous administrations, including years of underselling to undercut other countries, a controversial prison project, weak escrow protections and investment sums not being properly paid into the citizenship unit.

He said his government had led major reforms to the Caribbean’s programmes with international partners, including the US treasury, but argued that continuing the programme risked visa-free travel to parts of Europe and access to international banking. That could disrupt services allowing visitors to use overseas cards and families abroad to send money home.

Brantley, however, argued it was unfair for the EU to force St Kitts and Nevis to make a move without offering assistance to navigate the transition to other industries.

“I have not yet seen from the EU any corresponding suggestions of assistance. And we have to realise that these citizenship-by-investment programs, not only in St Kitts and Nevis, but … smaller countries of the Caribbean, have contributed significantly to our gross domestic product and also to ensuring that our people … can get adequate access to water and to education, to healthcare and the like,” he said.

Brantley described the 1 June 2028 deadline the EU gave the country to phase out the lucrative programme as “frankly ludicrous”.

In his statement, Drew said the CBI programme “did a lot of good, but it also created a danger”.

“When one source pays for half or more of everything, you are at its mercy. If it stops, everything stops. That is putting all of your eggs in one basket,” he added.

The prime minister said the government aimed to diversify its economy by strengthening tourism, yachting, and digital and creative industries, among others. He added that the sale of clean, geothermal energy was “at the heart of it” with drilling and exploration already planned.

“I will not pretend that the road is easy. Less CBI money means we must spend wisely and plan carefully. We will. Our plan is to make this change by growing our economy. Raising taxes is not part of that plan,” Drew said.

Antigua and Barbuda has also expressed willingness to end the programme, though it is trying to negotiate a longer transition period. The five Caribbean governments have taken differing positions on the EU demand. According to local media, Grenada has indicated it wants its programme to continue until 2032, while Dominica and St Lucia have responded to Brussels without publicly committing to ending their programmes.

The spotlight is on €5bn in EU “soft” loans to Egypt – lending with easy repayment terms, known as macro-financial assistance, that is meant to be available only to countries that respect human rights and democracy. While the EU conditions on democracy and rights are vague, the loans impose much more specific requirements on Egypt to carry out economic reforms.

Separate data sources – from leaked EU documents and deleted from an Egyptian government website – raise questions about the European Commission’s claims that Egypt met targets linked to social welfare programmes, which enabled the EU to release €2.5bn in assistance.

In confidential documents, the commission told EU lawmakers that Egypt had “broadly fulfilled” and then “fulfilled” two separate targets to increase the numbers of poor people on social welfare programmes between 2024 and 2026. But data points to a net reduction of 85,734 households as the Egyptian government removed people from its welfare programmes rather than expanded coverage.

If Egypt had expanded the programmes in line with the EU’s apparent conditions – and not removed any households – about 245,000 households, equivalent to roughly 909,000 people, would be getting these benefits. (The Guardian used World Bank data to make this rough calculation).

According to the leaked documents, the commission wanted Egypt to “further expand cash transfer programmes”, but despite a net reduction in beneficiaries it gave the green light anyway to two separate “soft” loans totalling €2.5bn.

In response to the Guardian’s findings, one of the European parliament’s leading spokespeople on EU-Egypt relations, the Dutch MEP Tineke Strik, said she was introducing a question to the European Commission demanding clarification.

The Takaful (Solidarity) programme gives poor families cash payments as long as recipients have regular health visits and good attendance at school. The Karama (Dignity) programme gives unconditional cash payments to people living with a disability, older people, orphans and widows.

#1. Fears grow that US will impose sanctions on ICJ judges ahead of Myanmar genocide ruling

A ruling against Myanmar could set precedent for court’s judgment on Israel’s actions in Gaza

Fears are growing that the US could impose sanctions on judges at the international court of justice, including this year’s Nobel peace prize winner, Navi Pillay, if the court rules on Thursday that the Myanmar government committed genocide against the Rohingya.

The ICJ has never found a state responsible for committing genocide, and a significant factor in previous cases has been how to prove “intent” in a crime defined as acts committed with the “intent to destroy, in whole or in part” a national, ethnical, racial or religious group.

Lawyers representing Rohingya survivors say patterns of behaviour by Myanmar’s military, including killing people as they fled, show clear attempts to destroy the group during assaults on Rakhine state in 2016 and 2017.

It is the role of independent judges to interpret the genocide convention. However, judges at the court have taken advice on how to protect their assets from potential US sanctions, as a genocide ruling could be interpreted by Washington as lowering the “legal threshold” for the crime.

The Foundation for Defense of Democracies, an influential thinktank close to the White House, has predicted a genocide ruling will lead to the US imposing sanctions on the ICJ.

The US and Israel fear the final judgment may clarify how to establish a government’s intent to commit a genocide, and so set a precedent that makes it more likely the court will later rule that Israel committed genocide in Gaza.

Myanmar, which has denied carrying out a genocide, claims its actions targeted a military uprising by insurgents.

The ICJ, sometimes described as the UN’s top court, is responsible for settling interstate disputes, unlike the international criminal court (ICC), which prosecutes individuals accused of war crimes and other international crimes.

The US has already imposed crippling sanctions on judges at the ICC after the court issued arrest warrants against the Israeli prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu. The US has also announced sanctions against the entire ICC as an institution, which it described as a “rogue court”. Washington fears the ICC could go after US troops.

The Gambia, as a litigant, was entitled to appoint an ad hoc judge in the case against Myanmar and chose the South African lawyer Pillay, who in October was awarded the Nobel peace prize. She is required to act as a jurist and not an advocate for the Gambia.

Mike Waltz, the US ambassador to the UN, has sent a warning shot to Pillay and the 15 ICJ judges, saying Washington “will not allow globalist, faux judges to weaponise the law against our troops”.

Waltz asserted last month: “The ICJ ad hoc judge Navi Pillay is tipping the scales on the ICJ’s current proceedings on Myanmar, intending to lower the legal threshold for proving genocide so that weakened and false standard can later be weaponised against American and allied soldiers whenever they raise a finger against terrorists that hide behind civilians.”

In remarks addressed to Pillay, he said: “We see your agenda and we’ll be watching the ICJ’s handling of these proceedings very closely.”

In a speech last Friday, the US secretary of state, Marco Rubio, said: “For this global elite, international law … is an instrument to advance their interests under the pretence of universal principles.”

He warned the ICC against quoting laws to “men with swords”.

Israel has previously criticised adverse advisory opinions issued by the ICJ and refused to implement 2024 orders requiring Israel to admit more aid into Gaza, and to cooperate with Unrwa, the UN relief and works agency.

In the Myanmar case, a 24-page submission jointly filed by the UK and five other mainly European states, argued that the ICJ, in judging intent to commit genocide, should examine a range of “facts and circumstances” assessed “comprehensively and holistically”.

It also stressed that genocide was not solely defined by killings, and that the treatment of children, gender-based violence and forced displacement were also relevant.

The 15 ICJ judges are elected for nine-year terms by the UN security council and the UN general assembly.

Rubio has given the ICC until April to respond to his criticisms before he imposes sanctions.

On Friday, the ICC formally decided to drop plans to prosecute US soldiers and intelligence agents for potentially unlawful acts in Afghanistan.
