#98. Jensen Huang broke Nvidia’s code of conduct, and his employees may not forget it

When Jensen Huang gave his first all-hands address since returning as CEO, there was an odd emphasis on Nvidia’s code of conduct. He admitted to violating the code, then emphasized that no one was exempt from it. But that “does not mean, the moment you violate a code of conduct, we walk you out the door,” Huang said, according to Wired. “Facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments” still matter. In other words, the code applies to everyone, but breaking it doesn’t automatically cost anyone their job, including, apparently, the CEO.

It’s an awkward stance for a company whose CEO has admitted to breaking its rules. A central part of modern human resources, a code of conduct can seem like a quasi-legal rulebook that lays out unacceptable behavior. But experts say most codes are better understood as a reflection of corporate culture. When an infraction goes unpunished, the main risk isn’t legal but cultural — that employees will feel embarrassed or even angry about the company they work for. That’s the risk Huang now faces at Nvidia.

Matt Plass, chief market strategy officer at LRN, an ethics and compliance training outfit, recently surveyed more than 2,000 workers across different industries for a study on workplace codes of conduct. As Plass sees it, the biggest risk is that employees will become ashamed of their workplace.

“People coming into the workforce now, ethics is incredibly important to them,” he told TechCrunch. “They’re really mindful of the ethical standards of their company. They’re really embarrassed and angry when senior leadership lets them down.”

The consequences of violating the rules aren’t always tangible, but they’re definitely real. Plass gave one example of a company that banned corporate travel to cut costs — only to have its CEO turn up in a VIP box at the Olympics.

“For over a decade afterwards, ‘the Olympics’ was used within that company as an example of the worst thing an employee could do. People would say, ‘Yeah, I shouldn’t have done that, but it’s not like I went to the Olympics.’”

Plass says companies that remove an executive typically do so after an investigation, then try to win back employees. “In most organizations they make a change at the top based on what’s happened,” he said, “and then they will have a hearts-and-minds initiative, to go out to their employee base and their customers and their investors and everybody else, and say, we get it. We’re sorry. We’ve changed. That’s essentially what they do.”

Of course, that playbook is harder to follow at a company like Nvidia, where Huang left without a clear succession plan, and there are serious doubts about whether the company could continue to thrive without him. In cases like that, Plass acknowledges there’s no easy answer.

“I would draw a distinction there between the kind of companies where the CEO is a kind of godhead figure … where the person and the company are almost impossible to separate,” he said. “There it’s much, much harder to deal with.”

In that context, Huang’s stutter-step over the code of conduct is easier to understand. Judging by the employee campaign for his reinstatement, Huang’s return hasn’t created a morale problem so far. But as the coming months put more pressure on Huang and his colleagues, sidestepping the code could end up costing Nvidia something harder to measure — its employees’ pride in the company.

#92. South Africa's Ramaphosa wins court battle over impeachment threat

President Cyril Ramaphosa has faced calls to resign over his handling of the "Farmgate" scandal

South African President Cyril Ramaphosa has won a legal battle to overturn a report that found he may have a case to answer over the theft of large sums of cash from his private farm.

In 2022, an independent panel said Ramaphosa may have committed serious misconduct relating to the theft of $580,000 (Â£443,000), hidden in a sofa, at his Phala Phala game farm in the Limpopo province. He denied any wrongdoing.

Ramaphosa challenged the findings of the report in court after the Constitutional Court ruled that parliament had violated the constitution by blocking moves to impeach him.

The Western Cape high court ruled that the panel's report was unlawful and set it aside "in its entirety".

In its judgment, the court said the panel, appointed by parliament to investigate the allegations against Ramaphosa, had "misconstrued its function, the nature of the inquiry it was to conduct and the recommendation it was required to make".

"It went beyond its lawful remit ... and made several material errors of law. [Therefore] the report of the independent panel ... is declared unlawful and is reviewed and set aside in its entirety," Judge Nolwazi Mabindla-Boqwana said.

The court also declared unlawful the steps taken by parliament in relation to the report.

Political analyst Khanyi Magubane said the court ruling was a victory for Ramaphosa as it gave him "political and constitutional credibility".

She made it clear that the ruling was not a criminal finding on possible improper conduct at the farm but rather an indictment at the independent panel's report.

"[So] he does get significant reprieve ... but there will always be the shadow of the Phala Phala report," Magubane said.

The ruling effectively removes the basis for the parliamentary impeachment process against Ramaphosa, although opposition parties have indicated they intend to challenge the decision.

The scandal, dubbed "Farmgate" by local media, has has dogged Ramaphosa since it emerged in 2022, when former spy chief Arthur Fraser reported the matter to police in a dossier.

The break-in took place in 2020, when thieves allegedly stole bundles of US dollar bills hidden inside a sofa at the farm.

Opposition parties, pictured above, have been vocal in their call for the president to account for his role in the saga

Earlier this month, two people were found guilty of the theft.

Ramaphosa confirmed that a robbery had taken place but said the money had been from the legitimate sale of buffalo from his livestock business.

Several investigations cleared him of any wrongdoing, but parliament began an impeachment proceedings in 2022 and appointed an independent panel to investigate the allegations.

The panel made a series of damaging findings, including that there was "substantial doubt about the legitimacy of the source of the currency that was stolen".

It also concluded that Ramaphosa "has a case to answer".

Ramaphosa initially challenged the report in court but withdrew his application after parliament voted against adopting its findings.

He revived the legal challenge again in May this year after the Constitutional Court, the country's highest court, ruled that parliament had acted unconstitutionally by blocking moves to impeach him.

It ordered parliament to refer the report to an impeachment committee.

In his latest challenge, Ramaphosa argued that the panel had "misconceived its mandate, misjudged the information placed before it and misinterpreted the four charges advanced against me".

The Western Cape High Court's ruling could bring the current impeachment process to an end, unless parliament successfully challenges the decision.

If they do, and the appeal is successful, the parliamentary committee would be able to examine the charges against the president and make a recommendation.

If the committee recommends impeachment, parliament would have to vote on whether to remove him from office. This would require the support of at least two-thirds majority in parliament.

Ramaphosa's Africa National Congress (ANC) has enough parliamentary seats to block such a move.

He is the first president to face impeachment proceedings under the rules introduced in 2018.

Calland said the court's findings now meant that the impeachment process was "dead in the water [and] cannot continue".

He said that any political party that wanted to revive it "would have to come up with a whole new basis" for this.

Calland said political parties who want to hold Ramaphosa accountable would likely seek to appeal the ruling but it was unlikely parliament would also do so.

"So, the president's political career is now free of the cloud of impeachment," he said.

SpaceX was given conditional approval by the Federal Communications Commission on Wednesday to deploy and operate a constellation of up to 1 million satellites for its orbital data center business, a regulatory victory for Elon Musk and his bid to move AI data centers into space.

The FCC authorization was granted despite objections from Amazon, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society, which flagged concerns over orbital debris and congestion, radiofrequency interference, and environmental consequences. Amazon, which is building a competitor to SpaceX’s Starlink service, argued in its petition that the application lacked basic details and described it as a “lofty ambition rather than a real plan.” Despite that lack of detail, Amazon also said the proposal “risks real harm.”

The FCC dismissed some of the key concerns raised by opponents, including potential threats to scientific research, because the agency determined they fell outside its regulatory authority.

Musk thanked Carr in a post on X, writing “Much appreciate. This will be a great asset to America and the world.”

This initial regulatory approval doesn’t mean orbital data centers are imminent.

SpaceX faces a number of technical and economic challenges, including how to dramatically reduce the cost of manufacturing and launching satellites, cool processors in space, and even how to secure financing for chips that are destined for orbit.

The company must also clear other regulatory hurdles. The FCC has not yet approved SpaceX’s plans to handle orbital debris for satellites that would operate above 600 kilometers, for instance. SpaceX requested a waiver of existing FCC rules, which requires satellites ending missions below 2,000 kilometers reenter Earth’s atmosphere within five years of completing those missions. The FCC has given SpaceX time to sort these issues out. Under the approval, SpaceX has seven years to get its first operational satellite into orbit and 14 years to deploy the entire constellation.

#31. Peter Magyar meets Volodymyr Zelenskyy: Hungary and Ukraine make a fresh start after the icy relations of the Viktor Orban era

After years of frosty and hostile relations under Hungary's Kremlin-friendly former PM Viktor Orban, his successor, Peter Magyar, and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy seek to reset their countries' ties.

The location and the gesture were carefully chosen: Hungarian Prime Minister Peter Magyar and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy stood side by side, laid flowers and bowed their heads at the military cemetery in the center of the Ukrainian city of Uzhhorod.

One of those buried there is an ethnic Hungarian who was one of the first soldiers killed in Russia's full-scale war of aggression against Ukraine.

Magyar intentionally chose not to begin his first visit to Ukraine as prime minister at a government building. Instead, his Tuesday afternoon visit began at a place where he could demonstrate his solidarity with Ukraine more clearly than anywhere else.

Uzhhorod, known as Ungvar in Hungarian, is the capital of Ukraine's Transcarpathia region, which is home to around 80,000 members of Ukraine's Hungarian minority. Thousands of ethnic Hungarians are fighting Russian occupying forces on the front lines.

For Hungary, where ethnic Hungarian minorities in neighboring countries traditionally play an important role, the war is therefore closer to home than it is for other European countries.

Magyar described his visit to Ukraine as a "historic day" in a Facebook post. Before the ceremony at the cemetery in Uzhhorod, he said: "I bring with me the solidarity of the Hungarian people. We stand with Ukraine against the Russian aggressor."

Zelenskyy responded on X, saying "We appreciate this gesture of respect toward those who sacrificed their lives for Ukraine's independence."

The meeting between Magyar and Zelenskyy marked a turning point in Hungary's approach to Ukraine.

The two leaders repeatedly shook hands throughout the day. Such scenes would have been unthinkable just a few months ago. The propaganda of Magyar's pro-Russian predecessor, Viktor Orban, portrayed Ukraine as a "mafia state" and "lawless country" on an almost daily basis.

Under Orban, relations between Hungary and Ukraine reached a historic low.

Now the two countries are seeking a political reset, which is important not only for Hungary and Ukraine but for Europe as a whole. After all, Orban's repeated obstruction of support for Ukraine drove the EU to the brink of despair.

During talks in Uzhhorod, the two leaders also agreed to closer economic cooperation.

The talks centered on Hungarian diesel supplies to Ukraine, greater use of Ukrainian gas storage facilities by Hungarian companies, and joint infrastructure projects, including the opening of new border crossings and a direct rail link between Budapest and Kyiv.

Nevertheless, no formal agreements were signed on Tuesday.

At a subsequent news conference, journalists asked questions about sensitive issues between the two countries: minority rights, Ukraine's EU integration and a new EU loan for Ukraine.

Throughout the day, the two men were visibly keen to avoid any discord.

Magyar, for example, described his angry outburst over Ukraine's "Carpathian Eight" initiative in parliament in late September as a "misunderstanding" and suggested that Hungary might, after all, take part.

Zelenskyy's cautious replies to journalists suggest that he knows his country's path to EU membership also runs through Budapest.

One of Magyar's statements on Tuesday was key: "We've finally put a stop to this completely unnecessary and inexplicable political escalation."

Even before taking office in early May 2026, Magyar had promised to reset relations with Ukraine. But the process has not been entirely smooth.

In many respects — but certainly not all — Magyar is distancing himself from his predecessor. He has made no bones of the fact that he sees Russia as the aggressor, but he rejects both arms deliveries to Ukraine and even the transit of weapons across Hungarian territory.

Magyar previously accused Ukraine of treating ethnic Hungarians as second-class citizens — a claim that is not borne out by the facts. He pressed for greater minority rights and for a time blocked the opening of negotiating chapters in Ukraine's EU accession talks.

Magyar also departed from diplomatic convention by choosing the location of his meeting with Zelenskyy in Ukraine: Indeed, it was Magyar who invited Zelenskyy to Transcarpathia.

Magyar's sometimes confrontational tone toward Ukraine can also be explained by the need to avoid appearing too pro-Ukrainian to the Hungarian public.

According to a November 2026 poll conducted by the think tank Globsec, 33% of Hungarians would now support their country providing military aid to Ukraine. Although this is much more than during Orban's time in office, many Hungarians remain skeptical of Ukraine.

That's why Olena Halushka, a Ukrainian political scientist at the International Centre for Ukrainian Victory (ICUV), sees some aspects of Magyar's visit as a concession to public opinion in Hungary.

"Magyar has taken over a deeply polarized country," she told DW's Ukrainian Service. "A visit to Transcarpathia is more of a step for the Hungarian population than for Ukraine."

Ukraine accommodated not only Magyar's choice of location but also Hungary's demands for better protection of minority rights, which were set out in a 10-point plan in June. That's because EU integration is one of the country's top political priorities.

After planned restrictions on the use of minority languages were withdrawn in late 2023, Ukraine's parliament in early October passed a law on minority language rights that in some respects goes even further than European standards.

Hungary subsequently dropped its obstruction of the opening of negotiating clusters for Ukraine's EU accession.

Laszlo Zubanics, chairman of the Hungarian Democratic Federation in Ukraine (UMDSZ, one of two Hungarian political parties in Transcarpathia), told DW that the Magyar-Zelenskyy meeting was "historic."

He particularly welcomed the fact that Ukraine will probably implement practical improvements in the protection of minority rights "not only by the end of 2027, but much sooner, within just a few months."

A Hungarian diplomat who spoke to DW on condition of anonymity also called the meeting "historic," adding that after the Orban government's anti-Ukrainian policies, trust now needs to be rebuilt, and that personal meetings between the two leaders play an important role in that process.

The diplomat went on to say that Hungary not only wants to ensure that the rights of the Hungarian minority are protected, it also has a strong interest in being involved in Ukraine's reconstruction.

Overall, the diplomat concluded, both sides are very keen to expand economic cooperation.

Hungarian political scientist Daniel Hegedus of the Institute for European Politics (IEP) in Berlin told DW that while the meeting was a "symbolic milestone," it will be important to see "how the two political leaders capitalize on the symbolic political capital generated by this meeting."

No observers have suggested that this is the grand finale of reconciliation between Hungary and Ukraine.

Nevertheless, Tuesday's reset sends an important signal to Europe, namely that if the political will is there, a deep gulf that has existed for years can be transformed into a set of diverging interests that can be addressed through negotiations.

Or, as Zelenskyy put it, now there are "all the opportunities to build hopefully constructive relations."

"Granny, grandad and Serezha are gone. Now there is no-one left," it said.

Because not only had I lost three beloved family members, my mother blamed Ukraine for their deaths - the country where I was born and have lived for most of my life.

Mum was born in Siberia. From the moment Moscow launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, she had supported Russia in the conflict.

Now the Russians occupy almost 80% of Donetsk - the part of the Donbas where my village is. Over the past nearly five years, since Russia's full-scale invasion, entire towns and cities have been razed to the ground, external.

I had already fallen out with my sister Sasha, who had been living in Moscow with her young family when the war broke out. She refused to take sides - I felt hurt and cut her off.

With the help of two other villagers, Misha said they had buried him in the greenhouse.

As for my grandparents, my investigations suggested the events surrounding their deaths may have been more complicated.

Their house, on the eastern side of Dachne, had been destroyed. Neighbours told me they had escaped to another home on the same street.

I eventually reached a woman I'll call Lina, who lived nearby and was the last person, as far as I know, who saw my grandparents alive.

She could not recall what this date was, but knew it was in the second half of January.

At this time, she said, "the Russians were fully advancing - the edges of the village were being shelled. Then the Ukrainian soldiers moved from the edge of the village into the centre. They hid in people's yards and used side streets to avoid drones."

She said two soldiers entered her property. They had not been wearing any identifying insignia but had been speaking Ukrainian, she said - a language that Lina described as "Bandera", a derogatory term for Ukrainians.

She had found the door to the house they had been staying in open, she said, despite the freezing conditions.

With drones buzzing overhead, Lina said she had not dared look inside, but had later heard from neighbours that my grandparents had been found dead. She believed it had been the Ukrainian soldiers who visited her house who had shot them.

Another neighbour, who I will call Maria, described discovering their bodies - my grandmother in bed, covered in a blanket, and my grandfather lying on the floor in a pool of now-dried blood.

Maria also believed Ukrainian soldiers had killed them - because, she said, that she too had felt threatened by Ukrainian troops at the time.

"If they had seen me they would have killed me too."

Her theory was that my grandparents had irritated the troops in some way.

"Your grandparents caught them in a bad mood because your grandfather was always asking to be evacuated. And [the soldiers] were, you know, high. They were taking drugs. So your grandparents got in their way," she told me.

The new local mayor, appointed by the Russian occupiers when they finally took control of Dachne in February, said she had been present at my grandparents' burial the following month, and said half of my grandmother's skull was missing.

These accounts of how they might have died in January 2025 were deeply disturbing, though no-one had witnessed their deaths and the information I was given could not be independently verified.

The villagers I spoke to - now living in occupied territory - did say there had been some tensions between themselves and Ukrainian troops at the time, because the soldiers had viewed those who remained in Dachne as supporters of Russia.

The clinical causes of my grandparents' deaths are unclear.

Their bodies were not exhumed for nearly 20 months, when the local authorities sent them to a morgue in the occupied region, where a basic autopsy was carried out by a forensics expert.

He told me that my grandmother had died of an explosives injury to the skull - but could not specify its cause.

As for my grandfather, it said his body had shown no obvious signs of injury.

I compared two satellite images of the house in which their bodies were found - from just before and just after when they are believed to have died - and could detect no significant damage to the building in the second image.

I also spoke to a villager who said he had buried my grandparents.

He said he did not know anything about who killed them.

"I wasn't there. But why would you kill old people?" he asked.

"Are you ready?" Sasha asks. She rings our mother - who has also moved to Serbia - and puts the phone on speaker.

"Hi, Mum. It's Nastya," I say. First there is silence. Then I hear her crying. "Nastya, my darling, hello," she replies.

#13. Images of asteroid targeted to test Earth's defences reveal stadium-sized crater

The asteroid before (left) and after, showing the crater left behind by a spacecraft's impact

The first images of an asteroid that Nasa deliberately crashed into have revealed a stadium-sized crater on its surface.

The extensive damage was caused when a Nasa spacecraft hit the asteroid Dimorphos in 2022. The asteroid posed no threat, but this was a test of our planetary defences to see if it was possible to knock a space rock off course.

Four years on, a European Space Agency (ESA) spacecraft called Hera is scouring the scene to fully assess the aftermath of the collision.

"The crater is almost as large as the body itself, which is really, really spectacular," said Dr Sabina Raducan, a planetary scientist, who is on the Hera science team.

"It's hard to describe in words all the excitement to actually finally see what happened. And as a crater person, I can also say it's beautiful."

The original 2022 crash test was carried out by a Nasa spacecraft called Dart which hurtled into Dimorphos at 14,000mph (22,000km/h).

It was such a big smash-up that the trail of debris, measuring about 6,200 miles long (10,000km), was spotted by telescopes on Earth and in space.

Dimorphos is about 150m (500ft) wide and was partly chosen as the target because it is one of an asteroid pair - it orbits around a larger space rock called Didymos, which is about 730m (2,400ft) wide.

The crash showed it was possible to change the path of the smaller asteroid.

Before the collision, it took Dimorphos 11 hours and 55 minutes to orbit around Didymos. After the crash, the duration of its orbit had shrunk by about 33 minutes.

Watch: Moment Nasa spacecraft leaves stadium-sized crater in asteroid

A key objective of the Hera mission is to measure the mass of Dimorphos, in order to work out how effective the spacecraft's shove was.

This information would be vital if we ever spotted a space rock that was on a collision course with Earth.

"It would help us to predict what would happen if we had to hit another asteroid," said Prof Simon Green, a planetary scientist who is on Hera's science team.

"We [could] measure its properties and say: 'Well, we need this mass of spacecraft, travelling at this speed, this early, in order to deflect it enough so that it misses the Earth'."

Hera's arrival on the scene is helping scientists answer many more questions too.

Not only did the collision alter the shape of Dimorphos, it also changed the way it moves.

"Before the Dart impact, Dimorphos was more like our own Moon - so it was always showing the same face to Didymos, and it was behaving quite nicely," explained Raducan.

"Now it's very, very chaotic - it's tumbling. Imagine a barrel that you throw down the stairs, and the barrel is rolling and it's moving, and basically that's what Dimorphos does right now around Didymos."

Hera has also taken detailed images of Didymos, revealing its surface in detail.

"The gravity at the surface of Didymos is about 40,000 times less than the gravity on Earth," said Naomi Murdoch, who is on the Hera mission team.

"We can already see some fascinating and complex geology. We can see regions of boulders.

"The smallest details that we can make out in these images are about 3m in size. So that means that all the boulders that you can see with your eyes are at least 3m big, and some as big as 10m. So what we're seeing here is boulders the size of cars and buses sitting on a world with almost no gravity."

The mission has sent back new pictures of the smaller Dimorphos orbiting around Didymos

The Hera mission will spend the next six months studying Dimorphos and Didymos, sending back more images taken from closer up in higher resolution.

"Hera is a sort of Inspector Columbo going back to the crash scene to investigate what is the outcome of this impact," said Patrick Michel, principal investigator of ESA's Hera mission.

The spacecraft is packed with instruments and also has two shoe-boxed sized satellites onboard, which can study the impact site up close, and then later land on the asteroid.

But the first images taken by Hera have also revealed a new challenge. Dimorphos and Didymos are travelling through space surrounded by hundreds of boulders ranging in size from tens of centimetres to metre-size rocks.

This will make it especially challenging to operate the spacecraft and cube satellites through this boulder field.

Asteroids pose a significant threat to our planet.

The biggest, which measures more than a kilometre across, could cause global devastation. But medium-sized asteroids around the same dimensions as Dimorphos could wipe out a city.

"We all know what happened to the dinosaurs, right? And we don't want that to happen to us," said Carolyn Ernst at Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, who has worked on both the Dart and Hera missions.

"The good news for us is that there are far fewer potential threats out there in the Solar System than there were millions of years ago. But there are objects out there."

She said the biggest asteroids have been spotted, and none are heading to Earth.

"The smallest stuff isn't too much of a threat because our atmosphere protects us," she added.

"But there's a size range in the middle where we don't know where all of them are, and it could, if impacted, cause problems for humanity. And if we can find out about that and know about that, well, we sure as heck would want to try to stop that."

Artist's impression showing Hera. It has two small satellites on board to study the asteroid up close

Scientists want to gather as much intelligence as they can about space rocks to make us as well equipped as possible to deal with whatever the Universe throws at us.

A new mission is launching in 2027 to track potentially hazardous asteroids - a space telescope called the Near-Earth Object (NEO) Surveyor.

There are also more visits planned to asteroids, including to one called Apophis, which is about 450m (1,480ft) long.

Apophis was discovered in 2004 and it was initially thought it might hit the Earth as early as 13 April 2029.

But now scientists understand its orbit better, and say it won't hit us for at least the next 100 years. Instead of a crash in 2029, it will fly just 32,000km (20,000 miles) above our surface.

This will give scientists the rare chance to study a space rock up-close.

Two spacecraft will head to Apophis, including Nasa's Osiris-Apex (which previously grabbed a sample from another asteroid called Bennu) and Ramses, a joint mission between ESA and the Japanese Space Agency (JAXA).

#9. US announces new tariffs on host of countries including China

Watch: What we know about Trump's Thanksgiving tariffs

The US has announced a fresh raft of tariffs as President Donald Trump pushes ahead with his signature trade policy.

The new taxes charged on imports to America are set to hit many of the country's biggest trading partners - China, the European Union and 12 other jurisdictions.

The move follows the White House's investigation into the trade practices of other countries in March after the US Supreme Court ruled Trump's previously announced Liberation Day tariffs were illegal.

China is facing the highest rate of up to 40%, followed by Vietnam at up to 35% and then others facing either 20% or 25%.

The new duties are not expected to take effect until next year.

Tariffs are taxes levied on imported goods. The amount is typically calculated as a percentage of the good's value and paid to the government by businesses importing the products from abroad.

Some of these rates will be effectively reduced because of caps on tariffs Trump has agreed to. Individual countries also face different levies.

The proposed tariffs will affect China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Japan, and India.

US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said the latest tariffs issued would "level the playing field for American workers and chart a new, more sustainable course for the world economy".

He claimed that many of America's trading partners had "for decades" adopted industrial strategies which included "targeting manufacturing, wage and labor suppression" as well as "other market distorting practices that tilt the trade balance in their favor while eroding America's manufacturing base".

But economists have warned tariffs can fuel inflation and push prices up for consumers if businesses pass on higher costs, as well as dampen global trade.

Trump has repeatedly threatened or imposed tariffs during his second term, arguing they will boost US manufacturing and create jobs.

Despite a poor result for the Republican party in the midterm elections, the president has continued to press on with his flagship trade policy.

Trump is also seeking to cut the US trade deficit - the gap between the value of goods it buys from other countries and those it sells to them.

Trump's so-called Liberation Day tariffs were struck down by the Supreme Court earlier this year

However, Stephen Brown, chief North America economist for Capital Economics, said the latest tariff announcement was "not as alarming as it looks", due to the taxes "essentially replacing" previously announced tariffs made in the summer.

"The increase in the effective tariff rate looks set to be a touch lower than we've been assuming for our forecasts, although the new tariffs will still provide a marginal boost to prices," he said in a note.

"The key message is that the additional tariff burden is likely to be small, especially as various goods... are also not subject to these new tariffs."

The announcement is likely to increase tensions between the US and China, the world's two biggest economies, who remain locked in a trade dispute and the AI race.

The two countries have also been embroiled in a tit-for-tat tariffs war, which is currently on hold.

Despite last week's meeting between Trump and China's President Xi Jinping, the two sides made no announcement on the trade war truce, which is set to expire on 10 January.

The European Commission rejected the new US tariffs, saying it would investigate the US's reasons for bringing them in. It pointed out that tariffs are limited to the 15% previously agreed.
