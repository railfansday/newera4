#47. South Korea’s high-stakes university exams upended by AI cheating scandal

South Korea has been gripped by revelations of AI-aided cheating, just days before more than half a million students are to sit high-stakes entrance exams

The integrity of South Korea’s career-defining university entrance exam has been thrown into doubt after the top scorer in a recent professional exam admitted using a concealed camera and AI to cheat.

On Thursday, more than half a million people, mostly school students, are due to sit the exam, known as the suneung, at 1,312 centres across the country. The exam runs for a gruelling eight hours over a single day and determines which university a candidate can attend and, in turn, their future prospects.

The country goes to great lengths to ensure the smooth running of the suneung. Aircraft take-offs and landings are rescheduled during the 25-minute English listening paper to minimise noise, while artillery fire and tank movements are suspended.

The stock market opens an hour late and cars are stopped 200 metres from every centre. Extra trains run from 6am, police drive latecomers to their exam halls, and tourist attractions open late to ease traffic. Offices are asked to open at 10am.

But in recent days, exam organisers have faced a renewed threat to the validity of the entire exercise, after the top scorer in this year’s tax accountancy exam- who got just one question wrong out of 160– admitted on Sunday he had cheated by using AI.

Prime minister Han Seong-sook told a cabinet meeting in Seoul on Tuesday that AI-assisted cheating was a “social evil” that the government would “root out”.

“We will block outright the carrying of smart devices during the exam,” she said. The education ministry and other agencies should approach it “as if staking their jobs on it”, she added.

Han said smart devices must be turned off and handed in, while bags left in corridors would be checked for any devices that were still on. Exam officials would also inspect all candidates’ glasses and watches, as well as their collars, sleeves and buttons for concealed cameras.

The government will also send emergency alerts to every phone in the country, which sound at full volume and cannot be silenced, in the hope of catching out anyone who has not handed in their device.

More invigilators will be deployed, and the government said it was preparing to switch off school wifi inside exam centres and public wifi nearby, and that personal hotspots, which cannot be blocked, would be traced through connection records.

The weekend’s cheating revelation captured national headlines in South Korea, stoking concern about the security of the suneung.

In a video posted to his lawyer’s YouTube channel, the exam cheat said he had photographed the questions with a concealed microcamera, sent them to an AI system and read the answers from a smartphone. “I cheated in the tax accountant exam,” he said. “My top placing is not the result of legitimate effort.”

His lawyer handed police a camera, a laptop and a phone. No charges have been brought, and the prime minister has ordered procedures to begin that would void his result and bar him for five years.

The education minister, Choi Kyo-jin, told parliament on Monday that while authorities could screen out the smart glasses already on sale at Thursday’s exam, newer variants could not be dealt with perfectly.

Choi has ruled out postponing the exam, which has been delayed several times before, including in 2017, after an earthquake struck the south-eastern city of Pohang, and in 2020 due to the Covid pandemic.

Smart glasses have been banned in exams since the end of June. In October, several candidates were caught with them while sitting university-run admissions exams. At the suneung, wearing a pair counts as using one even when switched off.

The education ministry looked at using radio-frequency equipment to detect the glasses in exam halls, but concluded conditions varied too much between venues for deployment this year. Checks will instead rely on visual inspection, with at least one dedicated official placed in each exam centre.

Teachers’ unions have criticised the government response as belated. A survey last month of 2,716 secondary school teachers by the Korean Teachers and Education Workers Union showed that 82.3% said they could not tell whether a candidate was wearing smart glasses under the current procedure, with only 3.5% saying they could.

To avoid facing scrutiny on exam day, some candidates are reportedly switching to contact lenses. An optician in Suwon, south of Seoul, said students who had never worn contacts were coming in to ask how to put them in.

Han said on X on Monday that the checks would be handled carefully “so that candidates acting in good faith do not suffer unnecessary anxiety or inconvenience”.

In his video confession, the accountancy test cheat addressed suneung candidates directly. “The suneung is coming up soon,” he said.

“To anyone thinking of cheating as I did: I am going to tell the investigators everything, so please don’t think, ‘I won’t get caught.’”

#37. Datacentres to create 70% more carbon emissions by 2030, IEA report warns

Global energy watchdog forecasts huge rise as US and China turn to fossil fuels to power AI growth agendas

The world’s datacentres will create 70% more carbon emissions within the next four years as the US and China turn to fossil fuels to power their AI growth agendas, according to the world’s energy watchdog.

The International Energy Agency (IEA) warned that datacentres will consume twice as much electricity by 2030 compared with today, with the global expansion of artificial intelligence accounting for three-quarters of this growth.

The Paris-based agency found that the breakneck pace of the industry’s growth means that within four years AI datacentres will consume an extra 350 terrawatt-hours of electricity every year, or more than the annual electricity consumption of Great Britain.

Its influential annual report warned that the world’s energy future was less certain after the impact of the Middle East war, which it has described as the greatest energy market disruption in history.

“I don’t see any credible pathway for the energy world to return to the way it was before,” said the IEA’s executive director Fatih Birol.

Instead, it will be reshaped by the policy decisions governments make after the crisis to meet the increasing demand for energy to power a growing global population, the rising need for air-conditioning and a booming AI economy, the IEA said.

Datacentre developers have emerged as the world’s biggest corporate buyers of renewable energy to help fuel their servers which have a connection to local power grids. Despite this, the report showed that the appetite for fossil fuels is expected to grow even faster, particularly in the US and China.

By the end of the decade, around half of the electricity needed to power China’s AI datacentres will come from coal power plants, according to the IEA. The rest will come from renewable energy, with smaller amounts from nuclear reactors and very little from gas plants.

In contrast, the US is expected to rely on gas to provide about 60% of the electricity for its datacentres by 2030. There were plans for about 180GW of new gas-fired power plants linked to US datacentres in August this year, the agency said. That is almost as much new gas capacity as the total now installed across the European Union.

As a result of the growth in fossil fuel generation for datacentres, emissions associated with this sector were on track to rise by 70% by 2030, the IEA said.

The appetite for gas power among US datacentre developers is more than 16 times higher than in Canada, where the IEA identified plans for 11GW of gas generation to power their servers. In the rest of the world, AI developers have put forward plans for new gas plants with a combined capacity of just 4GW, the agency said.

This is because renewable energy is expected to play a greater role in supplying datacentres outside the US and China. In the EU, the IEA expects datacentres to rely on renewables for about 70% of their electricity by 2030 – followed by nuclear power, gas generation, and a very small amount of coal power.

The agency based its forecasts for the notoriously opaque industry on data showing the annual global shipment of AI servers, which have climbed eighteen-fold since 2020. It also used space-based imagery to track the buildout of 93 large “AI factories” around the world.

Its analysis suggests that the world’s datacentre capacity has climbed tenfold in the last 24 months to 17GW. By the time the AI factories were completed, their combined capacity was likely to be about 50GW, the IEA said.

The world’s existing stock of datacentres, including those with AI-optimised servers, now stands at 120GW, with 80% of this capacity in China, the US and the EU. A further 120GW will be built by the end of the decade under the IEA scenario often considered its central base case, of which three-quarters will be AI datacentres.

#16. Myanmar faces historic genocide verdict at UN court over Rohingya massacres

When the judges of the International Court of Justice read out their verdict in the seven-year-old genocide case filed by The Gambia against Myanmar on Thursday, they could make history.

In the years since the Genocide Convention was signed in 1948, after the Nazi Holocaust, dozens of individuals have been successfully prosecuted for the crime of genocide, in conflicts such as those in Rwanda and the former Yugoslavia.

But no state has ever been found guilty by an international court of committing genocide.

"It would be a judgment of landmark importance if The Gambia were to win this case," says Arsalan Suleman, the lawyer leading the case against Myanmar at the court. "It would give renewed vitality to the 1948 Genocide Convention and to the notion of international legal accountability for crimes committed by a state."

The mass expulsion of the Rohingya population of Rakhine State in August and September 2017 was without question a military operation of exceptional brutality.

I was among a very small number of journalists who witnessed the deliberate burning of Rohingya villages in Rakhine, and also the dreadful human suffering of the hundreds of thousands who fled to neighbouring Bangladesh.

Their accounts of killing, torture and mass rape by Burmese soldiers have been thoroughly documented by human rights groups and a United Nations fact-finding mission, external.

I have on many occasions interviewed ethnic Rakhine people about their animosity towards the Muslim population, and I have documented the systematic obliteration of what remained of Rohingya villages by the Myanmar state.

But the question posed at the UN's top court, the ICJ, is whether all of this amounted to genocide.

Hundreds of thousands of Rohingya fled across the Myanmar border in 2017 as attacks on them grew

The ICJ was established in 1945 to help settle disputes between states, not to try international crimes. However, it was chosen to address the attacks on Rohingyas in Myanmar because there were few other avenues of redress for the victims.

Myanmar authorities have denied accusations of ethnic cleansing or genocide, saying they were only responding to terrorist actions by Rohingya militants with so-called military "clearance operations".

A UN report in 2018, based on hundreds of interviews, said the army's tactics were "grossly disproportionate to actual security threats". It said top military figures in Myanmar must be investigated for genocide in Rakhine State and crimes against humanity in other areas. Myanmar rejected the report.

As Myanmar was not a signatory to the International Criminal Court, it was not possible to file a case there against the officials accused of atrocities in Rakhine State.

Instead in November 2019 The Gambia, backed by the Organisation of Islamic Co-operation, filed a case at the ICJ, arguing that all states are bound by the Genocide Convention, so all have an interest in upholding it.

It accused the state in Myanmar, not just the military, of genocide.

This was a novelty in international law; a state not directly affected by an act, arguing that it had legal standing to make a complaint because it was indirectly affected by any violations of such an important convention.

Myanmar objected to this argument, but the ICJ judges ruled that The Gambia was eligible to file the case.

Min Aung Hlaing, the general who now runs Myanmar, was responsible for the military's operations in 2017

The case drew even more attention when the Nobel laureate Aung San Suu Kyi, who at the time headed the elected government in Myanmar, chose to lead the initial legal defence of her country at The Hague in person, for which she was widely criticised.

She had very limited influence over the actions of the Myanmar military in Rakhine State, but her refusal to condemn them dismayed many of those who until then had admired her courage and adherence to democratic principles.

Since she was deposed by a coup in 2021 and placed under arrest the defence has been led by a new team appointed by the military.

Historically, proving that genocide has been committed has been very difficult. Judges apply a high bar to this.

Typically, where there is no explicitly genocidal programme, like that of the Nazis, the judges require that the evidence presented at court must be such that the only reasonable inference from the actions of the defendants is that they had genocidal intent.

When Bosnia-Herzegovina filed a genocide case against Serbia at the ICJ in 1993, it took the court 14 years to conclude that while incidents like the 1995 massacre at Srebrenica did amount to genocide, there was not sufficient proof that the state of Serbia had genocidal intent, though it was found to have failed to prevent genocide.

Myanmar's lawyers and Aung San Suu Kyi have argued something similar.

Suu Kyi said that The Gambia's team mischaracterised a complex conflict and military operation, and that while she accepted that war crimes may have been committed against the Rohingyas, that is not proof of genocidal intent.

Aung San Suu Kyi's refusal to condemn the Myanmar military's actions in Rakhine State dismayed her admirers

But Arsalan Suleman believes the weight of evidence his team gathered is enough to meet that high bar.

"What we presented to the court was the long history of discrimination targeting the Rohingya, and how that history, and the policies connected with it, provide a background to understand the government's language, rhetoric, propaganda, and policies that were part of the 2017 'clearance operations'," Suleman said.

"We discussed the 1982 citizenship law, and the tightening noose of discriminatory laws that restricted access to education, healthcare, livelihoods, and travel - all of which made life really impossible for the Rohingya and, we argued, was one of several indicators of genocidal intent."

What happens if the court does find in favour of The Gambia?

Myanmar would then find itself tarnished by having committed "the crime of crimes", as sitting on the same bench as Nazi Germany, the Rwandan Hutu militants and the Bosnian Serbs at Srebrenica.

That might seem a setback for Min Aung Hlaing, the general who now runs Myanmar, who above all was responsible for the military's operations in 2017, and who has been trying to improve his dismal international image in recent months.

There is no right of appeal against an ICJ verdict. However, there is also no penalty that follows an ICJ verdict.

The Burmese coup leader is focused mostly on rebuilding relations with neighbouring Asian countries, who are unlikely to pay too much attention to such a verdict, and much less on rebuilding relations with Western countries who do care.

Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh's Cox's Bazar mark the ninth anniversary of their exodus from Myanmar

But for the huge Rohingya diaspora this verdict matters a great deal.

"This is like formal recognition of our suffering," says Mohammed Nowkim, a Rohingya who was driven out of the Rakhine town of Buthidaung in 2017 and now lives in Canada.

"For the Rohingya, this trial is a moment of acknowledgement of our suffering after years of denial. The Rohingya have been waiting for justice for decades, not just this year."

He and other Rohingya activists hope that a ruling in favour of The Gambia will prompt more co-ordinated action by other countries to find a resolution which allows them to go home to Rakhine State, in safety.

More than a million are stuck in wretched camps in Bangladesh, barred from employment as international funding dries up.

But the obstacles to realising this hope are formidable.

Rakhine State has been ravaged by a conflict between the military and the insurgent Arakan Army, which now controls most of the territory. It is supported by the indigenous Rakhine population who are very hostile to the idea of the Rohingyas coming back.

Many Rohingya villages have been completely destroyed. And international support for them has faded over the years. Even an unprecedented genocide verdict at the world's highest court cannot change that reality.

Germany, Austria, Netherlands, Denmark and Greece likely to face challenges to policy criticised as ‘grave moral failing’

Five EU member states have announced plans to set up an offshore centre in Rwanda for people with no legal right to stay in Europe, in a pilot scheme that is expected to face legal challenges and has been denounced as “a grave moral failing”.

The five countries – Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Denmark and Greece – are the first to seek to use the EU’s newly agreed returns law that allows people who have exhausted all avenues of appeal against their removal from Europe to be sent to a third country.

In a statement released on Monday the five EU countries said “concrete negotiations with partner country Rwanda will begin for implementation”, saying the scheme would make “an important contribution to further curbing illegal migration, dismantling the business model of smugglers and relieving the burden on our population”.

Officials hope to send 100 people to Rwanda from early next year to test the model. The scheme will apply to adults whose claims to asylum have been rejected or others with no legal right to live in the EU.

In theory, according to diplomatic sources, migrants would stay in a reception centre in Rwanda for a couple of months, before choosing to integrate into life in the Great Lakes country or return to their home nation.

Unlike the failed scheme pursued by the British Conservative government in Rwanda, or Italy’s agreement with Albania, it will not apply to people pursuing asylum claims.

The reception centre, which would be an open facility, was not intended to house people long term but to accommodate regular flows of people, the countries said. Diplomatic sources said the EU countries were not using British-funded facilities built in Kigali.

The governments involved argue it will protect the European asylum system while removing the incentive for irregular migrants to come to Europe.

Denmark’s immigration minister, Morten Bødskov, whose government has pioneered a tough line on migration widely emulated across the EU, said it was “a huge breakthrough”.

The German interior minister, Alexander Dobrindt, said: “Return hubs offer a positive approach for people without the right to stay and with no prospects in Europe, whose countries of origin do not cooperate in the repatriation process.”

Andy Burnham told reporters last week that the UK had not had direct discussions with European governments involved in return hubs but “we will watch that with interest, learning from what went on in the past. The public want us to do whatever we can to deal with illegal immigration.”

Critics have highlighted the ethical difficulties of striking an agreement with Rwanda’s repressive government, which was found in 2024 by the US state department to allow state security forces and civilian officials to commit human rights abuses with impunity, while also raising concerns about freedom of expression and forced labour.

Spain’s socialist prime minister, Pedro Sanchez, said on Monday the move had no “future or humanity”. “I don’t see any future or humanity in that. It’s not the solution,” Sanchez, an outspoken defender of immigration in a Europe that has tilted to the right in recent years, told Telecinco television.

The French Green MEP Mélissa Camara denounced the agreement between the five countries and Rwanda as “an agreement of shame” and “a grave moral failing”. She said: “Under this agreement, people will be deported thousands of kilometres away from the European Union, with no guarantees regarding the compliance with their rights.”

Alain Berset, the secretary general of the Council of Europe, the continent’s human rights body, said it was not possible “to be so precise a priori” about what any return hubs proposal meant. Speaking to the Guardian before the announcement, he said all members of the Council of Europe were committed to the European convention on human rights “and this must be the lighthouse” for all discussions on return hubs.

“On concrete cases, then we will have to see if this is compliant with the convention,” he said. He stressed that “all countries must be able to define their migration policy”.

Officials involved in setting up the migration centres expect legal challenges, but say the scheme is grounded in human rights law.

Rights organisations say the scheme is bound to fail. “Member states may call them ‘return hubs’ or even ‘welcome centres’, but no euphemism can hide the violence of uprooting people from the life they’ve been building in Europe, and sending them to a country they don’t even know, without family, networks or meaningful prospects,” said Chiara Catelli of Picum, a pan-European organisation that campaigns for the rights of undocumented migrants.

#5. European Commission accused of sugarcoating figures to release funds to Egypt

Leaked documents raise questions about claims Egypt met welfare targets to qualify for €2.5bn in loans

The European Commission has been accused of sugarcoating figures to enable it to release funds to Egypt under a landmark pact with the authoritarian government of Abdel Fatah al-Sisi.

The charge relates to a €7.4bn deal promised by Ursula von der Leyen, the European Commission president, to al-Sisi on a high-profile visit to Cairo in 2024. The three-year “strategic and comprehensive partnership” was intended to bolster Egypt’s shaky economy, deter migrants from coming to Europe and shore up a region reeling from the 7 October attacks and Israel’s war on Gaza.

The disclosure raises broader questions about the EU-Egypt relationship, as Egyptian authorities face scrutiny over shrinking press freedom following the jailing and alleged torture of six journalists, the entire newsroom of Matsadaash, a factchecking and investigative platform, and the closure last month of one of the country’s last independent news outlets and conviction of its high-profile editor, Lina Attalah.

Attalah is appealing against that conviction and will be in court this week as she seeks to overturn other charges including “publishing false news”, for which she was sentenced to a year in prison in absentia without her knowledge.

The spotlight is on €5bn in EU “soft” loans to Egypt – lending with easy repayment terms, known as macro-financial assistance, that is meant to be available only to countries that respect human rights and democracy. While the EU conditions on democracy and rights are vague, the loans impose much more specific requirements on Egypt to carry out economic reforms.

Separate data sources – from leaked EU documents and deleted from an Egyptian government website – raise questions about the European Commission’s claims that Egypt met targets linked to social welfare programmes, which enabled the EU to release €2.5bn in assistance.

In confidential documents, the commission told EU lawmakers that Egypt had “broadly fulfilled” and then “fulfilled” two separate targets to increase the numbers of poor people on social welfare programmes between 2024 and 2026. But data points to a net reduction of 85,734 households as the Egyptian government removed people from its welfare programmes rather than expanded coverage.

If Egypt had expanded the programmes in line with the EU’s apparent conditions – and not removed any households – about 245,000 households, equivalent to roughly 909,000 people, would be getting these benefits. (The Guardian used World Bank data to make this rough calculation).

According to the leaked documents, the commission wanted Egypt to “further expand cash transfer programmes”, but despite a net reduction in beneficiaries it gave the green light anyway to two separate “soft” loans totalling €2.5bn.

In response to the Guardian’s findings, one of the European parliament’s leading spokespeople on EU-Egypt relations, the Dutch MEP Tineke Strik, said she was introducing a question to the European Commission demanding clarification.

“The finding by this investigation that the commission is seemingly sugarcoating Egypt’s performance is worrying,” Strik said. “From the beginning, the commission has used macro-financial assistance primarily as a tool to lure Egypt into migration cooperation, while considering formal conditions and its reporting obligations towards parliament as an inconvenient formality.”

Under the plan, Egypt has so far received €3.5bn in soft loans in three instalments, with a final payment of €1.5bn expected before the end of 2027.

The charge of sugarcoating figures relates to two social welfare programmes: the Takaful and Karama cash transfer programmes for poor and vulnerable households.

The Takaful (Solidarity) programme gives poor families cash payments as long as recipients have regular health visits and good attendance at school. The Karama (Dignity) programme gives unconditional cash payments to people living with a disability, older people, orphans and widows.

One of the EU conditions for Egypt to gain the first €1bn loan was to increase the number of Takaful and Karama beneficiary households by 100,000 to 4.7 million.

In a leaked report to lawmakers in the EU council of ministers and the European parliament, the commission described that requirement as “broadly fulfilled”.

The data in that report, however, reveals a net fall of 62,281 households during this period, owing to what the EU officials describe as “a multi-annual recertification process”. Some beneficiaries were added to the list but a larger number were removed because they were deemed ineligible.

In order to achieve a later instalment of €1.5bn, Egypt was required to “further expand” the programmes, adding 60,000 households to the Takaful and Karama beneficiaries list. A second leaked report to EU lawmakers shows that the commission concluded the target was “fulfilled” because 442,118 families were added to the list.

On this occasion, the commission did not report on numbers removed. Figures from Egypt’s ministry of social solidarity, however, show a net reduction of 23,453 beneficiaries to the programmes in the 11 months until June 2026, the period leading up to the release of that payment. The EU announced the release of the €1.5bn payment in July 2026.

#2. Fears grow that US will put Nobel-winning judge under sanctions after Myanmar genocide ruling

A ruling against Myanmar could set precedent for court’s judgment on Israel’s actions in Gaza

Fears are growing that the US could impose sanctions on judges at the international court of justice, including this year’s Nobel peace prize winner, Navi Pillay, if the court rules on Thursday that the Myanmar government committed genocide against the Rohingya.

The ICJ has never found a state responsible for committing genocide, and a significant factor in previous cases has been how to prove “intent” in a crime defined as acts committed with the “intent to destroy, in whole or in part” a national, ethnical, racial or religious group.

Lawyers representing Rohingya survivors say patterns of behaviour by Myanmar’s military, including killing people as they fled, show clear attempts to destroy the group during assaults on Rakhine state in 2016 and 2017.

It is the role of independent judges to interpret the genocide convention. However, judges at the court have taken advice on how to protect their assets from potential US sanctions, as a genocide ruling could be interpreted by Washington as lowering the “legal threshold” for the crime.

The Foundation for Defense of Democracies, an influential thinktank close to the White House, has predicted a genocide ruling will lead to the US imposing sanctions on the ICJ.

The US and Israel fear the final judgment may clarify how to establish a government’s intent to commit a genocide, and so set a precedent that makes it more likely the court will later rule that Israel committed genocide in Gaza.

Myanmar, which has denied carrying out a genocide, claims its actions targeted a military uprising by insurgents.

The ICJ, sometimes described as the UN’s top court, is responsible for settling interstate disputes, unlike the international criminal court (ICC), which prosecutes individuals accused of war crimes and other international crimes.

The US has already imposed crippling sanctions on judges at the ICC after the court issued arrest warrants against the Israeli prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu. The US has also announced sanctions against the entire ICC as an institution, which it described as a “rogue court”. Washington fears the ICC could go after US troops.

#1. One in four women globally unable to refuse sex within marriage, says UN

Almost half of women in west and central Africa cannot withhold consent, with girls worst affected, UNFPA finds

One in four women around the world have no power of consent if their husband or partner demands sex, according to a UN report.

In west and central Africa, almost half of women (45%) are not able to say no in their own marriage, while a third of women (33%) in east and southern Africa and in Arab states face the same predicament. In Latin America and the Caribbean, one in 10 women are not able to refuse sex, said the report by the UN Population Fund (UNFPA) published on Thursday.

Younger women and girls aged 15 to 19 had even less power to say no, compared with older women aged 45 to 49, according to analysis in the report of eight countries with comparable data (Angola, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kazakhstan, Malawi, Mali, Nigeria, Tajikistan and Zambia). The findings align with research showing adolescent girls endure very high rates of intimate partner violence.

“These are really sad figures,” said Jean-Paul Murunga, a lawyer with the global human rights organisation Equality Now. “Especially given that the continent of Africa as a whole has adopted the Maputo protocol, which sets [binding legal standards for] equality between women and men. We still have a lot of work to do to ensure women and girls benefit from these commitments.”

The Maputo protocol, a landmark treaty adopted by the African Union in 2003, guarantees women’s rights and obliges states to protect women from violence. It affirms that marriage must be based on full consent and guarantees women’s right to health.

“The underlying principle is unambiguous: sexual intercourse without free and voluntary consent constitutes sexual violence, including within marriage,” said Murunga. “Marriage does not create perpetual or implied consent. A woman retains the right to decide whether, when and how she engages in sexual activity”.

He said that as a first step rape laws across the continent needed to change. According to Murunga’s 2024 report, rape within marriage is not criminalised in some African countries. Seven countries expressly exempt husbands from prosecution for marital rape, including Côte d’Ivoire, Ethiopia and South Sudan. Some countries, such as Tanzania and Eritrea, only criminalise marital rape when the couple do not live together.

In countries where child marriage is permitted and marital rape is not criminalised, children are left unprotected. A legal loophole is created for what, outside marriage, would be classed as rape, and young wives have few options when it comes to protecting themselves from sexual violence and exploitation. In Gabon, for example, when a man has married a child he abducted, he can only be prosecuted after the marriage is annulled.

Underpinning these laws is the mistaken belief that marital rape does not exist as a concept because, by marrying, wives are assumed to have permanently consented to sex with their husbands. In many areas, religion plays a key role in affirming these beliefs.

Alimatu Dimonekene, founder of A Girl At A Time, an organisation supporting adolescent girls in Sierra Leone, said a conversation around consent in marriage had yet to begin in Africa. “We’ve not even started the conversation [around marital rape],” she said. “Women will never call it rape; they make excuses for it or even blame themselves.”

She added: “In west Africa, sex is seen as an obligation in marriage. You must do whatever your husband demands.

“Once he has paid your bride price [a traditional marriage custom in which the bride’s family are paid money, livestock or goods by the groom], he owns you and you cannot reject his demands. Whatever he wants, he gets, including in terms of intimacy, finances and raising the children.”
