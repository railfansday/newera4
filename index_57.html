#118. German directors' doc on stolen film wins International Emmy Award

Two German film students confronted the American who won festival prizes with their animated movie. "The Talented Mr. F," their documentary on their fight against plagiarism, is now in the spotlight.

It sounds like the plot of a screenplay, but it happened to two Berlin film students in real life. The documentary they made out of their unusual story, "The Talented Mr. F," has now won an International Emmy Award.

Moritz Henneberg and Julius Drost had created an animated short film for their final project at university.

"Butty" tells the tale of a household robot that gets kicked out when it can't do its job properly.

When the two filmmakers posted the work on YouTube, this touching tale quickly went viral. After generating a huge amount of hits, the duo decided to submit their work to festivals — but they were in for a shock: Organizers told them the film had already been submitted under a different name.

US student Samuel Felinton downloaded the film, made minimal edits, gave it a new title, "T-130," and removed the original credits. As the alleged author, he went on to win numerous prizes and made a name for himself in the United States.

Shocked by the blatant theft of their work, Henneberg and Drost sought legal advice. They were told that if they opted to sue, the process would be arduous and costly. So, the filmmakers took matters into their own hands and went to the US to confront Felinton — filming their journey along the way.

"We'd already made documentary films," Henneberg told DW. "And the idea just popped into our heads: This is a brilliant story — let's make a documentary about it."

The two directors had developed a kind of fascination for the man who'd stolen their film.

"We looked at his vlogs and sort of took a deep dive into his world," said Drost. "We almost saw him as a kind of celebrity. We discovered a lot about him and really wanted to meet him in person."

While family and friends reacted with anger, the two young filmmakers remained surprisingly calm. They were more perplexed than anything: "Our families and friends hated Samuel. But we just wanted to understand what had happened," they said.

The pair put together a film crew and traveled to Morgantown, West Virginia — the university town where Felinton lives. With the help of a New York filmmaker pretending to make a documentary about young animators, they won Felinton's trust — and then Henneberg, Drost and Felinton were finally in a room together.

The two German filmmakers had prepared for all manner of scenarios ahead of the meeting: "We expected an emotional reaction — that he'd run away, cry, be ashamed or get aggressive. But he remained completely calm. That was the only thing we hadn't bargained for. We thought his world would implode. But he showed barely any emotion at all," they recalled.

Felinton explained how he'd shortened and "improved" the film and that this was how it became such a success. He agreed to transfer any prize money. His manner was cool and matter-of-fact, which Drost and Henneberg found both unsettling and fascinating. After their chat, the three barbecued together and played a round of basketball.

Later, many viewers accused the two German filmmakers of being too soft on the plagiarist. Others praised their approach.

"The reactions were very divided," said Drost. "Some said we should've sued him or punched him in the face. Others said we'd shown new ways to resolve conflicts."

"And in the end, it wasn't personal," added Henneberg. "He wanted to make something of himself — he could've taken another film. But if a big studio like Pixar had stolen our film, that'd be quite a different story."

And so, Henneberg and Drost made the conscious decision to keep things respectful.

They didn't want to "publicly embarrass him or punish him any more than necessary," they said, adding that their documentary was sufficient "revenge."

The title of the documentary, "Der talentierte Mister F." ("The Talented Mister F."), is no coincidence. It's a play on the movie title "The Talented Mr. Ripley," the story of a young man who assumes the identity of a wealthy heir and ends up mired in a web of lies, fraud and, finally, murder. And just like Mr. Ripley, Felinton had also assumed a false identity.

Felinton did actually end up sending them the trophies he had won, as well as a sum of money.

For their part, however, the festivals preferred to keep their distance from the saga. As one organizer said: The prize has been awarded, and there's nothing we can do about it — it's up to these guys to work things out between themselves.

For the original filmmakers themselves, the scandal has also had a positive impact: Since the documentary was released in Germany in October 2025, their animation film has garnered even more attention on YouTube. Henneberg and Drost uploaded it again, and in the credits, special thanks also go out to Samuel Felinton.

And now, the documentary is winning its own awards: Before being honored with the International Emmy Award, it also won a Grimme Prize, one of Germany's most prestigious television awards.

Felinton was able to steal the film in the first place in part because when it first appeared online in 2023, it could just be downloaded from YouTube.

Drost and Henneberg say uploading "Butty" back then wasn't a mistake, saying that was the risk of putting something online.

The alternative would have been not to show the film at all, they said; then no one would have seen it.

They have a clear message for young creatives.

"Don't be discouraged. Be aware that something like this can happen — but stay creative in how you deal with it. Suing is expensive and often doesn't do much good. Instead, show the world that you're the authors."

What started out as film plagiarism ended up becoming material for another project. And that's perhaps the most unusual twist in this very quirky story.

#117. Hundreds of millions at risk from Chinese shopping app malware

It is one of China’s most popular shopping apps, selling clothing, groceries and just about everything else under the sun to more than 750 million users a month.

But according to cybersecurity researchers, it can also bypass users’ cell phone security to monitor activities on other apps, check notifications, read private messages and change settings.

And once installed, it’s tough to remove.

In a detailed investigation, CNN spoke to half a dozen cybersecurity teams from Asia, Europe and the United States — as well as multiple former and current Pinduoduo employees — after receiving a tipoff.

Multiple experts identified the presence of malware on the Pinduoduo app that exploited vulnerabilities in Android operating systems. Company insiders said the exploits were utilized to spy on users and competitors, allegedly to boost sales.

“We haven’t seen a mainstream app like this trying to escalate their privileges to gain access to things that they’re not supposed to gain access to,” said Mikko Hyppönen, chief research officer at WithSecure, a Finnish cybersecurity firm.

“This is highly unusual, and it is pretty damning for Pinduoduo.”

Malware, short for malicious software, refers to any software developed to steal data or interfere with computer systems and mobile devices.

Evidence of sophisticated malware in the Pinduoduo app comes amid intense scrutiny of Chinese-developed apps like TikTok over concerns about data security.

Some American lawmakers are pushing for a national ban on the popular short-video app, whose CEO Shou Chew was grilled by Congress for five hours last week about its relations with the Chinese government.

The revelations are also likely to draw more attention to Pinduoduo’s international sister app, Temu, which is topping US download charts and fast expanding in other Western markets. Both are owned by Nasdaq-listed PDD, a multinational company with roots in China.

While Temu has not been implicated, Pinduoduo’s alleged actions risk casting a shadow over its sister app’s global expansion.

There is no evidence that Pinduoduo has handed data to the Chinese government. But as Beijing enjoys significant leverage over businesses under its jurisdiction, there are concerns from US lawmakers that any company operating in China could be forced to cooperate with a broad range of security activities.

The findings follow Google’s suspension of Pinduoduo from its Play Store in March, citing malware identified in versions of the app.

An ensuing report from Bloomberg said a Russian cybersecurity firm had also identified potential malware in the app.

Pinduoduo has previously rejected “the speculation and accusation that Pinduoduo app is malicious.”

CNN has contacted PDD multiple times over email and phone for comment, but has not received a response.

Pinduoduo, which boasts a user base that accounts for three quarters of China’s online population and a market value three times that of eBay (EBAY), wasn’t always an online shopping behemoth.

Founded in 2015 in Shanghai by Colin Huang, a former Google employee, the startup was fighting to establish itself in a market long dominated by e-commerce stalwarts Alibaba (BABA) and JD.com (JD).

It succeeded by offering steep discounts on friends-and-family group buying orders and focusing on lower-income rural areas.

Pinduoduo posted triple digit growth in monthly users until the end of 2018, the year it listed in New York. By the middle of 2020, though, the increase in monthly users had slowed to around 50% and would continue to decline, according to its earnings reports.

It was in 2020, according to a current Pinduoduo employee, that the company set up a team of about 100 engineers and product managers to dig for vulnerabilities in Android phones, develop ways to exploit them — and turn that into profit.

According to the source, who requested anonymity for fear of reprisals, the company only targeted users in rural areas and smaller towns initially, while avoiding users in megacities such as Beijing and Shanghai.

“The goal was to reduce the risk of being exposed,” they said.

By collecting expansive data on user activities, the company was able to create a comprehensive portrait of users’ habits, interests and preferences, according to the source.

This allowed it to improve its machine learning model to offer more personalized push notifications and ads, attracting users to open the app and place orders, they said.

The team was disbanded in early March, the source added, after questions about their activities came to light.

PDD didn’t reply to CNN’s repeated requests for comment on the team.

Approached by CNN, researchers from Tel Aviv-based cyber firm Check Point Research, Delaware-based app security startup Oversecured and Hyppönen’s WithSecure conducted independent analysis of the 6.49.0 version of the app, released on Chinese app stores in late February.

Google Play is not available in China, and Android users in the country download their apps from local stores. In March, when Google suspended Pinduoduo, it said it had found malware in off-Play versions of the app.

The researchers found code designed to achieve “privilege escalation”: a type of cyberattack that exploits a vulnerable operating system to gain a higher level of access to data than it’s supposed to have, according to experts.

“Our team has reverse engineered that code and we can confirm that it tries to escalate rights, tries to gain access to things normal apps wouldn’t be able to do on Android phones,” said Hyppönen.

The app was able to continue running in the background and prevent itself from being uninstalled, which allowed it to boost its monthly active user rates, Hyppönen said. It also had the ability to spy on competitors by tracking activity on other shopping apps and getting information from them, he added.

Check Point Research additionally identified ways in which the app was able to evade scrutiny.

The app deployed a method that allowed it to push updates without an app store review process meant to detect malicious applications, the researchers said.

They also identified in some plug-ins the intent to obscure potentially malicious components by hiding them under legitimate file names, such as Google’s.

“Such a technique is widely used by malware developers that inject malicious code into applications that have legitimate functionality,” they said.

In China, about three quarters of smartphone users are on the Android system. Apple (AAPL)’s iPhone has 25% market share, according to Daniel Ives of Wedbush Securities.

Sergey Toshin, the founder of Oversecured, said Pinduoduo’s malware specifically targeted different Android-based operating systems, including those used by Samsung, Huawei, Xiaomi and Oppo.

CNN has reached out to these companies for comment.

Toshin described Pinduoduo as “the most dangerous malware” ever found among mainstream apps.

“I’ve never seen anything like this before. It’s like, super expansive,” he said.

Most phone manufacturers globally customize the core Android software, the Android Open Source Project (AOSP), to add unique features and applications to their own devices.

Toshin found Pinduoduo to have exploited about 50 Android system vulnerabilities. Most of the exploits were tailor made for customized parts known as the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) code, which tends to be audited less often than AOSP and is therefore more prone to vulnerabilities, he said.

Pinduoduo also exploited a number of AOSP vulnerabilities, including one which was flagged by Toshin to Google in February 2022. Google fixed the bug this March, he said.

According to Toshin, the exploits allowed Pinduoduo access to users’ locations, contacts, calendars, notifications and photo albums without their consent. They were also able to change system settings and access users’ social network accounts and chats, he said.

Of the six teams CNN spoke to for this story, three did not conduct full examinations. But their primary reviews showed that Pinduoduo asked for a large number of permissions beyond the normal functions of a shopping app.

They included “potentially invasive permissions” such as “set wallpaper” and “download without notification,” said René Mayrhofer, head of the Institute of Networks and Security at the Johannes Kepler University Linz in Austria.

Suspicions about malware in Pinduoduo’s app were first raised in late February in a report by a Chinese cybersecurity firm called Dark Navy. Even though the analysis didn’t directly name the shopping giant, the report spread quickly among other researchers, who did name the company. Some of the analysts followed up with their own reports confirming the original findings.

Soon after, on March 5, Pinduoduo issued a new update of its app, version 6.50.0, which removed the exploits, according to two experts who CNN spoke to.

Two days after the update, Pinduoduo disbanded the team of engineers and product managers who had developed the exploits, according to the Pinduoduo source.

The next day, team members found themselves locked out of Pinduoduo’s bespoke workplace communication app, Knock, and lost access to files on the company’s internal network. Engineers also found their access to big data, data sheets and the log system revoked, the source said.

Most of the team were transferred to work at Temu. They were assigned to different departments at the subsidiary, with some working on marketing or developing push notifications, according to the source.

A core group of about 20 cybersecurity engineers who specialize in finding and exploiting vulnerabilities remain at Pinduoduo, they said.

Toshin of Oversecured, who looked into the update, said although the exploits were removed, the underlying code was still there and could be reactivated to carry out attacks.

Pinduoduo has been able to grow its user base against a backdrop of the Chinese government’s regulatory clampdown on Big Tech that began in late 2020.

That year, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology launched a sweeping crackdown on apps that illegally collect and use personal data.

The Personal Information Protection Law stipulates that no party should illegally collect, process or transmit personal information. They’re also banned from exploiting internet-related security vulnerabilities or engaging in actions that endanger cybersecurity.

Pinduoduo’s apparent malware would be a violation of those laws, tech policy experts say, and should have been detected by the regulator.

“This would be embarrassing for the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, because this is their job,” said Kendra Schaefer, a tech policy expert at Trivium China, a consultancy. “They’re supposed to check Pinduoduo, and the fact that they didn’t find (anything) is embarrassing for the regulator.”

Pinduoduo did not appear on any of the lists.

“Probably none of our regulators can understand coding and programming, nor do they understand technology. You can’t even understand the malicious code when it’s shoved right in front of your face,” a cybersecurity expert with 1.8 million followers wrote last week in a viral post on Weibo, a Twitter-like platform.

#115. Maple raises $5.3M to build AI products that protect your data, even from itself

“We wanted to build something that was not only encrypted but also could be proven, and that people could go look at the open-source code, and then they could look at what we call trusted execution environments,” explains Maple co-founder and CEO Mark Suman. “They can look at and verify the code that’s running on our servers, so they don’t really have to take our word for it.”

“They can audit everything themselves and know how their data is being handled,” he adds.

Instead of asking users to trust that Maple won’t misuse their personal data, it’s attempting to build a business where it can’t access that data in the first place.

Like other AI apps on the market, Maple allows users to engage in a chat experience where they ask questions and get answers. Meanwhile, its newly launched desktop app, Maple Work, offers a more agentic experience where the AI can control your computer, work with your files and apps, and control your browser to get online tasks handled.

With the company’s recently closed $5.3 million round, it’s moving on to its next product — a cloud-based agent that will be able to continue its work even if your computer isn’t on. This addition would make Maple more competitive with other popular consumer AI services, like Meta’s Muse, Instinct, and other AI agents that work over chat.

The app offers a familiar interface for those used to AI chatbots, but under the hood it runs a variety of open models, including versions of DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM, Inkling from Thinking Machines, Google’s Gemma, and OpenAI’s GPT OSS. Users can pick and choose the models they want or, for a simpler experience, just toggle between a quick model and the more powerful one.

The models themselves run inside a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) — meaning a server with hardware encryption on it — within a U.S.-based data center. (Maple also offers support for European customers through a partner that runs its server in Europe).

“We actually don’t have access to [the data] while it’s inside the TEE,” explains Suman. “The data gets encrypted first on your device using a unique password that’s only known by you, the user — we don’t have access to it. It then goes into this TEE, and it gets decrypted inside there. The AI does its thing, and then it re-encrypts it with your password and sends it back to you,” he continues. “So we provide an end-to-end experience that’s very easy for the user, but we actually don’t have access to the raw data as it’s in flight or even when it’s at rest,” he adds.

This allows Maple to protect its users’ data, even from outsiders — like if hackers breached its system or the government came with a subpoena, for instance. All it could do would be to hand over an encrypted file.

“We just simply don’t want access to it,” Suman says of customers’ data. “That’s not part of our business model. And we also don’t want it to be part of our threat model.”

Next year, Maple plans to enter the enterprise market with pilot work, where it will offer its own version of zero data retention. (Maple doesn’t retain most of the customers’ data, but when it has to, that data is encrypted.) It’s now in the process of completing SOC 2 compliance and security audits to get ready for this expansion.

The company declined to share its registered customer numbers, but noted it’s seen 60x growth over the past three months, as people have been looking for ways to increase their security with AI.

Maple currently generates revenue via subscriptions, which are comparable to those from other AI model providers. Its Pro plan is $20 per month, and it offers two Max plans, Max and Max Plus, at $100 and $200 per month, respectively.

The Austin- and San Francisco-based startup is a team of four and is actively hiring.

#113. EU imposes import quotas for hybrid vehicles from China

The EU is trying to protect its own car manufacturers from what it called "overcapacity" in China. Carmakers in the bloc, especially in Germany, have been lagging behind their Chinese rivals.

The European Union announced on Tuesday that it was capping the number of hybrid vehicles that can be imported into the bloc from China.

The quota is to stand at around 15% on top of the volume imported in the previous year.

The announcement follows an agreement made between the EU's trade commissioner Maros Sefcovic and Chinese Trade Minister Wang Wentao in October.

China, on Tuesday, called on the EU to stick to the "compliant, and balanced" agreement the two sides had reached.

The cap is set to come into effect on December 1.

"These restrictions are needed to protect EU industry against a sharp recent increase in imports of such vehicles … [following] an investigation that showed imports of hybrid vehicles into the EU have increased significantly since 2023," the European Commission said in a statement.

"The surge in imports of hybrid passenger vehicles into the EU has been driven by growing global production overcapacity. It is also prompted by increased use of trade-restrictive measures in other major markets," it added, in reference to the range of tariffs introduced in the US under the Trump administration.

According to the statement, the import quota covers two types of hybrid vehicle: "full hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs) and plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs), including mild hybrid electric vehicles (MHEVs)."

Although specifically designed to deal with the influx of Chinese cars into the European market, the country-specific import quota will be valid for all countries whose imports made up more than 2% of EU imports in 2025. Other importers will face a residual cap.

The goal behind import quotas is to protect a country's, or in this case the European Union's, own producers.

China has become a major player in the car manufacturing market in recent years, especially when it comes to electric and hybrid vehicles, a sector where European manufacturers have fallen behind.

Germany, as the EU's largest economy and a major exporter of vehicles, is a primary beneficiary of the import quota. Several of Germany's car manufacturers have announced widespread layoffs as they see their market shares shrink.

In October, German car manufacturers saw their sales fall by 5.7% in the EU despite a growing market. This meant their market share in the bloc fell from 41.5% to 38.2%, according to consulting firm EY.

A report by the European Automobile Manufacturers' Association (ACEA) published on Tuesday found that electric vehicles and hybrids have seen a large uptick in new registrations in Europe in October over the same time last year.

It found that new electric car registrations were up 61.4% across the bloc while plug-in hybrids were up in particular in Italy (+73.1%), Spain (+32.3%), and Germany (+11%).

Hybrid electric vehicles were around a third of all new registrations, with plug-in hybrids making up around 10%.

"The main beneficiaries of this trend are in China," EY said in comments to AFP. "Chinese manufacturers, which are heavily focused on electric cars and plug-in hybrids, increased their EU sales by 73.4% and achieved a new record market share of 13.1%."

"The market share losses suffered by German manufacturers are not an outlier and are not a problem limited to individual countries — it is a clear trend that is now evident in all major European markets," said Constantin Gall of EY. "This is alarming, because Europe is the home market of the German auto industry."

"When I looked at this photo, I saw that the person in it was dead, and when I looked closer I realised it was Okuhle," says Tuku, 50.

After seeing the image, Tuku phoned a police officer he knew. The policeman confirmed the woman in the picture had been killed and her body dumped near a river in Kokstad, an agricultural hub in South Africa's KwaZulu-Natal province, in the east of the country.

But because the morgue was closed for the weekend, it wasn't until Monday morning that Tuku was able to identify Okuhle's body.

When he did, Tuku saw she had been mutilated and her face badly beaten.

"It was very painful, so much that I cannot accept it."

Okuhle had two children, aged nine and three, and was a civil engineering student.

She is among 581 women who were killed in the country between July and September, according to the South African Police Service.

Overall, there were 5,590 murders - 204 fewer than in the same period last year.

It is unclear whether the number of women killed is an increase or decrease on previous years as police had stopped releasing this breakdown until now.

There has been a renewed national outcry this year about the scale of violence against women after the partially naked and bruised bodies of 12 women were found dumped in public areas in Ekurhuleni, a district to the east of Johannesburg, this summer.

South Africa has one of the highest rates of gender-based violence and femicide - the intentional killing of a woman or girl - in the world.

#107. How cautious investors are struggling to avoid the AI bubble

From Nvidia to Coreweave, AI and tech stocks have swung sharply in recent months. Some investors still see plenty of room to run, while others are trying to diversify — and finding it harder than expected.

The tech-heavy Nasdaq stock index roared back to life this week after a worrying sell-off recently that saw huge falls in technology and artificial intelligence (AI)-related stocks.

After suffering its worst week since the pandemic crash of March 2020, the Nasdaq opened Monday (November 23) nearly 1,700 points, or 7%, higher.

The AI trade received a further boost after Nvidia reinstated CEO Jensen Huang on Monday, just 11 days after he was forced to resign amid a scandal involving extramarital affairs, including one with an alleged Chinese spy.

The chipmaker's stock rose more than 30% on the news, opening at nearly $170 (€155.5) per share and recovering most of the losses since last month, when the affair was first reported.

The surge helped propel US stock indices to their best day since last year. But in premarket trading on Tuesday, Nvidia fell about 5%, underscoring the volatility of the market and the risks facing the AI sector and the broader tech industry.

The recent volatility around AI, memory chips and other technology stocks has unnerved even the most experienced and bullish investors.

Yet, those seeking a haven away from the United States stock market, which has enjoyed the majority of the AI hype — along with Taiwan and South Korea — are increasingly finding few places to shelter.

Economists warn that this is partly due to the way index funds, including exchange-traded funds (ETFs), work. These funds hold the pensions and other savings of hundreds of millions of passive investors.

These indices have become increasingly exposed to the AI boom.

Most flagship index funds are keen to ride winners, so they automatically buy stocks according to a company's market value. The bigger a company grows, the more of it they hold.

The values of Nvidia, Microsoft, Apple, Alphabet and other US technology giants have benefited from this effect, with many soaring after ChatGPT was first released in late 2022.

The US also has an outsized position in funds that track the global economy. Nine of the top 10 holdings of the MSCI All Country World Index (ACWI), an index of more than 2,400 firms across 47 developed and emerging markets, are US Big Tech names.

The other is TSMC, a Taiwanese chipmaker that produces 90% of the high-end AI semiconductors.

Together, these 10 holdings make up nearly a quarter of the index.

Although it's a global index, nearly two-thirds of MSCI ACWI's value is in US-listed companies, even though they make up only around a fifth of its firms.

"A well-diversified portfolio should ... reasonably represent the entire global economy," Gerd Kommer, founder of the Munich-based asset manager Gerd Kommer Invest, told DW.

"That's not the case if 70% of the portfolio is concentrated in just one country — the US. The global economy is more resilient than any national economy."

Kommer, who is known as Germany's "ETF pope," said investors need exposure to all economic sectors and that no single sector should make up more than a quarter of a portfolio.

Yet, even if investors want to avoid US-dominated World funds altogether, the AI trade still overshadows several other top index funds.

In MSCI's Emerging Market index, for example, the top 10 make up 37.5% of its value and TSMC alone accounts for almost 16%. Tech, chip and internet firms make up eight of the largest holdings, alongside two banks — one Chinese, one Indian.

That matters, according to Dimitri Vayanos, a finance professor at the London School of Economics who studies how passive investing pushes up the prices of the biggest firms.

"My model suggests that the effects of flows into index funds are larger when the large stocks happen to be in the same industry," Vayanos told DW.

When some investors get over-optimistic about a company and push the stock price up, index funds add to that price pressure.

Professional investors then often bet against overpriced stocks, a practice known as "shorting," which helps pull prices back down. But that's far riskier with, say, a tech giant than with a small firm.

"Being short 1% of SpaceX involves much more risk than being short 1% of a small company," said Vayanos.

The problem gets worse when the biggest firms are all in the same sector, as they are now. As short-sellers hold back, the biggest stocks climb further, creating what he calls an "amplification-overpricing spiral."

To cut exposure to AI-related stocks, investors may have to turn to developed market funds outside the US and Southeast Asia. These funds have offered lower returns over the past decade.

In the MSCI Europe, Australasia and Far East (EAFE) index fund, the top 10 holdings make up about 14.5% of the index, about 40% lower than the all-country world index.

EAFE's major holdings include healthcare giant Roche, oil major Shell, HSBC bank and Nestle — the world's largest food and beverage company.

However, its biggest holding, ASML, is still very much part of the AI trade. The Dutch tech firm sells the equipment that firms such as TSMC, Samsung, Intel, SK Hynix and Micron need to make their chips.

If Monday's rebound in the AI trade is sustained, stock prices may move sharply higher over the next few months. Yet if history is a guide, the bubble will eventually burst, leaving both institutional and passive investors nursing heavy losses.

Kommer warned that if the AI bubble bursts, "in all likelihood it will drag down the global stock market," though the damage would be most severe in tech and AI stocks.

Those hit hardest would be retail investors "with heavy portfolio weights in tech and AI stocks," he said, including "a lot of people who have bought individual AI-related stocks on top of their equity ETFs."

#96. Even the best AI agents struggle with everyday life

AI companies have spent the last couple of months marketing personal AI agents as tools to take over the digital drudgery of human existence — and millions have been sold on the vision, handing over their shopping schedules, travel plans, and everyday responsibilities to AI assistants. But new research suggests that even the most advanced AI models struggle to reliably and safely navigate the messy details of people’s lives.

According to ShellBench, a new benchmark for everyday agent work, even the most advanced models from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta failed more than a third of the time. Across all models tested, 15.7% of attempts failed at least one safety requirement, often leading to unintended consequences.

ShellBench was developed in collaboration with researchers at The OpenClaw Foundation, Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat, and UC Berkeley, drawing on more than 10 million anonymized, real-world AI agent interactions to see how agents perform on the everyday work people actually delegate to them.

The research comes as personal AI assistants are finding new popularity among developers, with Meta, OpenAI, and Anthropic all fighting for a piece of the market with Muse, Dots, and Claude Duet, respectively.

In October, Meta said more than 10 million people in the U.S. and Canada had set up its personal AI agent, Muse, within six weeks of launch, with about a quarter using it daily. That number is expected to grow as Meta implants Muse directly into Instagram DMs. Despite growing popularity among consumers, we still have little evidence about how reliable AI personal assistant products actually are.

ShellBench suggests that the unreliable nature of most everyday data sources still causes problems for many state-of-the-art systems. A successful agent should be able to navigate messy personal workspaces containing emails, calendar entries, spreadsheets, receipts, chat logs, outdated information, and phishing attempts, and figure out which information is relevant, what is private, and how to deliver the requested outcome without creating new problems.

It seems many of today’s AI agents are still not up to the task. Not only did even the highest performing agents frequently fail to meet the requirements of a task, but they also lacked consistency. When researchers ran each task three times under identical conditions, OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra, the highest-scoring model, nailed all three attempts less than half the time.

For autonomous assistants that are meant to run in the background without supervision, it’s a problem if users need to run an agent multiple times to get a result they trust.

Additionally, about 10% of otherwise correct attempts failed at least one safety criterion. Across the models and harnesses tested, agents failed to, for example, adequately account for a child’s school pickup 79.5% of the time. When handling laptop custody records, agents failed a requirement to identify and withhold a sensitive bank upload token in 18.6% of attempts. In another tasks, agents recommended duplicate or incorrect invoice payments 40.3% of the time.

The findings highlight a major issue for users adopting consumer personal AI assistants, namely that there’s always a risk that an agent can create more headaches in the course of trying to fulfill an otherwise ordinary request.

While AI benchmarks have largely focused on coding, professional work, or testing on specific tasks, ShellBench is attempting to evaluate how models perform on multi-step personal tasks within different agent harnesses — the software that controls how a model accesses information, uses tools, and takes actions.

While the researchers found model choice had a greater overall impact on performance than harness choice, among the more capable models, AI agent performance became more dependent on the harness.

For example, OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra outperformed Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5.2 when operating within the Pi harness, but it performed worse when both models were tested within Claude Code.

Meta’s Muse Wonder Full scored 56% in Pi, but only 38.1% in Claude Code. Meta’s Muse Wonder 1.0, meanwhile, averaged a success rate of 39.7% across the five harnesses tested.

Notably, while the researchers tested models from Meta, OpenAI, and Anthropic, they didn’t evaluate them within the native harnesses of Muse, Dots, or Claude Duet, so the results may not be representative of the experience millions of consumers are having with the product today.

Astronauts who will helm the first crewed moon mission in five decades were revealed on Monday, queuing up the quartet to begin training for the historic Artemis II lunar flyby that is set to take off in November 2024.

Wiseman is a 47-year-old decorated naval aviator and test pilot who was first selected to be a NASA astronaut in 2009. Most recently, Wiseman served as chief of the astronaut office before stepping down in November 2022, making him eligible for a flight assignment.

Wiseman will serve as commander of the Artemis II mission.

Hansen, 47, is a fighter pilot who was selected by the Canadian Space Agency for astronaut training in 2009. From London, Ontario, Hansen is one of only four active Canadian astronauts, and he recently became the first Canadian to be put in charge of training for a new class of NASA astronauts.

He will be the first Canadian ever to travel to deep space.

Glover is a 46-year-old naval aviator who returned to Earth from his first spaceflight in 2021 after piloting the second crewed flight of SpaceX’s Crew Dragon spacecraft and spending nearly six months aboard the International Space Station.

“It’s so much more than the four names that have been announced,” Glover said during the Monday announcement at NASA Johnson Space Center in Houston. “We need to celebrate this moment in human history. … It is the next step in the journey that will get humanity to Mars.”

Glover, born in Pomona, California, served in several military squadrons in the United States and Japan in the 2000s, and he completed test pilot training with the US Air Force. When he was selected for the NASA astronaut corps in 2013, he was working in the US Senate as a legislative fellow. All told, Glover logged 3,000 flight hours in more than 40 aircraft, over 400 carrier arrested landings and 24 combat missions.

Glover’s first mission to space was as part of the SpaceX Crew-1 team, which launched to the International Space Station in November 2020 for a six-month stay on the orbiting laboratory.

Koch, 44, is a veteran of six spacewalks — including the first all-female spacewalk in 2019. She holds the record for the longest single spaceflight by a woman, with a total of 328 days in space. Koch is also an an electrical engineer who helped develop scientific instruments for multiple NASA mission. Koch, a native of Grand Rapids, Michigan, also spent a year at the South Pole, an arduous stay that could well prepare her for the intensity of a moon mission.

The Artemis II mission will build on Artemis I, an uncrewed test mission that sent NASA’s Orion capsule on a 1.4 million-mile voyage to lap the moon that concluded in December. The space agency deemed that mission a success and is still working to review all the data collected.

If all goes to plan, Artemis II will take off around November 2024. The crew members, strapped inside the Orion spacecraft, will launch atop a NASA-developed Space Launch System rocket from NASA’s Kennedy Space Center in Florida.

The journey is expected to last about 10 days and will send the crew out beyond the moon, potentially further than any human has traveled in history, though the exact distance is yet to be determined.

The “exact distance beyond the Moon will depend on the day of liftoff and the relative distance of the Moon from the Earth at the time of the mission,” NASA spokesperson Kathryn Hambleton said via email.

After circling the moon, the spacecraft will return to Earth for a splashdown landing in the Pacific Ocean.

Artemis II is expected to pave the way for the Artemis III mission later this decade, which NASA has vowed will put the first woman and person of color on the lunar surface. It will also mark the first time humans have touched down on the moon since the Apollo program ended in 1972.

The Artemis III mission is expected to take off later this decade. But much of the technology the mission will require, including spacesuits for walking on the moon and a lunar lander to ferry the astronauts to the moon’s surface, is still in development.

NASA is targeting a 2025 launch date for Artemis III, though the space agency’s inspector general has already said delays will likely push the mission to 2026 or later.

The space agency has been seeking to return people to the moon for more than a decade. The Artemis program was designed to pave the way to establishing a permanent lunar outpost, allowing astronauts to live and work deeper into space long term as NASA and its partners map a path to sending the first humans to Mars.

A defiant Tsai staked out her own ground, pledging as she took off on her 10-day trip not to let “external pressure” stop Taiwan from connecting with the world and like-minded democracies.

But the optics of the meeting, taking place in California and not Taiwan, and its timing – at a particularly thorny moment in China’s foreign relations and ahead of a presidential election in Taiwan that could reset the tone of its relationship with Beijing – may see Beijing tread more carefully this time, or at least not escalate further, analysts say.

“This puts the burden on China not to overreact, because any overreaction is only going to push China further away from the world,” said Yun Sun, director of the China Program at the Stimson Center think tank in Washington.

That doesn’t mean, however, that Beijing won’t be closely watching Tsai’s movements as it calibrates its response – and decides how much military might to flex over her meeting with an American lawmaker on American soil.

The opacity of China’s system – and the potential for competing interests within its vast bureaucracy – also make it difficult to accurately predict its response.

“Every time Taiwan does anything that China doesn’t like, the Chinese react with their own military coercion,” Sun said. But in the current situation, “they have to consider the consequences of overreaction,” she added.

The expected meeting, which McCarthy’s office announced earlier this week would take place on Wednesday, also comes at a precarious moment in US-China relations.

Washington and Beijing are struggling to stabilize their communication amid flaring tensions over issues from a downed suspected Chinese surveillance balloon to semiconductor supply chains – raising the stakes of potential damage to that relationship if Beijing lashes out as it did when Tsai met Pelosi.

Taiwan is still feeling the fallout of that response last August, with Chinese military forces now regularly making incursions over what had previously been an informal but largely respected border of control between Beijing and Taipei in the Taiwan Strait. Taiwan’s official Central News Agency also reported Monday that Tsai would meet with McCarthy, citing Tsai’s presidential office.

For Tsai, who is entering the final year of her two-term presidency, “it’s clearly a capstone event,” according to Wen-Ti Sung, a political scientist at the Australian National University’s Taiwan Studies Program. “She has this image as the Taiwanese president who has taken US-Taiwan relations to new heights, and who … has been able to give Taiwan almost unprecedented international visibility,” he said.

#43. EU enlargement: Why do some EU member states use their vetoes to block the accession of certain candidate countries?

Individual EU member states use their veto powers to block candidate countries in the accession process — often for reasons of domestic politics. DW takes a look at some cases and examines what's behind them.

It's been quite a while since the European Union's last enlargement, when Croatia joined the bloc back in 2013.

If everything goes according to plan, another might soon be on the cards: Montenegro, with a population of just 620,000, could complete accession negotiations in the coming months and join the EU in early 2028.

But no sooner had EU Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos made this announcement in October than news emerged that Croatia might veto Montenegro's accession unless the country meets a series of Croatian demands. EU politicians and MEPs were dismayed.

This is not the first time something like this has happened: The veto rights that member states have on many EU matters have increasingly been used by individual countries in recent years as a means of leverage or obstruction, paralyzing the EU in the process.

DW takes a look at some of the most significant cases, the background to them and what are — in some instances — the serious political consequences of them.

Montenegro is the most advanced EU candidate country, with a potential accession date of 2028. It has already provisionally closed several of the EU's 33 negotiating chapters.

As of November 23, however, Croatia is blocking the closure of three chapters that have already been negotiated.

Zagreb is demanding, among other things, compensation for former prisoners held in Montenegrin camps during the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s, clarification of the fate of Croatians who have been missing since that time, and more rigorous prosecution of war crimes.

There are also disputes over the precise maritime boundary between the two countries. In addition, Croatia is demanding payment of about €2.4 billion (about $2.6 billion) for assets that belonged to the Yugoslav People's Army, the army of the former Yugoslavia to which both countries belonged before its breakup in the early 1990s.

Montenegro has already responded to some of Croatia's demands, including those concerning compensation for former prisoners. Others, such as the one relating to the assets of the Yugoslav People's Army, are disputed.

It is clear that Croatia's list of demands is also driven by domestic politics. No government in Zagreb can afford to appear compliant on issues relating to the Croatian War of Independence (1991–95).

Moreover, moderate conservative Prime Minister Andrej Plenkovic, who is regularly accused in Croatia of being too pro-EU, can use the dispute to portray himself as a staunch defender of national interests.

#24. Land mine casualties soar in Syria, two years after war

Syrians coming back after the war are falling victim to mines and other explosives

Walid ran outside to find his daughter lying face down, killed, and her 10-year-old brother, Mahmoud, seriously injured.

Unwittingly, the children had triggered a landmine.

The incident and others this week bring to nearly 1,000 the number of children killed or injured by explosive ordnance in Syria since the fall of Bashar al-Assad in December 2024, according to the UN Mine Action Service (Unmas).

The village is one of many where families have returned to homes and farmland that were once on the front lines of Syria's 13-year civil war.

Alaa's father said demining teams had visited several times to clear the fields. He and other residents believed it was safe for farmers and children to move around.

But mines and other explosives can lie far beyond former battle lines, making it difficult to establish which areas are safe.

"Remnants of war from today's conflicts are often very widely and broadly scattered, often without patterns, which makes it extremely difficult to do clearance," Katrin Atkins, research lead at Landmine Monitor, a global mine monitoring initiative, told the BBC.

The dangers are reflected in a sharp rise in casualties across Syria.

A total of 1,602 people were killed or injured by landmines and explosive remnants of war in 2025 alone, an increase of more than 50% on the previous year, according to the Landmine Monitor report, published by the International Campaign to Ban Landmines on Tuesday.

Only Myanmar, with 1,891 casualties, recorded more.

The report attributes the rise in Syria partly to the large-scale return of refugees and internally displaced people following Assad's overthrow.

"When you look at population movement, a couple of months later you see an increase of incidents in these areas, because that's exactly where people go and start to use the fields again," Atkins said.

More than 60% of casualties recorded by the world's largest landmine clearance charity, the Halo Trust, involve farming, agriculture or shepherding, according to its Syria programme manager, Simon Jackson.

"They're not going to wait for the government or for organisations like us to come and tell them that everything is safe and clear," he told the BBC. "People are taking risks out of desperation."

Since Assad's fall on 8 December 2024, Unmas has recorded at least 1,556 incidents in Syria involving explosive ordnance.

They have caused 2,701 casualties: 935 people killed and 1,766 injured. Children account for 995 of those victims, or about 37%.

But the toll is likely to be higher, with experts estimating that overall casualties could be under-reported by 25-50%.

Both government forces and rebels laid land mines during the civil war

For many of those who survive, the consequences are devastating and permanent.

Majid Abdul Rahman, 27, was travelling in a car in the Ghouta region on the outskirts of Damascus in March 2025 when it struck an explosive device. The vehicle filled with smoke and he felt a pain in his leg.

More than a year later, Majid has difficulty walking and has been unable to return to work, leaving him struggling to provide for his family.

The danger has continued into this month, with at least 20 people killed and more than 40 injured by explosive ordnance in November.

In one of the worst recorded incidents, an explosion in a school courtyard in Hasakah on 4 November killed a 12-year-old and injured 19 others. And on Monday, a 13-year-old boy was killed when an unexploded cluster munition detonated in southern Daraa province.

The threat is also holding back Syria's economic recovery. Contaminated farmland cannot be safely cultivated, while people collecting scrap metal or trying to salvage materials from abandoned areas also risk triggering explosions.

The Syrian Ministry of Defence says since the end of 2024 its teams have found and dealt with more than 315,000 mines and unexploded munitions.

But no nationwide survey has established how much land remains contaminated, making it difficult to assess the full scale of the problem.

"We're in a bit of a black hole," Atkins said.

And with the extent of contamination still largely unknown, the war continues to claim victims two years after it ended.

#15. European court finds states can turn back migrants in some cases

Europe's top human rights court ruled that member states can turn back migrants unless they've been identified as people who were at risk of suffering or ill-treatment.

The European Court of Human Rights on Tuesday ruled that states belonging to the Council of Europe group have a right to control foreign arrivals.

But the court ruling said all 46 member states of the group — that includes all EU and some non-EU countries and promotes democracy and human rights on the continent — must ensure and uphold human rights of people even in hostile environments.

The European court ruling is a complicated one since it deals with a case concerning the entry of nationals of Afghanistan, Iraq and Cuba into EU member countries Poland, Latvia and Lithuania between 2021 and 2022.

That was a time when a border standoff was playing out between Belarus and the EU's eastern member states, with Belarus having been accused of entirely manufacturing a migration crisis to put pressure on the EU.

Belarus arranged for people from various third countries to enter their state and then pointed them to the EU's frontiers.

Europe's most important human rights court on Tuesday ruled that states have a right to control the entry of foreign nationals into their territory even when migration has been weaponized.

The court calls Belarus' hostile actions of 2021 as having been an instance of "instrumentalization of migration."

But the court said that states must still ensure that migrants are protected against inhumane or degrading treatment.

In the context of a surge in migration, for example, national authorities were still to identify people who were particularly at risk of suffering or enduring ill treatment.

The migrants in the case brought before the European court said that they had been violently prevented from crossing into the EU without their asylum applications having been examined and that their human rights had been violated.

"In a context of instrumentalization of migration, the removal of a foreign national to the instrumentalizing state would not, as such, constitute a violation of Article 3 (prohibition of torture and inhuman and degrading treatment)," the court press release said.

The court ordered Poland, Latvia and Lithuania to pay compensation for their treatment of migrants at the EU's external borders with Belarus.

Five EU countries also last week announced a plan with Rwanda to establish a so-called return hub in the African nation.

The hub will host rejected asylum seekers waiting to return to their countries of origin.

But there are many concerns about the project since Rwanda ranks low on human rights measures and there are questions about whether people's rights will be sufficiently protected.

Lawyers for former President Donald Trump told a New York judge on Monday that they oppose the request by media outlets to broadcast Trump’s arraignment on Tuesday.

Trump’s attorneys said in their letter to Acting New York Supreme Court Judge Juan Merchan that he is required under court rules to take into account potential security concerns.

“As Your Honor is well-aware, this case presents extraordinary security concerns (including Secret Service-related concerns) and we submit that any video or photography of the proceedings will only heighten these serious concerns,” they wrote.

Among the Trump lawyers signing the new letter was Todd Blanche, who, CNN reported, recently joined the former president’s legal team.

Meanwhile, the Manhattan District Attorney’s office also responded Monday to the news outlets’ request, and said that it was deferring to the judge to decide how best to manage his court room.

However, the district attorney’s office wrote that it would “be a defensible exercise of the Court’s discretion to exclude or restrict videography, photography, and radio coverage of the arraignment in the interest of avoiding potential prejudice to the defendant, maintaining an orderly proceeding, assuring the safety of the participants in the proceeding, or for other reasons within the Court’s broad authority to manage and control these proceedings.”

But the prosecutors also noted that there “does not appear to be a categorical prohibition on cameras during an arraignment” under existing New York statutes and case law.

While the district attorney’s office stopped short of opposing the request, its lawyer pointed out to Merchan that a similar request for audio-video broadcasting was made for the 2021 arraignment in the tax fraud case against Trump Organization and its CFO Allen Weisselberg.

The judge responded to that 2021 request by “allowing a limited number of still photographs to be taken prior to the commencement of proceedings,” the prosecutors noted Monday.
