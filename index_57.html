The Shenzhou-24 spacecraft lifted off on a Long March-2F rocket at 8:18 am local time (0018 GMT) from the Jiuquan launch center in the Gobi Desert.

The China Manned Space Agency reported that the capsule separated from the rocket about 10 minutes later and entered orbit, and called the launch a "complete success".

State broadcaster CCTV showed the three crew members leafing through a manual minutes after liftoff and reported that they were in good condition.

Sheikh Hasina has been living in exile in India since 2024

The announcement comes amid renewed unrest in Bangladesh, where supporters of her Awami League party have staged protests despite a ban on its activities.

"Sheikh Hasina will not be able to return to the country as an ordinary commercial passenger; she will be brought back in handcuffs," he told a press briefing on Tuesday.

Kabir also accused Hasina and Delhi of fomenting recent unrest in Bangladesh, alleging that they were backing "terrorist activities" in the country.

#78. Beijing promised to 'fight back' over Taiwan leader's US visit. But this time it has more to lose

An anticipated meeting between Taiwan’s President Tsai Ing-wen and US House Speaker Kevin McCarthy in California this week has sparked concerns of a repeat of the pressure campaign China launched last year when then-Speaker Nancy Pelosi visited Taipei.

At that time, Beijing encircled the island democracy with unprecedented military drills – firing multiple missiles into its surrounding waters and sending dozens of warplanes speeding across a sensitive median line dividing the Taiwan Strait.

It also cut off contact with the United States over a number of issues from military matters to combating climate change, in retaliation for what it viewed as a violation of its sovereignty.

This time, Beijing has already threatened to “resolutely fight back” if a Tsai-McCarthy meeting goes ahead.

It also slammed Washington for allowing Tsai to stopover in the US while en route to and from official visits in Central America, warning it could lead to “serious” confrontation between the two powers.

A defiant Tsai staked out her own ground, pledging as she took off on her 10-day trip not to let “external pressure” stop Taiwan from connecting with the world and like-minded democracies.

But the optics of the meeting, taking place in California and not Taiwan, and its timing – at a particularly thorny moment in China’s foreign relations and ahead of a presidential election in Taiwan that could reset the tone of its relationship with Beijing – may see Beijing tread more carefully this time, or at least not escalate further, analysts say.

“This puts the burden on China not to overreact, because any overreaction is only going to push China further away from the world,” said Yun Sun, director of the China Program at the Stimson Center think tank in Washington.

That doesn’t mean, however, that Beijing won’t be closely watching Tsai’s movements as it calibrates its response – and decides how much military might to flex over her meeting with an American lawmaker on American soil.

The opacity of China’s system – and the potential for competing interests within its vast bureaucracy – also make it difficult to accurately predict its response.

“Every time Taiwan does anything that China doesn’t like, the Chinese react with their own military coercion,” Sun said. But in the current situation, “they have to consider the consequences of overreaction,” she added.

The expected meeting, which McCarthy’s office announced earlier this week would take place on Wednesday, also comes at a precarious moment in US-China relations.

Washington and Beijing are struggling to stabilize their communication amid flaring tensions over issues from a downed suspected Chinese surveillance balloon to semiconductor supply chains – raising the stakes of potential damage to that relationship if Beijing lashes out as it did when Tsai met Pelosi.

Taiwan is still feeling the fallout of that response last August, with Chinese military forces now regularly making incursions over what had previously been an informal but largely respected border of control between Beijing and Taipei in the Taiwan Strait. Taiwan’s official Central News Agency also reported Monday that Tsai would meet with McCarthy, citing Tsai’s presidential office.

But a meeting between Tsai and the leader of the Republican majority in the US House of Representatives, who ranks second in line to the Presidency, would mark another symbolic moment for Taiwan and the US, which only maintain unofficial ties.

For Tsai, who is entering the final year of her two-term presidency, “it’s clearly a capstone event,” according to Wen-Ti Sung, a political scientist at the Australian National University’s Taiwan Studies Program. “She has this image as the Taiwanese president who has taken US-Taiwan relations to new heights, and who … has been able to give Taiwan almost unprecedented international visibility,” he said.

That increased visibility – and enhanced cooperation with the US – has followed China’s mounting pressure on the island, which sits fewer than 110 miles (177 kilometers) from the mainland coast.

China’s Communist Party claims the self-governing island democracy as its own despite never having controlled it, and has vowed to take the island, by force if necessary.

China thinks it's diplomatically isolating Taiwan. It isn't

The party has undertaken a sweeping expansion of its military capabilities over the past decade under leader Xi Jinping – and ramped up its pervasive economic, diplomatic and military pressure on Taiwan.

That’s driven concerns, among some in Washington, that Beijing is preparing for an invasion, though China’s official language still suggests that scenario is not its preferred option for achieving the claimed goal of “reunification.”

It is those pressures – and how to support Taiwan against unilateral actions by Beijing – that are likely to be on the table when Tsai, McCarthy and a bipartisan group of US lawmakers sit down on Wednesday.

Congress has been a pillar of increasing American support for Taiwan in recent years. Lawmakers regularly visit the island and drive bipartisan legislation enhancing support and cooperation.

While the US switched its diplomatic relations to Beijing decades ago, it maintains unofficial ties with Taiwan and is bound by law to provide the democratic island with the means to defend itself.

Under Washington’s longstanding “One China” policy, the US acknowledges China’s position that Taiwan is part of China, but has never officially recognized Beijing’s claim to the island of 23 million.

Though McCarthy does not have Pelosi’s decades-long record of advocacy regarding China, the California Republican is now a leading voice pushing for closer scrutiny of Beijing, and meeting Tsai could help him to burnish that image.

Last month, McCarthy told reporters that meeting Tsai in the US would not impact whether he travels to Taiwan in the future – something he had earlier said he wanted to do.

A meeting in California, on US soil, is widely seen as less likely to provoke Beijing than a McCarthy visit to Taiwan.

Pelosi’s trip – the first from a lawmaker of that rank to the island in 25 years – generated a fever pitch of nationalist and anti-US rhetoric in mainland China.

This time, so far, domestic conversation in China’s heavily controlled media sphere has been significantly muted.

But the stakes remain high – including for Beijing itself – over how it responds, analysts say.

As Taiwan prepares for a presidential election in January, a fierce response could push voters away from Taiwan’s main opposition party Kuomintang (KMT), widely seen as more friendly toward Beijing.

It could also jar with another high profile trip happening now: a tour of mainland China from former Taiwan president and senior KMT member Ma Ying-jeou, the first visit from a current or former Taiwan leader since the end of the Chinese Civil War in 1949.

Ma’s tour is a “once in a half a century opportunity to send a conciliatory message between the two sides, Beijing shouldn’t want to tank that,” said Sung, the political scientist.

What you need to know about China-Taiwan tensions

China is also acutely aware that its actions toward Taiwan are under a significantly brighter global spotlight following the invasion of Ukraine by Russian President Vladimir Putin, a close diplomatic partner of Xi. Putin’s rhetoric over Ukraine has echoes of how Xi speaks of Taiwan.

Beijing has recently sought to position itself as an agent of peace in that conflict – especially as it aims to repair frayed ties with Europe.

This week, as Tsai is expected to meet with McCarthy, French President Emmanuel Macron and European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen will head to China – an important opportunity that Xi may not want to overshadow with military posturing.

An aggressive response also risks stoking confrontation with the US, not yet six months after Xi and US President Joe Biden called to enhance communication during a face-to-face meeting in Bali.

“(A less overtly aggressive response) would imply that Beijing does not wish to escalate tension with the US to a level that can risk getting out of hand,” said Steve Tsang, director of the SOAS China Institute in London.

“A reset in US-China ties is not on the agenda, but an easing of tension is not beyond the realm of possibility.”

#75. Iranian authorities flog singer who performed without hijab, lawyers say

Parastoo Ahmadi's performance was livestreamed on her YouTube channel in December 2024

A prominent singer who performed without the mandatory hijab and four members of her band and production team have been flogged by Iranian authorities, lawyers say.

Ahmadi, 29, wore a sleeveless dress during a concert that was livestreamed in December 2024. Iran's laws ban public performances by women and require them to wear headscarves and modest clothing.

This June, a criminal court in Qom convicted Ahmadi and eight others of "offending public decency" through the publication of "obscene and indecent material", which they had denied.

In addition to being sentenced to 74 lashes, they were banned from audiovisual activities and from leaving the country for two years.

At the end of September, an appeals court in Qom rejected the defendants' appeal and confirmed the sentence, her lawyers said.

There was widespread condemnation of the sentences from other Iranian artists and human rights activists, who warned that flogging constituted cruel, inhuman and degrading punishment, and violated the international prohibition on torture.

On Monday, one of the defendants' lawyers told the BBC that Ahmadi and four of her male co-defendants had been flogged at the third branch of the Criminal Sentence Enforcement Office in Qom.

"We did everything in our power to have the verdict overturned, or at least to secure a reduction in the sentence or a suspension, but it didn't work out," he said.

"The other lawyers and I even tried to get the enforcement of the sentence postponed, but the enforcement branch insisted on carrying it out immediately."

In January this year, thousands of people were killed and tens of thousands were arrested when security forces crushed another wave of mass anti-establishment protests, this time sparked by the country's economic crisis.

That raises questions about whether there is intelligence the balloon was able to gather that the US still doesn’t know about.

Still, the intelligence community has not been overly concerned about the information the balloon was able to gather, the person said, because it is not much more sophisticated than what Chinese satellites are able to glean as they orbit over similar locations.

A US intelligence official told CNN on Monday that “although analysis of the wreckage of the High Altitude Balloon remains ongoing, thus far, its flight over the United States does not appear to have provided critical new insights to the People’s Republic of China.”

The US also knew what the balloon’s path would be and was able to protect sensitive sites and censor some signals before the balloon was able to pick them up, officials have said.

As CNN has reported, the US intelligence community last year developed a method of tracking what it says is a fleet of these Chinese balloons operating across the globe, controlled by the Chinese military.

The FBI is still examining the balloon, but so far officials have been able to glean additional information about how the device worked, including the algorithms used for the balloon’s software and how it is powered and designed.

CNN has reached out to the National Security Council at the White House and the Pentagon for comment. NBC was first to report on the new intelligence.

The balloon first crossed into US airspace over Alaska in late January before passing through Canada and down into Montana, where it hovered for a few days, leading the US to believe it was trying to surveil sensitive military sites, like Malmstrom Air Force Base in Montana. It was eventually shot down by the US off the East Coast on February 4, and the incident further escalated tensions between Washington and Beijing, including the postponement of a diplomatic visit by US Secretary of State Antony Blinken to China.

A senior State Department official said in February that, as the balloon floated across the US, it “was capable of conducting signals intelligence collection operations.”

Gen. Glen VanHerck, the commander of US Northern Command and NORAD, said at the time that the US “did not assess” that the balloon “presented a significant collection hazard beyond what already exists in actionable technical means from the Chinese.”

The surveillance program, which includes a number of similar balloons, is in part run out of the small Chinese province of Hainan, officials told CNN. The US does not know the precise size of the fleet of Chinese surveillance balloons, but sources tell CNN that the program has conducted at least two dozen missions over at least five continents in recent years.

Roughly half a dozen of those flights have been within US airspace, although not necessarily over US territory, according to one official familiar with the intelligence.

China has maintained that the balloon was actually just a weather balloon thrown off course, and the US has been assessing the possibility that it was not deliberately maneuvered into the continental US by the Chinese government, CNN has reported.

Still, China did maintain some ability to maneuver the balloon, officials believe. And once the balloon was over Montana, China appeared to take advantage of its position to loiter over sensitive sites and try to collect intelligence.

#43. Land mine casualties soar in Syria, two years after war

Syrians coming back after the war are falling victim to mines and other explosives

Eleven-year-old Alaa was tending her family's goats with her siblings in a field behind their home in Umm Khuzaym, north-west Syria, on Sunday when there was a flash and a deafening bang.

"Then we heard people screaming," Alaa's father, Walid Mohammed Al-Ghafar, told the BBC.

Walid ran outside to find his daughter lying face down, killed, and her 10-year-old brother, Mahmoud, seriously injured.

Unwittingly, the children had triggered a landmine.

The incident and others this week bring to nearly 1,000 the number of children killed or injured by explosive ordnance in Syria since the fall of Bashar al-Assad in December 2024, according to the UN Mine Action Service (Unmas).

The village is one of many where families have returned to homes and farmland that were once on the front lines of Syria's 13-year civil war.

Alaa's father said demining teams had visited several times to clear the fields. He and other residents believed it was safe for farmers and children to move around.

But mines and other explosives can lie far beyond former battle lines, making it difficult to establish which areas are safe.

"Remnants of war from today's conflicts are often very widely and broadly scattered, often without patterns, which makes it extremely difficult to do clearance," Katrin Atkins, research lead at Landmine Monitor, a global mine monitoring initiative, told the BBC.

The dangers are reflected in a sharp rise in casualties across Syria.

A total of 1,602 people were killed or injured by landmines and explosive remnants of war in 2025 alone, an increase of more than 50% on the previous year, according to the Landmine Monitor report, published by the International Campaign to Ban Landmines on Tuesday.

Only Myanmar, with 1,891 casualties, recorded more.

The report attributes the rise in Syria partly to the large-scale return of refugees and internally displaced people following Assad's overthrow.

"When you look at population movement, a couple of months later you see an increase of incidents in these areas, because that's exactly where people go and start to use the fields again," Atkins said.

More than 60% of casualties recorded by the world's largest landmine clearance charity, the Halo Trust, involve farming, agriculture or shepherding, according to its Syria programme manager, Simon Jackson.

"They're not going to wait for the government or for organisations like us to come and tell them that everything is safe and clear," he told the BBC. "People are taking risks out of desperation."

Since Assad's fall on 8 December 2024, Unmas has recorded at least 1,556 incidents in Syria involving explosive ordnance.

They have caused 2,701 casualties: 935 people killed and 1,766 injured. Children account for 995 of those victims, or about 37%.

But the toll is likely to be higher, with experts estimating that overall casualties could be under-reported by 25-50%.

Both government forces and rebels laid land mines during the civil war

For many of those who survive, the consequences are devastating and permanent.

Majid Abdul Rahman, 27, was travelling in a car in the Ghouta region on the outskirts of Damascus in March 2025 when it struck an explosive device. The vehicle filled with smoke and he felt a pain in his leg.

More than a year later, Majid has difficulty walking and has been unable to return to work, leaving him struggling to provide for his family.

The danger has continued into this month, with at least 20 people killed and more than 40 injured by explosive ordnance in November.

In one of the worst recorded incidents, an explosion in a school courtyard in Hasakah on 4 November killed a 12-year-old and injured 19 others. And on Monday, a 13-year-old boy was killed when an unexploded cluster munition detonated in southern Daraa province.

The threat is also holding back Syria's economic recovery. Contaminated farmland cannot be safely cultivated, while people collecting scrap metal or trying to salvage materials from abandoned areas also risk triggering explosions.

The Syrian Ministry of Defence says since the end of 2024 its teams have found and dealt with more than 315,000 mines and unexploded munitions.

But no nationwide survey has established how much land remains contaminated, making it difficult to assess the full scale of the problem.

"We're in a bit of a black hole," Atkins said.

And with the extent of contamination still largely unknown, the war continues to claim victims two years after it ended.

#36. China-linked network using AI fakes to smear Taiwan election candidates, BBC Verify finds

Taiwan's ruling party is being repeatedly targeted with AI videos spreading false claims about its leader and other key members ahead of crucial local elections, BBC Verify has found.

A China-linked network has pumped out more than 500 fake videos about the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) in the past year, which experts suggest is part of a long-running campaign to influence the outcome of the elections.

The videos appear to try to smear President Lai Ching-te by claiming he holds a US green card - and would abandon Taiwan if war broke out with China - which Taiwan's presidential office says is completely false. Deepfakes also appear to show Puma Shen, who is running to be the mayor of capital Taipei, using drugs, which he denies. Others seek to undermine or ridicule the politicians by showing them in children's clothing or as popular film characters.

The Taipei mayoralty has traditionally served as a springboard to the presidency. Experts say this has made Shen, who has been openly critical of Beijing, an obvious target for disinformation campaigns. The current mayor, Chiang Wan-an, is part of Taiwan's Kuomintang (KMT) party, which favours closer ties with China.

Taiwan is a self-governing island that China claims as part of its territory. Beijing and Taipei have long observed an uneasy status quo under which Taiwan, backed by the US, neither formally declares independence nor unites with China.

But tensions have risen since the DPP came to power in 2016. Beijing has repeatedly accused the party of pursuing independence while China's leader Xi Jinping, who has said "reunification" with Taiwan is a key goal, has stepped up pressure against the party with increased military activity around the island.

Experts believe the current disinformation activity is part of a broader campaign against Taiwan designed to undermine trust in its democratic process.

The campaign has targeted Taiwan's President Lai (top) and his predecessor Tsai (bottom)

"The account operators are trying to make people distrust the DPP politicians so that they will not believe whatever they say," Austin Horng-En Wang, an expert in cognitive warfare at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, told BBC Verify. "If you don't trust that candidate then you will not listen to what they say," he added.

The videos, many of which were shared from an account called "noxparable" or carried a watermark with the same name, have been viewed tens of millions of times across several social media platforms since May 2025.

More than half of those views were tracked in the past four months as the activity has ramped up ahead of the elections. Many of the accounts involved in spreading disinformation listed their locations on social media as mainland China and Hong Kong, while a number of the clips carried labels showing they were generated with AI. Many people who engaged with the videos were often seen repeating the false claims or attacking DPP figures in the comments, while others appeared to treat the clips as entertainment.

One of the most widely shared videos targeted Shen by superimposing his face onto a man who is seen holding a lighter and exhaling smoke alongside claims he is smoking drugs. The video appears to build on accusations from Taiwan's opposition party that he supports drugs decriminalisation, which Shen denies, and was debunked by a local news network.

Shen has taken legal action against people sharing deepfakes, local media reported, while his campaign office said AI videos showing the candidate appeared to be part of a "classic tactic China uses to smear specific targets". BBC Verify contacted the Chinese embassy in London for comment.

Many of the fake videos ridiculed President Lai and Shen while others portrayed other KMT candidates such as Ko Chih-en (above) in a positive light

Other debunked claims include allegations that Lai's predecessor Tsai Ing-wen had fraudulently obtained her PhD from the London School of Economics. The university pushed back on identical allegations in 2019 and confirmed it had awarded her the doctorate.

A number of the social media accounts BBC Verify identified as sharing the disinformation videos appeared to be linked to Chinese firm Borderless, which produces websites that blend apolitical content with pro-China messaging. The network has also been investigated by journalists at the Taiwanese independent media outlet The Reporter, who shared their findings with BBC Verify. Borderless has previously been accused of carrying out "cognitive warfare" against Taiwan by Taipei's National Security Bureau and the firm has been contacted for comment.

Analysts confirmed to BBC Verify there were also linguistic clues that the accounts, which presented themselves as based in Taiwan, were actually posting from China. The posts occasionally used a simplified Chinese script introduced by the Communist government which was never adopted in Taiwan.

The posts mostly appeared during regular office hours, which Wang suggested showed a "professional" operation, while the accounts originating outside Taiwan showed evidence of election interfering. "When they pretended to be citizens in another country and used these accounts to manipulate the public opinion of the other country during the campaign, it is definitely election interference," Wang added.

Earlier this month, Taiwan's central election commission aired an advert warning voters to "beware of AI-generated deepfake videos" circulating online that could seek to influence the upcoming election. It comes as government officials flagged nearly 2,700 social media accounts to Meta that it claimed were part of a Chinese information warfare campaign, Reuters reported. Meta said it had removed the accounts.

Andrew Yeh of the China Strategic Risk Institute said the ultimate goal is not to favour any party. "China's information operations succeed not just by backing one candidate or opposing another, but by diluting Taiwan's policy debates, driving polarisation and degrading Taiwanese citizens' trust in their democratic processes," he said.

"Regardless of whether the KMT or DPP comes out on top, a more fractured and more divided Taiwan is in Beijing's interests."

#29. These North Koreans say they were spied on, beaten and jailed for being Christian

Kim Jong Un's communist regime is accused of brutal crackdowns on religious freedom in a new report

Living in North Korea, Kim Bo-bin got used to praying with her eyes open.

She found ingenious ways to keep her Christian faith a secret in the Communist dictatorship, which tightly controls how and where its citizens worship. Unauthorised displays of faith could land people in prison, she tells the BBC.

So Kim would silently mouth hymns and clap with just two of her fingers. "In North Korea, if there are three people in a room, you are being spied on. So I would only close my eyes [and pray] when I lay on the floor."

Now 44, Kim lives just outside the South Korean capital Seoul, where she fled 15 years ago.

Her testimony - of how she prayed in secret for years, and was jailed and beaten for her faith - is part of a new report documenting the experiences of 570 North Korean escapees, most of whom say they experienced or witnessed anti-Christian persecution.

Spanning five years and featuring interviews with defectors from across the country, the report by British human rights group Korea Future accuses Kim Jong Un's regime of repeatedly violating religious freedom through a system of surveillance, fear and violence. It suggests little has changed after sustained international pressure, with multiple incidents of suspected Christians being beaten and tortured as recently as last year.

North Korea maintains that it allows religious freedom in spite of years of evidence heard by rights groups and the United Nations. While churches exist in Pyongyang, they are controlled by the state and widely seen as projecting an illusion of religious liberty.

"The North Korean leader's authority is god-like," says Kang Dong-wan, professor at Dong-a University. "So they cannot allow gods whose authority is above him." Pyongyang fears that religious freedom may threaten the regime itself, he adds.

Kim Bo-bin has been living in South Korea for 15 years now

First established in Korea in the late 1700s, Christianity exploded across the north after Protestant missionaries arrived in the 1880s. North Korea provides no figures on its Christian population but the UN has referred to estimates of 200,000 to 400,000 of them practising secretly, some of whom are detained in camps.

Surveillance and control over religion, like many other aspects of North Korean life, intensified under Kim Jong Un, who succeeded his father in 2011.

Three South Korean missionaries operating in the Chinese border region were arrested between 2013-2014 and sentenced to life in prison on charges of espionage and plotting a coup. It's not clear if they are still alive, as Seoul says Pyongyang has refused to share any information about the detainees.

Pyongyang has also been cracking down on smuggled memory sticks containing South Korean-made videos of Christian sermons. In 2020, it passed a law - on Rejecting Reactionary Ideology and Culture - to block so-called "foreign influence". Owning Bibles and hosting services can now result in imprisonment, while distributing religious books or videos is punishable by death.

Lessons against religion begin as early as kindergarten for most North Koreans. Children must attend "self-criticism" sessions at school in which they are warned against "superstitions", widely understood to refer to Christianity and other faiths.

An image from 2010 showing the inside of a state-sanctioned church in Pyongyang

This is what Kim Bo-bin recalls being taught. The faiths Koreans have long practised - Christianity since the 18th Century and shamanism, a millennia-old practice rooted in ancestral spirit worship - were dismissed as superstitions.

Her teachers never mentioned that Kim Il Sung himself was born into a fervently Christian family, or that Pyongyang had once been dubbed the "Jerusalem of the East."

Rather, every child and adult is expected to know North Korea's foundational Ten Principles by heart. They state that citizens must hold the "firm perspective" they know of "nothing greater than the great comrades" Kim Il Sung, the founder of North Korea, his son Kim Jong Il and the ruling party. The "smallest attempt" to dent this authority must not be tolerated, it adds.

The report documents both the indoctrination as well as the constant surveillance, with police and a network of neighbourhood spies monitoring suspicious activities. A former police informant says she was instructed to gather information on neighbours' worshipping, and handed a memo by an officer describing Christianity as an "anti-socialist activity".

North Korean authorities are also reported to hold intergenerational records of families' beliefs condemning Christians' children and grandchildren to a life without higher education or decent career prospects.

All of this illustrates how various North Korean institutions, including schools and the police, control its citizens' beliefs from "cradle to the grave," co-author James Burt says.

And yet many North Koreans turn to religion because their lives are so precarious, he adds. Faith offers them something that the state ideology "with its rigid doctrines and disconnect from their lived realities" cannot.

North Koreans pay tribute to statues of the late Kim Il Sung and Kim Jong Il

Kim Bo-bin grew up in North Hamgyong province, along the border with China. It was a remote, impoverished part of the country where those deemed "untrustworthy" by the state ended up, such as landowners and Christians.

Stuck at the bottom of a state-enforced hierarchy, this "hostile class" had little chance of moving up the ladder. Many of Kim's neighbours were barred from higher education and government positions, or ever living in Pyongyang.

One of them was an exiled "crazy grandma" who used to disappear into a coal shed every morning to chant words 10-year-old Kim didn't recognise. "One day, [the grandma] told me we go from ashes to ashes and dust to dust," she recalls. It was only 12 years later, when she encountered a Bible for the first time in her life that she realised the grandma was a Christian.

When a devastating famine began in the late 1990s, North Hamgyong was one of the first regions to starve because it was cut off from the state's food supply which prioritised Pyongyang. Millions were killed in what the regime described euphemistically as the "Arduous March". It was a time when many North Koreans crossed into China seeking food and work.

Kim was one of them. Aged 22 in 2003, she took what she believed was a job opportunity in China offered by a friend's aunt. But once she crossed the border, she was forced into marriage with a Chinese man and became pregnant.

While picking up a free pram for her newborn daughter at a building adorned with a cross, she encountered a missionary for the first time. Kim would join a growing group of people who discovered Christianity in China - a group that Pyongyang saw as a threat when they were returned to the North, according to Prof Kang.

A view of the city of Chongjin in North Korea's North Hamgyong province

Kim was arrested in China in 2007 and deported. During interrogation, she says North Korean secret police asked her if she'd ever worn a cross on her neck. She pretended not to understand. She told the police she only recognised the symbol from hospital buildings.

Kim Bo-bin says it was God's blessing the interrogator seemed more interested in whether she intended to flee to South Korea, a grave offence. She says she was beaten for 23 days and sentenced to three years of hard labour at a prison. Her story tallies with multiple accounts in Korea Future's report of extreme police violence causing broken bones and severe bleeding.

Lee Young-joo, who Kim Bo-bin met in prison also after being deported from China, tells the BBC she was interrogated about whether she'd met any missionaries, been to a church or seen a Bible. "Of course you must deny all of it. I said no, I haven't seen it," she told them.

In her prison cell, Lee says she'd keep her eyes open, begging God to save her life. Whenever anyone shut their eyes, guards would swear and shout: "Are you praying?"

Lee is glad she wasn't found to be a Christian, because she says she "would have been sent to a political prisoners' camp. I would be tortured and never allowed to leave and be buried there." This is a widely-held fear among defectors, although Korea Future's report did not hear any first-hand testimonies of Christians being held in such camps.

Lee, who previously shared with the BBC the abuse she suffered inside prison, says she was sentenced to three-and-half years. Outside of hard labour, she would spend time praying, sometimes with other Christian inmates. They would hold hands and whisper Bible verses from memory.

Lee Young-joo first met a Christian after she escaped from North Korea into China

She and Kim Bo-bin eventually returned home after serving their sentences, where they continued to practise their religion in secret, they tell the BBC.

Kim says she managed to convert her younger sister and other relatives: "My aunt's walls are thick. The neighbours can't hear us so I quietly taught them hymns and about the all-knowing and all-powerful God."

Lee tells the BBC her mother was horrified when she learned of her Christian faith. Undeterred, she told her that she would escape again to join her daughter, who had been left in China as Lee was arrested and deported. When her mother protested, she responded saying they could all live together later.

Her incredulous mother asked: where? "In heaven," she replied.

Today, Lee attends a small church of North Korean escapees in Seoul. She prays daily, to thank God for the chicken soup and the stroll along the river. She prays for her daughter, who escaped with her in 2010, to find a nice Christian man to marry. Her mother died in North Korea some years after her escape, she was told.

Kim managed to flee to South Korea eventually, in 2012, at the age of 30, along with her daughter. She prays throughout the day - and not silently. At church on Sundays, she is the loudest singer.

#20. The rising cost of putting food on the table in India

As food inflation rises, vegetables are becoming an increasingly expensive part of the household budget

India's inflation is rising again. And the biggest worry is what's happening to food prices.

The latest government data, external shows retail inflation climbed to 6.09% in October, up from 5.54% in September.

But look more closely at food inflation. It has surged to 8.94%.

The squeeze was sharper in rural India, where food inflation was 9.09%, compared with 8.68% in urban areas.

In other words, food prices are rising considerably faster than the overall rate of inflation.

Food inflation this year has been driven by a combination of weather disruptions, supply shortages and rising input costs, say economists.

With nearly 60% of its farmland dependent on rainfall, external, India remains acutely vulnerable to the vagaries of the monsoon. This year, the southwest monsoon was 13% below normal and unevenly distributed, while intensifying El NiÃ±o conditions have heightened risks to farm production.

Onion prices have surged, adding to the pressure on household food budgets

A survey by polling agency LocalCircles found that six in 10 consumers say household essentials cost them over 20% more each month than a year ago, prompting calls for government intervention.

Rising prices are also forcing cutbacks: 74% are curbing discretionary spending on eating out, shopping and entertainment, while half are buying smaller quantities of essentials.

Food accounts for a smaller share of Indian household spending than it once did, but remains the biggest driver of inflation, with significant economic, social and political consequences.

In India's latest consumer price index, food and beverages account for 36.75% of the basket of goods and services used to measure inflation.

That is down sharply from 45.86% under the previous series, reflecting changing consumption patterns as Indians spend more on services, transport, healthcare, education and other non-food items.

Yet a sharp increase in food prices can move the overall inflation rate significantly, while disproportionately affecting people whose incomes leave little room for adjustment.

"Adjustability for a poor country like India is significantly lower," says Rajeswari Sengupta, an associate professor of economics at Mumbai-based Indira Gandhi Institute of Development Research, pointing to the fact that a substantial proportion of household income is still spent on food.

Rising food prices hurt the poor the most

In urban India, the corresponding rates were 6.1% and 5.3%.

The pressure is visible even in the cost of a simple meal.

Crisil's Roti Rice Rate analysis, external found that the cost of a home-cooked vegetarian thali - a meal platter - rose 12% year-on-year in October, driven by higher prices of onions, tomatoes, vegetables, edible oil, rice and cooking gas. A non-vegetarian thali became 9% more expensive.

Rice prices were up about 10% year-on-year, pulses 8%, edible oil 13% and LPG 10%, according to Pushan Sharma, director of intelligence at Crisil. "It's no longer a few commodities that are driving this increase, it's becoming a little more broad-based," he says.

When onion prices spike, households can cut back on their consumption to some extent. But substituting rice, pulses and cooking oil is considerably harder.

And food inflation is not just about what farmers produce. It is also about how efficiently that food is stored, processed and moved to the market. Weaknesses at each stage can turn seasonal shortages into sharp price spikes - and gluts into waste.

Onionomics: Peeling away the layers of India's food economy

Onions, for instance, are particularly susceptible to seasonal price swings.

The rabi crop - winter crops, sown after the monsoon and harvested in spring - which accounts for roughly two-thirds of onion production, is harvested in March and April and stored for several months. As stocks dwindle, prices can rise sharply before the next crop arrives.

Tomatoes, meanwhile, tend to see price spikes during the summer months, when arrivals decline in some markets or heat stress affects production.

Sharma says perishables are often responsible for the sudden spikes in food inflation, while prices of staples such as rice and wheat tend to rise more gradually but can stay higher for longer.

"The perishables are what caused the volatility. The non-perishables, like rice and wheat, are the ones that structurally increase the cost," he says.

Economists say India still lacks adequate infrastructure to sort, grade, store and process much of its perishable produce.

Tomatoes, for example, can become so abundant during a glut that farmers struggle to sell them at remunerative prices, only for consumers to face sharply higher prices months later.

With nearly 60% of its farmland dependent on rainfall, India remains vulnerable to erratic monsoons

Better storage and processing could help turn some of that surplus into products such as puree, smoothing the extremes between abundance and scarcity, according to Sharma

Energy shocks feed into the cost of transport, irrigation and agricultural inputs. Fertiliser prices can rise. Global edible-oil markets matter because India relies heavily on imports.

To be true, India, however, has some protection against the worst effects of food inflation.

Government stocks of cereals are comfortably above prescribed minimum levels, and the free-food programme covering nearly 800 million people offers a cushion against rising prices. Rice stocks, external are particularly ample.

There is a wider economic risk, says Sengupta.

"Food inflation may begin with a supply shock, but its effects can spread. Workers who find that their wages no longer cover basic living costs may seek higher pay. Businesses facing higher labour costs may raise prices. What begins as a rise in the price of food can gradually become a more general inflationary pressure - the familiar wage-price spiral that central banks try to prevent," she says.

For India, this creates a particularly difficult balancing act.

Agriculture is a relatively low-productivity sector, and the government has long sought to protect farmers by setting minimum support prices (MSPs) for certain crops - a price floor at which it promises to buy produce, helping farmers guard against a market crash.

At the same time, food prices are politically sensitive. Sharp increases in the price of staples such as onions and tomatoes can quickly anger consumers, prompting governments to step in with measures such as restricting exports, limiting how much traders can stockpile, or otherwise trying to bring prices down.

The broader story of India's changing food consumption makes the current inflation more interesting.

Households are eating differently from a decade ago. An analysis, external of the 2011-12 and 2023-24 consumption surveys shows that the share of cereals in food spending has fallen sharply, particularly among the poorest households, reflecting the impact of subsidised food programmes.

Meanwhile, consumption of nutrient-rich foods such as dairy, fruit and meat has increased across income groups, with the biggest gains among the poorest, the study revealed.

India's food basket is becoming more diverse. But diversity is meaningful only if ordinary Indians can afford it. Ultimately, food inflation is about what makes it to the plate - and what has to be left out.

#12. Four years after huge asteroid crash, a spacecraft has now returned to assess the damage

The Hera spacecraft will study the 150m-wide (490ft) asteroid called Dimorphos

A spacecraft has arrived at an asteroid to investigate the damage caused by an unprecedented cosmic crash.

It what may sound like the plot of a disaster movie, Nasa's Dart spacecraft deliberately smashed into the space rock in 2022 to knock it off course.

The Nasa craft was obliterated on impact. Four years on, a European Space Agency (ESA) mission, called Hera, is scouring the scene to fully assess the aftermath of the collision - with the first images set to be released later on Wednesday.

The asteroid poses no threat, instead this was the first ever test of our planetary defences to see what action we could take if a space rock was heading for our planet.

"Hera is a sort of Inspector Columbo going back to the crash scene to investigate what is the outcome of this impact," said Patrick Michel, principal investigator of ESA's Hera mission.

This was the last image from Dart before it crashed into Dimorphos in 2022

The crash test focussed on a pair of asteroids: a larger one called Didymos - about 730m (2,400ft) wide - which is orbited by a smaller rock called Dimorphos, about 150m (490ft) wide.

The Dart spacecraft targeted Dimorphos, hurtling into the rock at 22,000km per hour (14,000mph). Images beamed back showed the asteroid's rocky surface coming ever closer until the feed cut out on impact.

It was such a big smash-up that the trail of debris, measuring about 10,000km long (6,200 miles), was spotted by telescopes on Earth and in space.

The crash showed it was possible to change the path of the smaller asteroid.

Before the collision it took Dimorphos 11 hours and 55 minutes to orbit around Didymos. After the Dart crash, the duration of its orbit had shrunk by about 33 minutes.

But the arrival of the second spacecraft, Hera, will help scientists to answer many more questions.

"Dart exploded in the asteroid, and therefore we don't have the information of what is its final state," explained Michel.

"The impact could have left a crater - and if it is the case, we need to understand the size and the depth. Or [the impact] may have entirely reshaped the asteroid."

A 10,000km-long (6,200 miles) debris trail was spotted by the Hubble Space Telescope

The Hera mission will spend the next year studying Dimorphos, and take a look at Didymos too.

The spacecraft is packed with instruments, and it also has two small shoe-boxed sized satellites onboard, which can study the impact site up close, and then later land on the asteroid.

A key task is to measure the mass of Dimorphos, to work out how effective the spacecraft's shove was.

This information would be vital if we ever spotted a space rock that was on a collision course with Earth.

"It would help us to predict what would happen if we had to hit another asteroid," said Prof Simon Green, a planetary scientist who is on Hera's science team.

"We [could] measure its properties and say: 'Well, we need this mass of spacecraft, travelling at this speed, this early, in order to deflect it enough so that it misses the Earth'."

Hera has already sent back an image of Didymos with Dimorphos in the background

Asteroids pose a significant threat to our planet.

The biggest, which measure more than a kilometre across, could cause global devastation. But medium-sized asteroids around the same dimensions as Dimorphos could wipe out a city.

"We all know what happened to the dinosaurs, right? And we don't want that to happen to us," said Carolyn Ernst, Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, who works on both the Dart and Hera missions.

"The good news for us is that there are far fewer potential threats out there in the Solar System than there were millions of years ago. But there are objects out there."

She said the biggest asteroids have been spotted, and none are heading to Earth.

"The smallest stuff isn't too much of a threat because our atmosphere protects us," she added.

"But there's a size range in the middle where we don't know where all of them are, and it could, if impacted, cause problems for humanity. And if we can find out about that and know about that, well, we sure as heck would want to try to stop that."

Hera has two small satellites onboard to study the asteroid up close

Scientists want to gather as much intelligence as they can about space rocks - to make us as well equipped as possible to deal with whatever the Universe throws at us.

A new mission is launching in 2027 to track potentially hazardous asteroids: a space telescope called the Near-Earth Object (NEO) Surveyor.

There are also more visits planned to asteroids, including to one called Apophis, which is about 450m (1,480ft) long.

Apophis was discovered in 2004 and it was initially thought it might hit the Earth as early as 13 April 2029.

But now scientists understand its orbit better, and say it won't hit us for at least the next 100 years. Instead of a crash in 2029, it will fly just 32,000km (20,000 miles) above our surface.

This will give scientists the rare chance to study a space rock up-close.

Two spacecraft will head to Apophis, including Nasa's Osiris-Apex (which previously grabbed a sample from another asteroid called Bennu) and Ramses, a joint mission between ESA and the Japanese Space Agency (JAXA).

#5. Russia's own figures show almost 500,000 deaths in war in Ukraine

Russia does not provide numbers of its fallen soldiers

At least 490,334 Russian soldiers have died since the start of the war in Ukraine in 2022, according to Russian government statistics analysed by the BBC.

The leaked Rosstat data reveal how the full-scale invasion of Ukraine has fundamentally changed mortality patterns in Russia, and show the high cost paid in human life in a war that Vladimir Putin had assumed would last only days but is now almost five years old.

BBC Russian has had access to more than 11 million death records from 2021 to September 2026, after a hack by Belarusian group Cyber Partisans BY and Ukraine's Silent Crow.

Russia's statistics agency admitted to a cyber-attack, but denied any protected information had been leaked.

Together with independent Russian outlet Mediazona, BBC Russian has compared the Rosstat data with its own list of confirmed military deaths, pulled from open sources since the start of the invasion, and it has found matches for 90% of the records.

The records show that 65% of 18 to 24 year-olds in Russia who lost their lives in 2025 died in the war. For those aged 25-34 the number was 59%. Only among 45 to 54 year-olds does cardiovascular disease claim more Russian lives than the conflict.

The data also reveal the extent of losses in specific areas of Ukraine. Some 20,000 Russian soldiers died fighting for control of 10 Ukrainian villages whose combined pre-war population was 12,000.

During the fight for one small village called Andriivka, south of the town of Bakhmut in Donetsk region, 1,122 Russians lost their lives. Before the war 74 people lived there.

Russia does not give public figures for losses sustained since the war began in February 2022. And two years ago Rosstat stopped publishing data of deaths from external causes, which was used to gauge the extent of military losses.

For example, only 70% of deaths we have established of soldiers from the occupied regions of Donetsk and Luhansk in eastern Ukraine who fought in the Russian army were accounted for in the Rosstat files.

The Rosstat dataset contains no names but does include ages, places of residence, places of death and also circumstances of death.

We have filtered the records first by location, leaving only Ukraine as well as Russian border regions that saw incursions by the Ukrainian army.

Next, we identified 425,174 entries explicitly indicating a military-related cause of death, such as explosions or shrapnel during combat operations.

As this count could include civilians, we filtered out women as well as men outside the 18-65 age group. And if someone lived and died in the four Ukrainian regions occupied by Russia, we excluded them as a possible civilian â unless he had a record of military service attached to his entry.

Another 14,093 entries were identified by looking at employment, and 51,067 were recorded in the database as deceased on the basis of a court ruling. This often happens when a soldier goes missing in a combat zone for more than six months and their body has not been recovered.

Again, we filtered out civilians by only counting deaths in the war zones in Ukraine and Russia.

BBC Russian's own detailed records of Russian losses has reached 290,012, verified together with Mediazona from official reports, social media, new memorials and graves. The actual death toll is likely to be higher, as not all deaths are reported publicly.

Overall, the Rosstat dataset confirms the trends that we have observed with our own count.

According to BBC Russian's analysis of the leaked files, in the first year of the war the average age of those killed was 34. By 2025, as prisoners and contract soldiers entered the ranks, it had risen to 40 (38 according to our own list).

Some of Russia's poorest regions in Eastern Siberia account for a disproportionately large share of the losses. Men from Tyva, Buryatia and Zabaykalsky Krai are 20-25 times more likely to die in the war than residents of the biggest cities of Moscow and St Petersburg.

In most regions, the distribution of deaths based on Rosstat data differs from that in the BBC's list by just a few tenths of a percentage point.

At least 5,017 foreigners are among those who died fighting for Russia, according to our analysis of the Rosstat files, including 1,031 Uzbeks and 720 Tajiks. We have left out Ukrainians from this count to avoid including civilians.

This is a conservative estimate as dual nationals are listed as Russians in the database, and there is no reference to North Koreans who died repelling the Ukrainian incursion into Russia in 2024.

The BBC identified 2,304 North Korean soldiers who died there by analysing satellite imagery and official photographs of a newly built memorial in Pyongyang.

In the context of a war that has lasted four years and 10 months, these are high numbers for Russia, when compared with the Soviet war in Afghanistan that ended in 1989.
