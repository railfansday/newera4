#144. The $500 billion beauty industry's 'green' ambitions are a patchwork at best. And they're falling short

The escalating climate crisis is shifting many people’s purchasing patterns and this extends to the $500 billion dollar global beauty industry which is grappling with a range of sustainability challenges across product manufacturing, packaging and disposal.

'Delay means death': We're running out of ways to adapt to the climate crisis, new report shows. Here are the key takeaways

Strategy and consulting firm Simon Kucher’s Global Sustainability Study 2021 found 60% of consumers around the world rated sustainability as an important purchase criterion, and 35% were willing to pay more for sustainable products or services.

This shift in consumer preferences has propelled many beauty brands to set environmental goals: to move away from single-use and virgin plastics, provide recyclable, reusable and refillable packaging and offer more transparency around products’ ingredients so customers can ascertain how “green” their purchase is.

However, consumers still struggle to understand the sustainability credentials of many products, according to the British Beauty Council. This is because the industry’s clean-up efforts have been inconsistent, and fall short of making a recognizable impact in the absence of collective goal-setting, global strategy and standardized regulations.

There is no international standard for the beauty industry on how much product ingredient information to share with customers — or how to do so. Brands can set their own rules and goals, giving rise to confusion and “greenwashing,” where sustainability claims are often touted but not substantiated.

Companies often use marketing language like “clean beauty” to make it seem like their products are natural, for example, when they may not actually be organic, sustainable or ethically made.

After rebranding in the West, many beauty companies are still offering to 'whiten' skin elsewhere

“The term ‘clean beauty’ has become quite dangerous. It’s used to sell more products,” according to British Beauty Council CEO Millie Kendall, who added that such buzzwords are losing traction in the UK as British customers wise up to their shortcomings. “Customers need better marketing information and certification information.”

In a 2021 report calling on the industry to have “the courage to change” their business practices, the British Beauty Council wrote that, all too often, even natural ingredients involved in manufacturing products give way to “over-consumption, non-regenerative farming practices, pollution, waste and neglect.”

“The only way out of this is transparency,” Kendall told CNN.

Jen Lee, chief impact officer at US-based brand Beautycounter, said she continues to see confusion over ingredients among consumers. (In 2013, the company launched and published “The Never List,” which currently cites more than 2,800 chemicals — including heavy metals, parabens and formaldehyde — it claims to never use in its products.)

“Natural vs. synthetic ingredients has been a conversation. People think natural is safer, but it’s not always the case,” Lee explained. “Natural ingredients formulated in the industry can have toxic load. Heavy metals can occur in natural components of the earth.”

Mother loses peripheral vision from apparent exposure to mercury in beauty creams. Toxic levels in her home put family at risk, say experts

“We used to be more natural and organic,” added Sasha Plavsic, founder of makeup brand ILIA Beauty. “What was challenging is (that) raw materials were difficult to source or would come in inconsistently or products wouldn’t perform.”

Most makeup is created and molded at high temperatures, Plavsic explained. Purely organic materials often fall apart in this heat, leading to inconsistent results and subpar product performance. “Not every synthetic is bad,” Plavsic said. “Sometimes, it helps create the best in class formula.”

The industry’s plastic packaging is a particular sustainability challenge — 95% is thrown away and the vast majority is not recycled, according to the British Beauty Council.

The cosmetics business is the fourth biggest plastic packaging user globally — after food and beverage, industrial packaging and pharmaceuticals — and plastic is about 67% of the industry’s packaging volume, according to Vantage Market Research. Beauty giant L’Oreal used 144,430 metric tons of plastic in its packaging material in 2021, for example, according to the Ellen Macarthur Foundation (EMF). Estee Lauder Companies reported its brands produced 71,600 metric tons of plastic in product packaging that same year.

And only 9% of the global plastic waste is recycled, according to a report from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. The United States only recycles 4% of its plastic waste.

Single-use plastic is wreaking havoc on the planet. Here's what you can do to minimize your impact

Many brands are trying to phase out harmful plastics from their operations and adopt post-consumer recycled (PCR) plastic. (L’Oreal has set a target of 50% PCR plastic usage by 2025, while Estee Lauder is targeting 25% “or more” PCR plastic — but both are far from achieving their targets.)

“Between 60-70 major global brands have made unprecedented progress” in PCR plastic usage across industries, EMF’s Plastic Initiative Lead Sander DeFruyt told CNN. But DeFruyt stressed that PCR plastic must be adopted in conjunction with brands removing single and virgin plastics from their usage cycles to truly make a difference.

However, PCR plastic is not easy to find — low recycling rates around the world mean there is limited supply. Meanwhile, demand for it is growing demand across industries, DeFruyt said. This competition hikes up its price, which is already higher than virgin plastic.

Hair care brand FEKKAI claims that it used up to 95% PCR content in its packaging, but pricing and supply issues posed a challenge, forcing it to currently aim for containers and packaging that feature at least 50% PCR in its packaging.

“PCR plastic is more expensive than stock plastic. The cost is hard and then sourcing it is too,” founder Frédéric Fekkai told CNN. “PCR is close to our heart, but there is a massive demand, so finding recycled plastic is difficult.”

Beauty retailers plays a pivotal — and under-utilized — role, with control over stocking decisions and supply chains. But many vary when it comes to the standards they set for brands they sell.

“Smaller businesses do more, full stop,” said Jessi Baker, founder of the technology platform Provenance, which helps brands display their sustainability credentials for customers. “They move more nimbly. Some of them are born-good brands — climate friendliness was part of their setup. They don’t need to restructure their entire supply chain. Their culture already has it compared to the larger brands who need to work hard to change.”

A history of beauty trends -- and the standards that shaped them

Sephora launched its “Clean + Planet Positive” initiative in 2021, which labeled products that met its set criteria. (This is separate from the French retailer’s “Clean at Sephora” program, which is currently facing a consumer lawsuit alleging it carries a significant percentage of products understood by customers to be harmful.) Target launched a similar program in 2022, featuring a “Target Zero” icon for both online and in-store offerings that either have reusable, recyclable, compostable or reduced plastic packaging, or feature waterless or concentrated products.

Still, many steps taken by brands and retailers do not even begin to touch on the waste and pollution generated throughout supply chains, manufacturing and shipping, all huge problems for the industry to grapple with.

The gaps in standardization in the beauty ecosystem can, to some extent, be filled by certifications such as the US-born B Corporation, or B Corp. This accreditation, one of the most well-known in the beauty space, is issued by the non-profit B Lab, which scores a company on a variety of criteria around ethics and sustainability. However beneficial it may be among eco-conscious consumers, though, it is currently completely voluntary for brands to apply for.

Governments and multinationals enforcing regulations and setting a base line for brands to operate from when making sustainability claims would go a long way to making change, many experts and business leaders believe.

Susanne Kaufmann, founder of her namesake beauty brand, says her efforts in Austria would reap better results if more countries around the world had stricter, more uniform garbage disposal laws.

A trash heap 62 meters high shows the scale of India's climate challenge

“I package our product in a recyclable material,” Kaufmann said. (Her products’ packaging, which is refillable and reusable, is made from 75% recycled plastic — and is 100% recyclable.) If I send this to the US, the garbage is not separated… and it’s not recyclable,” she explained, referring to inconsistencies in recycling laws across the United States.

And when it comes to ingredients, the European Chemicals Agency lists 2,495 substances banned from use in cosmetic products marketed for sale or use in the bloc. But the US Food and Drug administration only lists 11, making it more challenging for American consumers to find safer, greener options. The Environmental Working Group, a non-profit watchdog, studied lab tests of 51 sunscreen products in 2021 and found that only 35% of products met the EU standard, compared with 94% that passed the US standard.

However, while government can set minimum requirements, Mia Davis, vice president of sustainability and impact at beauty retailer Credo Beauty, says the needle will move in the private sector.

Sustainable ships: The world's most eco-conscious cruises

“Regulation can raise the floor a bit. A person who doesn’t know about any (sustainability issues) should still be able to walk into a bodega and get clean products… But that’s never going to be what the market can do,” she said. “Market leadership is key.”

In the absence of bold regulations or global standards on sustainability practices, this “leadership” — undertaken both by brands and customers in the beauty marketplace — is likely to be the most immediately impactful vector for addressing the industry’s climate shortcomings. It will take continued collective advocacy and initiative to see meaningful climate-conscious change.

#142. UK ministers told not to engage formally with Indian mega-zoo after lobbying

Guardian and Mongabay reveal approaches to officials and MPs on behalf of Vantara zoo ahead of UK-chaired Cites meeting on endangered animal imports

British government ministers were told not to engage formally with Vantara, the controversial mega-zoo run by the Indian billionaire Anant Ambani, after lobbying efforts by the private animal sanctuary in the run-up to a UK-chaired meeting on the trade in endangered species, according to documents.

This week, the UK heads a 32-country committee meeting in Geneva of Cites, the United Nations body that regulates trade in endangered species. The meeting will examine how India has responded to compliance concerns about imports of endangered animals.

Documents obtained by the Guardian and the news outlet Mongabay under the UK’s Freedom of Information Act reveal Vantara’s efforts to lobby British officials and ministers before the meeting.

Ambani, 31, is the heir to the oil and gas fortune of one of India’s richest men, Mukesh Ambani. He set up a 1,400-hectare (3,500-acre) private zoo near his family’s oil-refining facilities in Gujarat, where more than 150,000 animals have reportedly been amassed since 2022. Vantara boasts high welfare standards for animals in its care, including facilities such as an elephant hydrotherapy pool, where more than 260 elephants can also receive oil therapies and Ayurvedic massage.

But conservation groups have repeatedly questioned how such a vast number of species, many of which are endangered, were gathered so quickly. Last year, India’s supreme court cleared the zoo’s operators of any wrongdoing.

A subsequent Cites fact-finding mission looking into the zoo raised concerns about India’s compliance with international rules on animal imports in 2025. It recommended temporary restrictions on India, although the recommendation was later withdrawn.

Its November 2025 report said: “There is a need for caution to ensure that imports of large numbers of animals by these facilities are not inadvertently creating a demand for illegally sourced animals.”

In May, officials from the UK’s Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra) met a representative from Vantara and the US lobbyist firm Checkmate Government Relations, founded by Ches McDowell, a hunting friend of Donald Trump Jr.

Before the meeting, internal briefing documents said Checkmate was “seeking to persuade the UK to take a more sympathetic view of India’s handling of imports to Vantara and of Checkmate’s argument that responsibility for verifying legality sits primarily with exporting or re-exporting parties rather than with India as importer”.

During the meeting, the Vantara representative invited UK officials to visit the zoo – an invitation that was subsequently declined to ensure the impartiality of the UK’s Cites chair role.

After the meeting, a representative from another lobbying firm, Atlas Strategy, working on behalf of Vantara, approached the then secretary of state, Emma Reynolds, and then nature minister, Mary Creagh, also raising the prospect of a visit to the zoo.

When a department official asked for advice, a note was issued recommending that ministers did not engage formally with Vantara.

The government documents also reveal concerns about an application by Vantara to import falcons and falcon hybrids, after an unnamed organisation made a £50,000 “donation” to a breeder in the UK, whose identity was also not disclosed. British Cites officials classified the transaction as commercial, according to the documents. The falcons were not exported to India.

A spokesperson for Vantara said it had not imported falcons from the UK, nor had the zoo hired or paid a lobbying firm. It is understood that Checkmate and Altas Strategy were working pro bono for Vantara and not required to register with the Office of the Registrar of Consultant Lobbyists.

After recent scrutiny, Vantara has sought to “reset” its activities by voluntarily suspending animal imports for a year and hiring John Scanlon, a former Cites chief, to oversee its operations. In an interview with the Sunday Times this year, Ambani said he only brought animals to Vantara legally to ensure their welfare, mentioning a group of hippos he had saved from a hunting lodge in South Africa and jackass penguins saved from a shopping centre in Qatar.

“I think since I was a baby I have been taking care of animals. It’s always been a passion,” he said.

Vantara was used to host Ambani’s lavish pre-wedding celebrations in 2024, which were attended by celebrity guests and politicians. In September, Newcastle University awarded the oil heir a professorship of practice for “his work in conservation, biodiversity protection, animal welfare and environmental stewardship”, sparking a backlash from conservationists.

A Defra spokesperson said: “The UK has strict rules in place to protect endangered species and ensuring international wildlife trade is legal and sustainable, with every application assessed individually against internationally agreed and domestic requirements.”

#138. Hundreds of millions at risk from Chinese shopping app malware

It is one of China’s most popular shopping apps, selling clothing, groceries and just about everything else under the sun to more than 750 million users a month.

But according to cybersecurity researchers, it can also bypass users’ cell phone security to monitor activities on other apps, check notifications, read private messages and change settings.

And once installed, it’s tough to remove.

In a detailed investigation, CNN spoke to half a dozen cybersecurity teams from Asia, Europe and the United States — as well as multiple former and current Pinduoduo employees — after receiving a tipoff.

Multiple experts identified the presence of malware on the Pinduoduo app that exploited vulnerabilities in Android operating systems. Company insiders said the exploits were utilized to spy on users and competitors, allegedly to boost sales.

“We haven’t seen a mainstream app like this trying to escalate their privileges to gain access to things that they’re not supposed to gain access to,” said Mikko Hyppönen, chief research officer at WithSecure, a Finnish cybersecurity firm.

“This is highly unusual, and it is pretty damning for Pinduoduo.”

Malware, short for malicious software, refers to any software developed to steal data or interfere with computer systems and mobile devices.

Evidence of sophisticated malware in the Pinduoduo app comes amid intense scrutiny of Chinese-developed apps like TikTok over concerns about data security.

Some American lawmakers are pushing for a national ban on the popular short-video app, whose CEO Shou Chew was grilled by Congress for five hours last week about its relations with the Chinese government.

The revelations are also likely to draw more attention to Pinduoduo’s international sister app, Temu, which is topping US download charts and fast expanding in other Western markets. Both are owned by Nasdaq-listed PDD, a multinational company with roots in China.

While Temu has not been implicated, Pinduoduo’s alleged actions risk casting a shadow over its sister app’s global expansion.

There is no evidence that Pinduoduo has handed data to the Chinese government. But as Beijing enjoys significant leverage over businesses under its jurisdiction, there are concerns from US lawmakers that any company operating in China could be forced to cooperate with a broad range of security activities.

The findings follow Google’s suspension of Pinduoduo from its Play Store in March, citing malware identified in versions of the app.

An ensuing report from Bloomberg said a Russian cybersecurity firm had also identified potential malware in the app.

Pinduoduo has previously rejected “the speculation and accusation that Pinduoduo app is malicious.”

CNN has contacted PDD multiple times over email and phone for comment, but has not received a response.

Pinduoduo, which boasts a user base that accounts for three quarters of China’s online population and a market value three times that of eBay (EBAY), wasn’t always an online shopping behemoth.

Founded in 2015 in Shanghai by Colin Huang, a former Google employee, the startup was fighting to establish itself in a market long dominated by e-commerce stalwarts Alibaba (BABA) and JD.com (JD).

It succeeded by offering steep discounts on friends-and-family group buying orders and focusing on lower-income rural areas.

Pinduoduo posted triple digit growth in monthly users until the end of 2018, the year it listed in New York. By the middle of 2020, though, the increase in monthly users had slowed to around 50% and would continue to decline, according to its earnings reports.

It was in 2020, according to a current Pinduoduo employee, that the company set up a team of about 100 engineers and product managers to dig for vulnerabilities in Android phones, develop ways to exploit them — and turn that into profit.

According to the source, who requested anonymity for fear of reprisals, the company only targeted users in rural areas and smaller towns initially, while avoiding users in megacities such as Beijing and Shanghai.

“The goal was to reduce the risk of being exposed,” they said.

By collecting expansive data on user activities, the company was able to create a comprehensive portrait of users’ habits, interests and preferences, according to the source.

This allowed it to improve its machine learning model to offer more personalized push notifications and ads, attracting users to open the app and place orders, they said.

The team was disbanded in early March, the source added, after questions about their activities came to light.

PDD didn’t reply to CNN’s repeated requests for comment on the team.

Approached by CNN, researchers from Tel Aviv-based cyber firm Check Point Research, Delaware-based app security startup Oversecured and Hyppönen’s WithSecure conducted independent analysis of the 6.49.0 version of the app, released on Chinese app stores in late February.

Google Play is not available in China, and Android users in the country download their apps from local stores. In March, when Google suspended Pinduoduo, it said it had found malware in off-Play versions of the app.

The researchers found code designed to achieve “privilege escalation”: a type of cyberattack that exploits a vulnerable operating system to gain a higher level of access to data than it’s supposed to have, according to experts.

“Our team has reverse engineered that code and we can confirm that it tries to escalate rights, tries to gain access to things normal apps wouldn’t be able to do on Android phones,” said Hyppönen.

The app was able to continue running in the background and prevent itself from being uninstalled, which allowed it to boost its monthly active user rates, Hyppönen said. It also had the ability to spy on competitors by tracking activity on other shopping apps and getting information from them, he added.

Check Point Research additionally identified ways in which the app was able to evade scrutiny.

The app deployed a method that allowed it to push updates without an app store review process meant to detect malicious applications, the researchers said.

They also identified in some plug-ins the intent to obscure potentially malicious components by hiding them under legitimate file names, such as Google’s.

“Such a technique is widely used by malware developers that inject malicious code into applications that have legitimate functionality,” they said.

In China, about three quarters of smartphone users are on the Android system. Apple (AAPL)’s iPhone has 25% market share, according to Daniel Ives of Wedbush Securities.

Sergey Toshin, the founder of Oversecured, said Pinduoduo’s malware specifically targeted different Android-based operating systems, including those used by Samsung, Huawei, Xiaomi and Oppo.

CNN has reached out to these companies for comment.

Toshin described Pinduoduo as “the most dangerous malware” ever found among mainstream apps.

“I’ve never seen anything like this before. It’s like, super expansive,” he said.

Most phone manufacturers globally customize the core Android software, the Android Open Source Project (AOSP), to add unique features and applications to their own devices.

Toshin found Pinduoduo to have exploited about 50 Android system vulnerabilities. Most of the exploits were tailor made for customized parts known as the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) code, which tends to be audited less often than AOSP and is therefore more prone to vulnerabilities, he said.

Pinduoduo also exploited a number of AOSP vulnerabilities, including one which was flagged by Toshin to Google in February 2022. Google fixed the bug this March, he said.

According to Toshin, the exploits allowed Pinduoduo access to users’ locations, contacts, calendars, notifications and photo albums without their consent. They were also able to change system settings and access users’ social network accounts and chats, he said.

Of the six teams CNN spoke to for this story, three did not conduct full examinations. But their primary reviews showed that Pinduoduo asked for a large number of permissions beyond the normal functions of a shopping app.

They included “potentially invasive permissions” such as “set wallpaper” and “download without notification,” said René Mayrhofer, head of the Institute of Networks and Security at the Johannes Kepler University Linz in Austria.

Suspicions about malware in Pinduoduo’s app were first raised in late February in a report by a Chinese cybersecurity firm called Dark Navy. Even though the analysis didn’t directly name the shopping giant, the report spread quickly among other researchers, who did name the company. Some of the analysts followed up with their own reports confirming the original findings.

Soon after, on March 5, Pinduoduo issued a new update of its app, version 6.50.0, which removed the exploits, according to two experts who CNN spoke to.

Two days after the update, Pinduoduo disbanded the team of engineers and product managers who had developed the exploits, according to the Pinduoduo source.

The next day, team members found themselves locked out of Pinduoduo’s bespoke workplace communication app, Knock, and lost access to files on the company’s internal network. Engineers also found their access to big data, data sheets and the log system revoked, the source said.

Most of the team were transferred to work at Temu. They were assigned to different departments at the subsidiary, with some working on marketing or developing push notifications, according to the source.

A core group of about 20 cybersecurity engineers who specialize in finding and exploiting vulnerabilities remain at Pinduoduo, they said.

Toshin of Oversecured, who looked into the update, said although the exploits were removed, the underlying code was still there and could be reactivated to carry out attacks.

Pinduoduo has been able to grow its user base against a backdrop of the Chinese government’s regulatory clampdown on Big Tech that began in late 2020.

That year, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology launched a sweeping crackdown on apps that illegally collect and use personal data.

The Personal Information Protection Law stipulates that no party should illegally collect, process or transmit personal information. They’re also banned from exploiting internet-related security vulnerabilities or engaging in actions that endanger cybersecurity.

Pinduoduo’s apparent malware would be a violation of those laws, tech policy experts say, and should have been detected by the regulator.

“This would be embarrassing for the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, because this is their job,” said Kendra Schaefer, a tech policy expert at Trivium China, a consultancy. “They’re supposed to check Pinduoduo, and the fact that they didn’t find (anything) is embarrassing for the regulator.”

Pinduoduo did not appear on any of the lists.

#137. The private creative world of Rabindranath Tagore, the man Yeats called ‘greater than any of us’

A previously unseen family archive offers a glimpse into the Indian Nobel prize winner’s artistic process and personal relationships in his final years

Don’t get This is India delivered to your inbox? Sign up here

It’s easy to run out of words to describe Rabindranath Tagore. The first non-European to win the Nobel prize in literature, Tagore was a Bengali poet, musician, novelist, philosopher, songwriter, painter, humanist and educator.

Born in 1861, Tagore left an indelible mark on the subcontinent’s literature, music, education and art. Among his enduring legacies are the national anthems of India and Bangladesh.

As Nobel laureate economist Amartya Sen once wrote, Tagore was “a towering figure in the millennium-old literature of Bengal”, whose songs resonate across eastern India and Bangladesh, while his ideas on education, nationalism and freedom of the mind remain relevant today.

Yet his work also made a deep impression on the literary world far beyond the shores of India. WB Yeats is said to have described Tagore as “greater than any of us”. Gitanjali, the collection of poems that won Tagore the Nobel prize that same year, was praised by the committee for its “profoundly sensitive, fresh and beautiful verse”. Tagore’s English translation was published in London that March and reprinted 10 times by the time the prize was announced in November.

Tagore’s life and works have, of course, been well documented, but a collection of material preserved in a private family archive is now being brought into public view for the first time at an exhibition by Christie’s, opening in Mumbai this month. The archive draws from Tagore’s visits to the family home of his protege, Maitreyi Devi, in the bucolic village of Mungpoo in Darjeeling between 1938 and 1940. He died in 1941.

The collection on display – artworks, manuscripts, letters, photographs and personal effects – offers an intimate glimpse into Tagore’s creative process and personal world in his final years.

I spoke to the exhibition curator, Nishad Avari, head of south Asian modern and contemporary art at Christie’s, about the stories behind the archive.

One instructive document Avari points to is a manuscript from 1940. It appears to be a draft of Tagore’s memoir Chelebela or My Boyhood Days, in which he reflects on his life – from growing up in a large family in Calcutta (modern-day Kolkata) to the rapidly changing world ushered in by electricity and the motorcar.

For Avari, what stood out was how the ideas in the document echoed the educational philosophy Tagore put into practice in Santiniketan (or Abode of Peace), the experimental school he set up about 100 miles from the state capital.

“He speaks about how education is like being in prison and that it should be open and free … about the idea of wonder and even fear as inspiring creativity,” Avari told me.

“That’s a direct relationship to Santiniketan and the open model of the school where people sat under trees with their gurus and learned in the open. Education didn’t feel like a restriction, prison or imposition,” he added.

A proposed trial of a hepatitis B vaccine on newborn babies in Guinea-Bissau has been cancelled following accusations that the trial was "unethical" and "neocolonial".

The trial was funded by the US, under Health Secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr, who has a long record of expressing scepticism about vaccines.

Police have seized a batch of vaccines from the research centre at the heart of the controversy.

The proposed trial drew international criticism because it planned to withhold a vaccine recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) from half of the participating newborns.

Some public health experts argued that the proposed trial raised broader questions about whether vulnerable populations in African countries were being asked to take risks that researchers would not accept in wealthier countries.

The BBC has asked the US department of health and human services for comment.

The study was planned at the Bandim Health Project, a research centre that has operated in Guinea-Bissau since 1978.

It was designed to involve 14,000 newborns, with half receiving a hepatitis B vaccine shortly after birth and the other half not being given the shot.

Bandim said the trial aimed to investigate whether the hepatitis B vaccine given at birth has broader health effects beyond preventing infection.

Previous studies in The Gambia and Guinea-Bissau have raised questions about a possible link to increased mortality.

Researchers argued that because Guinea-Bissau does not currently offer routine hepatitis B vaccinations at birth, despite WHO recommendations, they have a unique opportunity to study its effects before such vaccines are introduced in 2028.

But critics questioned the ethics of withholding the vaccine from some babies, particularly in one of the world's poorest countries, where the infection is widespread.

The Trump administration funded the proposed study, with the CDC awarding the University of Southern Denmark (SDU) $1.6m (Â£1.2m) in December 2025.

Walter Kazadi Mulombo, the WHO representative in Guinea-Bissau, told the BBC they became concerned after receiving questions about the study and finding no publicly available protocol.

He said the main ethical concern was that the trial would withhold an intervention already proven to protect newborns against hepatitis B.

"The vaccine has been used for more than 30 years in more than 115 countries," he said. "A study that will withhold that intervention from other newborns is unethical."

He warned that babies could be exposed to the virus during childbirth if their mothers were infected, as well as later in life.

#41. Brazil publishes first global ‘roadmap’ for phasing out fossil fuels

All countries can start to break their dependence on fossil fuels now without needing to wait for new technologies or economic changes, Brazil’s government has said ahead of a global climate summit.

Brazil, the host of last year’s UN climate conference, set out the world’s first global “roadmap” for phasing out fossil fuels on Tuesday, showing that countries could boost their economies and pull people out of poverty while breaking the link with coal, oil and gas.

But Joeri Rogelj, a professor of climate science and policy at Imperial College London, said that although the Brazilian roadmaps represented a step forward, they were limited. “The roadmaps are valuable but fall short of clearly indicating the pace of change needed by including no timeline or key benchmarks,” he said.

“Because of this omission, they still leave much to be imagined about what is needed to achieve the Paris agreement’s ultimate goal: limiting global warming to well below 2C, and preferably to 1.5C, to avoid dangerous climate harm.”

André Corrêa do Lago, Brazil’s president of the Cop30 summit, said the roadmaps were intended to help move the annual UN Cops away from their previous focus on negotiating global agreements to tackle the climate crisis, and on to implementing the targets that had already been agreed. Cop31 takes place in Antalya, Turkey, this month.

“They are documents of implementation,” Corrêa do Lago told the Guardian in an interview. “There is a different group of people that negotiate and that implement. The problem we’ve had at many Cops is to think that the same people who negotiate might also need to coordinate the implementation – this is not the case.”

He said the roadmaps should be taken up by ministries of finance, transportation, energy and infrastructure to work on how they could be implemented in each country.

Brazil hosted the Cop30 summit last November in the coastal city of Belém, in the Amazon rainforest, where a bitter dispute broke out over whether the final set of resolutions should include pledges on fossil fuels. As a compromise, the presidency agreed to produce two roadmaps, one on “transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems in a just orderly and equitable manner” and the other on halting and reversing deforestation.

These roadmaps are separate from similarly named plans from governments that met after Cop30 at a conference in Santa Marta, Colombia, in April, to discuss the transition away from fossil fuels. At that conference, nearly 60 countries formed a new “coalition of the willing”, separate to Cops, to set out their own national roadmaps to phase out fossil fuels. Several countries have since produced national plans, including Germany, France and the Netherlands.

The roadmaps produced by governments under the Santa Marta initiative are much more specific and prescriptive than Brazil’s, as each of those is written by the national government responsible for implementing the policies recommended.

The Brazilian roadmaps only set out steps that are possible. They are not binding, they contain no deadlines or milestones for actions, and they leave it to countries themselves to “diagnose” their degree of dependence on fossil fuels and select steps to reduce it.

“Every country will have a different rhythm [for moving away from fossil fuels] – it’s very important to concentrate on the diagnoses of countries’ circumstances,” Corrêa do Lago said. “Based on these diagnoses, they can choose their levers [of policy] and implement the transition.”

#8. Sinkholes, scarred land and scapegoats: the scourge of illegal gold mining in Johannesburg

There is no end in sight for the drivers of illegal mining, while police action appears ineffective

Cora Bailey has been encountering illegal miners ever since she first started treating injured animals in the townships around Roodepoort, west of Johannesburg, in 1991. But as the price of gold has soared, so too has activity in the abandoned mine shafts that mark the area.

In late September, dozens of zama zamas – the name given to illegal miners in South Africa, meaning roughly “to take a chance” in Zulu – arrived to dig for gold across the dirt road from her charity, Claw (Community Led Animal Welfare).

Soon, they were taking water for their panning and processing from the organisation’s two 25,000-litre water tanks, which are filled irregularly by the city government for the 100 or so rescued and injured animals on site.

“We’ve asked them really nicely and haven’t ever refused them water to drink … But, for the first time ever, we closed the gate at 12 [midday], because we literally had about two buckets of water left,” Bailey said in mid-October.

Police told her that the scale of the illegal mining was too big for them to do anything about, she said at the time. About a week later, officers sheared away the grass that had partly hidden the zama zamas. But within a few days a smaller group had returned, although Bailey said they were now taking Claw’s water only for drinking.

South Africa’s police regularly trumpet their success in tackling illegal mining. On 19 October, they said they had arrested 71 suspected zama zamas across the country in the previous week.

However, researchers put the number of illegal miners in the tens of thousands. In 2019, Marcena Hunter, a researcher at the Global Initiative against Transnational Organised Crime, estimated that a tenth of South Africa’s gold production, worth about $1bn, was being exported illegally.

Police operations have also led to tragedy. In 2025, 93 bodies were pulled from a mine shaft in Stilfontein, after officers blocked food and water from being delivered underground.

Meanwhile, there is no end in sight to the dynamics driving illegal mining, including the high price of gold, poverty, unemployment, corrupt politicians and police profiting, and the failure of mining companies to fulfil their legal obligation to seal mining shafts when they cease operating.

South Africa’s modern history is tied to mining. In 1884, gold was found on a farm, sparking a gold rush that led to the creation of Johannesburg. Gold production peaked in the 1970s, but South Africa was the world’s largest producer, a title it held for more than a century, until 2007.

In 2009, Morris Viljoen, a geology professor at the University of the Witwatersrand, estimated that the reef that gave the university its name had produced more than a third of the gold ever mined globally. Claw is housed in the old offices of the Durban Deep mine, which shut at the turn of the century, having once lent its name to an Elton John song.

The city government claims there is still 40,000 tonnes of gold underground in the region. But, with the remainder lying ever deeper, the costs of recovering it have risen.

Mining companies are supposed to seal up old mine shafts and rehabilitate the environment around them. However, in 2025 there were “6,100 derelict and ownerless mines and 1,170 unsafe mine openings”, the mining minister, Gwede Mantashe, said in response to a parliamentary question.

Zama zamas have stepped into those voids left in the earth. While there is no comprehensive data on them, experts agree that a majority are from neighbouring countries, principally Lesotho, Mozambique and Zimbabwe.

The skyline south of Johannesburg is punctuated by the pale yellow ridges of mine dumps. Increasingly, the ground is also caving open.

For politicians seeking votes in local elections on 4 November, the culprit is clear: illegal miners. In May, Kenny Kunene, the Johannesburg council member responsible for transport, took journalists on a tour of sinkholes that he claimed were the fault of zama zamas, including a section of Wemmer Pan Road that had completely collapsed.

The road was once the main artery linking the city’s central business district to the south-eastern suburbs. In one section it has completely disappeared, now raw earth pitted with the holes where illegal miners have been digging.

However, when it comes to sinkholes, experts say zama zamas are being scapegoated.

Rosalind Morris, an anthropology professor at Columbia University, who has worked extensively with illegal miners, noted that the porous dolomitic rock to Johannesburg’s south and west becomes unstable when water is removed.

“The biggest culprit of sinkholes in South Africa is formal industrial mining, which dewaters at a scale of hundreds of millions of gallons of water removed from underground every day,” she said.

“There are areas where there is a lot of residual gold, which are being very intensely mined by zama zamas, and some of that definitely causes structural collapse, there’s no doubt about it. But the idea that this was invented or caused for the first time by zama zamas is completely fallacious.”

Zama zamas are also frequently blamed for violence in mining areas, including a September mass shooting in Wedela township, west of Johannesburg. However, David van Wyk, the lead researcher at Bench Marks Foundation, said culpability laid with the often-feuding gangs that controlled access to the shafts.

“The guys working in the mine are actually super-exploited cheap labour … The guys with the guns are the guys who take the gold,” he said. “They don’t go underground.”

In Khutsong, about 50 miles (80km) west of Johannesburg, illegal miners, local and foreign, kept the township afloat, said Vusi Cele, the youth secretary for the local branch of Mining Affected Communities United in Action, a campaign group that advocates for “artisanal miners” to be granted mining licences. “Without those rents, it’s a ghost town,” he said.

While Cele, 33, currently hustles above ground selling mobile phone covers and screen protectors, he said he too would mine if he could get a licence. “On the surface there’s peanuts,” he said. “Underground there’s money.”

The African National Congress is on course for a bruising result in South Africa’s local elections on Wednesday as voters go to the polls amid deep discontent over water outages, power cuts and crumbling infrastructure.

The former liberation movement, which Nelson Mandela led to power in 1994 at the end of apartheid as South Africa’s first black president, is still likely to be the largest party. However, polls showed it getting less than a third of the vote, down from 45.6% at the last municipal elections in 2021 and 40.2% in 2024’s national elections.

In Kensington, a diverse Johannesburg suburb that has sustained repeated water and power outages in recent weeks, voters said they wanted to see change. The city is currently led by Dada Morero, the ANC mayor, in coalition with the far-left Economic Freedom Fighters (EFF) and the rightwing populist Patriotic Alliance (PA).

“Service delivery is bad, it’s horrible,” said Tshepiso Makotse, 26, a call centre worker. “Water, electricity – everything, basically.”

Makotse said she had split her two votes, casting one for the centre-right Democratic Alliance (DA) local councillor and her party vote for the leftwing uMkhonto weSizwe (MK) party of the former ANC president Jacob Zuma. “I just want change,” she said.

Terrence Pieters, a retired boilermaker, said: “The infrastructure in Johannesburg is shot.” He declined to say which party he had voted for, other than it was “one of the offshoots of the DA”.

“I would like to see things get better and great people in charge,” said the 72-year-old. “But … there’s no one party that’s going to take over here. It’s going to be a circus, it’s going to be a coalition.”

Vote fragmentation means the ANC could still end up leading many city coalition governments while losing control of others.

Unstable coalitions are already a feature of many of South Africa’s 257 municipalities, and analysts point to their high turnover as a major reason for poor service delivery.

Johannesburg, a city of 4.8 million people that has faced water shortages for more than a week, has had 10 mayors in as many years. The neighbouring municipality of Ekurhuleni has had three mayors since 2021. To the north, in Tshwane, which contains the capital, Pretoria, the mayoralty has rotated between parties four times since 2020. In October, more than 800 people contracted a gastrointestinal illness from contaminated water in Bronkhorstspruit, in east Tshwane.

The ANC’s national leadership is open to working with the DA in Gauteng, according to South African media, mirroring the government of national unity, where the two parties are the largest in the national coalition. However, local ANC leaders are reportedly not in favour of a DA tie-up. Currently, the ANC governs in various coalitions across the province’s three metros, including under an ActionSA mayor in Tshwane.

In Johannesburg, the DA’s Helen Zille has led a spirited campaign, with viral stunts including the 75-year-old snorkelling in a pothole and kayaking along a flooded street. However, the former Cape Town mayor is still viewed with suspicion by many, especially black voters, due to past comments appearing to excuse colonialism. Some voters also point to soaring housing costs in Cape Town, which has been run by the DA since 2006, as a reason not to vote for Zille.

The former Johannesburg DA mayor Herman Mashaba, who now leads ActionSA, which is polling around 10%, has ruled out working with Zille in Johannesburg. Mashaba has called Zille a “racist”, an allegation she has always fervently denied. The ANC, for its part, has three possible mayoral candidates for the city, amid factional infighting.

In eThekwini metro, which contains the port city of Durban, the MK party was polling above 40%. Zuma, its 84-year-old leader, who has weathered and denied numerous corruption allegations, is set to take many of the votes of his former party, which won 42% in eThekwini in 2021 and is now polling around 10%.

The DA looks set to retain its majority in Cape Town, although probably with less than the 58% of the vote it won in 2021, as unaffordable housing turns more voters against the party.

South Africa’s electoral commission will have a week to announce the final results of Wednesday’s vote, which chooses councillors and parties, with mayors not directly elected. Parties will then have two weeks from the announcement to form councils and elect mayors.

#1. British healthcare worker with Ebola evacuated from DR Congo to UK

Patient is ⁠in ​stable condition and will be transferred to a specialist ‘high consequence’ infectious disease unit

A British healthcare worker with Ebola is being evacuated from the Democratic Republic of the Congo to the UK for medical treatment, it has been confirmed.

“A British citizen currently in the DR Congo has tested positive for Ebola,” a spokesperson for the UK government said in a statement on Wednesday. “The UK has well-established procedures for dealing with cases like this, and those processes have been activated … The risk to the UK public from Ebola remains low.”

The patient, whose identity is unknown, is in a stable condition and is expected to arrive in the UK on Wednesday or Thursday before being transferred in a specialist ambulance to a “high consequence” infectious disease unit, according to an official document seen by Reuters.

A humanitarian organisation sent the worker to DR Congo to help in the response to the outbreak, which is the second largest on record. It is the first case involving a British national contracting Ebola in the current outbreak.

The Royal Free hospital in London has a high-level isolation unit for infectious diseases such as Ebola; other centres are in Newcastle and Liverpool.

Ebola is a highly contagious, severe and often fatal illness. It is spread through direct contact with body fluids such as the blood or vomit of infected people, or dead bodies.

The current outbreak, declared on 15 May by DR Congo’s health ministry, has surpassed 10,000 cases, and 5,000 deaths. It is the 17th in DR Congo since Ebola was identified, was caused by the Bundibugyo virus, and has become the largest and deadliest in the country’s history. The Bundibugyo virus has no approved treatment, though vaccine trials are under way.

Since the outbreak started, it has spread to Kenya and Uganda in east Africa, while last month Nigeria confirmed a case in the capital, Abuja, after a woman who arrived from Uganda tested positive for the virus. She, along with a close contact, were evacuated to Switzerland for treatment.

In an interview with the Guardian last month, Dr Jean Kaseya, the director general of the Africa Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), said the “unprecedented” spread of the Ebola outbreak meant it could reach the UK and other countries.

“We want all countries to know that they are not safe if they don’t take appropriate measures,” he said. “Each country, not only in the African region, but all continents, must now increase the preparedness level and the possibility for treatment.

“It is the first time three African regions have been affected [by Ebola]. The geographic extension of this outbreak is unprecedented.”

Other patients who have been medically evacuated for specialised care since the start of the outbreak include two sent to Germany and one to the Netherlands. Another case was reported in France in July.

British health authorities say the risk ⁠to the wider public from imported Ebola cases remains low and the NHS has procedures in place to manage suspected and confirmed infections.

There have only been four confirmed cases recorded in Britain since 1976, ‌according to the UK Health Security Agency.
