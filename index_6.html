A short clip can help a project in different ways. It can show an idea you have not filmed, put an existing design in motion, or give an announcement something to look at. The same approach will not work if the clip needs to demonstrate a real feature or show exactly what happened. Before choosing a generator, decide what the viewer needs to learn from the shot.

This article uses Kling 4.0 as a worked example, but the decision process applies to any AI video generator. It covers how these tools turn prompts and images into video, how Kling 4.0 and a few alternatives differ, how to choose a starting mode, how to add exact text and branding outside the generated clip with a short ffmpeg script, and when real footage is the better choice. Feature details come from each vendor’s own pages at the time of writing and change often, so confirm current terms before you plan a production.

● Generated video is best for imagined shots. It suits concepts, backgrounds and early explorations, not instructions or faithful records.

● Your starting material decides the mode. A written idea, a reference image or a pair of opening and closing frames each call for a different input.

● Keep exact wording out of the generated clip. Titles, dates and logos belong on separate layers in an editor.

● Tools differ on control, cost and licensing. Compare inputs, length, resolution, credits and terms before you commit.

● Always review. Watch the whole clip at its final size and check it against the real product or design.

Most current systems extend diffusion models across time. A model learns to turn noise into a coherent set of frames guided by a prompt, and sometimes by an image or a clip. The research paper Video Diffusion Models describes this extension from images to video. The practical consequences are easy to observe.

● The prompt describes change over time. One visible change and one camera instruction usually work better than a long list of actions.

● Reference material steers but does not lock. A picture can suggest shape and colour without guaranteeing an exact match.

● Longer clips give more room for drift. Faces, logos and fine text are the first things to change between frames.

● Cost grows with length and resolution. Most hosted tools charge credits per second, so short previews are cheaper than full shots.

If you only have an idea, start with text. For a short announcement, you might ask for a band of light to move across a wall of coloured tiles. No one needs to recognise a particular wall, so the model can decide how it looks. Give it one visible change and a clear view of the subject. A prompt such as Soft morning light travels slowly across a wall of blue ceramic tiles. Fixed camera, no lettering is enough to see whether the idea works.

If you already have an illustration or a photo, reference mode gives you another starting point. Suppose you want a short concept clip for a desk accessory that only exists as a drawing. The picture can show the general shape and colour while the prompt describes the view you want. A video reference can help when it is the motion that is hard to explain, such as a slow camera slide. Say what each asset is meant to guide, and do not add a clip just because you have one.

Use keyframes when a particular view matters at a particular moment. Start with an opening image. If a short reveal also needs to end with the object facing forward, add an ending image to show that view. That is different from a reference image that mainly shows what the object looks like. Neither method guarantees an exact match, so check the result rather than assuming the uploaded picture has locked the design.

Reading the table: hosted studios such as the one on JXP are the quickest way to test an idea and need no setup, but you pay per second and work within their supported inputs. Kling’s own site is the place to check announcements and API options directly, since its page says little about durations or resolution. Runway positions its models for motion quality and prompt following, while Google’s Gemini API video documentation suits teams building generation into their own products. The Diffusers video guide covers open-source pipelines for developers who want local control and are ready to manage GPUs and licences.

Trying an idea before making it. An illustrator might want to see how an imagined landscape feels when the light changes. A quick clip gives the team something to discuss: is the change visible, and does it happen at the right pace? It does not need to be the final animation to answer those questions.

Showing a product concept. A rotating view or a slow camera move can help people discuss how a design might be presented. It is useful early, when the shape and lighting are still being explored. Once the clip represents a product someone can buy, the standard changes. If a port moves or a button appears that the real product does not have, it is the wrong clip for that job.

Making a short visual for a post. A few seconds of moving colour can give an event announcement or a project update a distinctive opening. Put the name, date and other exact wording in your editor so they are readable and easy to change. The background only needs to support the message. If it competes with the text, use less movement or keep the background still.

Generated video is unreliable at lettering, so add titles in an editor or with a script. The command below overlays a title on a generated background and then checks the file properties. It assumes you already have a clip named background.mp4 and ffmpeg installed.

-vf "drawtext=text='Launch Night | 12 March':fontcolor=white:fontsize=64:x=(w-text_w)/2:y=(h-text_h)/2:box=1:boxcolor=black@0.35:boxborderw=24" \

-c:v libx264 -pix_fmt yuv420p -an announcement.mp4

-show_entries stream=width,height,r_frame_rate,duration \

-of default=noprint_wrappers=1 announcement.mp4

Because the text lives in the command, you can reuse the same background for three events by changing one string. The font may need to be set with a fontfile option on some systems. This is the practical version of keeping the moving background in one file and the editable details in another.

If people will follow the clip as instructions, use real footage or a screen recording for the steps. A convincing shot of an app is not proof that a button is in the right place. A generated close-up of a product is not a reliable way to show how its parts fit. You can still use an imagined opening, but make the switch to the real demonstration clear.

Think about revisions, too. If your team expects to adjust the same title, logo or date every week, put those elements on separate layers in an editor. And if the only movement you need is a slow zoom on an approved picture, apply that zoom directly. There is no reason to risk changing the picture when a simple edit will do.

This matters when someone else will maintain the video. A reusable announcement template is easier to hand over if the moving background is one file and the event details are editable. If the team likes the movement but needs three versions with different dates, they can reuse the same clip. If every change calls for a new generation, the time you saved on the first version may disappear on the second.

The Kling 4.0 video generator on JXP currently offers 720p and 1080p settings and clip lengths from 3 to 30 seconds. According to its page, reference mode accepts up to 10 images and 5 videos, keyframe mode takes a first frame, middle frames and an optional last frame, and credits are charged per second with a higher rate at 1080p. For a first attempt, choose the frame shape you plan to use and make the clip only as long as it needs to be. Check the displayed credit cost before generating, because a short preview can tell you whether the direction works before you spend on a longer shot.

Decide what a good result looks like before you generate it. For the tile background, you need to see the light move without distracting from the words you will add. For a product concept, check whether the shape stays recognisable as the viewpoint changes. Watch the whole clip at the size the audience will see it. If the shot misses the point, revise one thing at a time: the motion, the framing or the source image.

#10331. Agent Distraction

What is easy for humans to skim past in code, agents see as a call to action. They do not know better, latching on to that which is littered across the files read during the planning and research phase of the coding journey.

As agents' agency through tools and skills grows the ability for side-quests scales proportionally. Obsolete specs, long resolved bugs, and lagging documentation no longer rest in long forgotten corners of the codebase, but rather are unintentionally ingested as context indirecting and misdirecting the next wave of coders - the agents. This indirection is not free: in the best case it only costs tokens, steps, and time - in the worst case it distracts and grounds the agent on falsehoods leading to incorrect logic and decisions during implementation.

Measuring how often agents fall into these traps across millions of monthly sessions is impossible with manual human review - we need an objective signal derivable from the code itself. Inline TODO(bug/...) comments provide the perfect proxy. TODOs tend to be left behind long after the underlying bug is resolved or abandoned, while the linked bug ticket acts as the canonical record. By cross-referencing every TODO an agent encounters against the TODO's bug status and the user's prompt, we can automatically separate valid work from wasted detours at scale.

In recap, as we task agents on more and more ambitious problems, "agent friendly codebases" require more than just the code in the hotpath being healthy. The forgotten corners of the codebase now more than ever carry a significant tax levied against agents.

Tooling like presubmit analyzers, entire corpus scanners, and automated coding agents to remediate dead-todos offer out of the box ready to scale solutions to address the tax enabling a healthier and distraction free codebase for agents to thrive.

#10328. Ten TV Series That Highlight the Best in Humanity: 2026

Become a subscribing member today. Help us continue to bring “the science of a meaningful life” to you and to millions around the globe.

Greater Good Science Center • Magazine • In Action • In Education

Every year, we invite staff and regular contributors to nominate TV series for Greater Goodies, the awards we give to stories that exemplify or illustrate kindness, community, empathy, and more keys to well-being. Here’s what we came up with this year: The Boroughs, Death by Lightning, Envidiosa, The Hawk, Heated Rivalry, A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms, Lucky, Murderbot, Pluribus, and Shrinking.

Name the last show you’ve seen that features a hero over the age of 60. Drawing a blank? It’s not your fault—the media’s modus operandi is to equate heroism with youthfulness. Netflix’s The Boroughs flips this stereotype on its head.

With a star-studded lead cast, all 70 or older, we follow Sam Cooper’s (Alfred Molina) experience as he reluctantly moves into a senior living community in New Mexico after the death of his wife. But a series of strange events suggests that this is no ordinary retirement community.

As Sam navigates grief, new friendships, and a new identity, he joins forces with his neighbors to solve and resolve the community mystery. Overcoming obstacles like age discrimination and self-doubts, the group peels back the layers of the strange occurrences. At the root of the mystery is a societal fear of aging.

The show highlights what researcher Margie Lachman calls “crystallized intelligence”: the culmination of life experience, memory, and know-how. In The Boroughs, that turns out to be just as heroic as super human strength or the ability to fly. — Shanna B. Tiayon

History has already told us how Death by Lightning ends: Charles Guiteau will assassinate President James Garfield in 1881. But why? That’s one of the questions explored by the series, and it finds motivation in a sense of purpose–how purpose forms and how people come to cross-purposes.

Garfield (Michael Shannon) is principled and drawn reluctantly into a presidential contest he entered to nominate someone else. Guiteau (Matthew Macfadyen), meanwhile, is disturbingly sympathetic: charming, articulate, and desperate to do something meaningful with his life. Despite failed ventures, including a stint at the Oneida free-love commune, he remains convinced greatness awaits him. Believing his campaigning secured Garfield’s victory, he expects a government position to confirm his worth.

As their stories converge, Garfield finds purpose in his presidency: fighting corruption while supporting education and equal opportunity. Guiteau pursues a reward he feels he deserves, an entitlement that leads to tragedy.

In these ways, Garfield and Guiteau offer sharply contrasting answers to what makes a life meaningful, and watching their stories unfold side by side, we see how differently the desire to matter can play out.

Who you really ought to be watching closely is Garfield’s Vice President, Chester A. Arthur (Nick Offerman). A comfortable beneficiary of the political machine, he becomes an unlikely source of hope as the story goes on. Offerman makes him funny, flawed, and increasingly affecting as the demands of office begin to collide with old loyalties.

In a political landscape that can leave us cynical–in both the 19th century and the 21st–the series makes room for something more hopeful: the possibility that even an unlikely person can rise to the occasion, if they know why they need to rise. – Aurelia Santos

When almost-40-year-old Vicky (Griselda Siciliani) breaks up with her long-term boyfriend, she is driven to embark upon a journey of self-discovery.

At first, Vicky is just looking for a replacement for her boyfriend—but the problem is that her compulsive comparisons with other women sabotage attempts to connect with other people. (Indeed, the name of this Argentinian show, Envidiosa, means “envious.”) Vicky is self-absorbed and yet at the same time she doesn’t have a strong sense of her own self.

We see this vividly in her hilarious sessions with her therapist. When, for example, she goes on an envious rant about a friend’s milestone, she is forced to confront the quiet, uncomfortable realization that she never once asked how her friend actually felt. In short, Vicky is not a polished role model—and over the course of four seasons, we see her empathy grow through the painful process of unlearning self-centeredness.

Personally, I see Envidiosa as an opportunity to question how we show up for others’ joys and struggles, and I take it as an invitation to reflect: How can I empathize with the ups and downs of those around me? Envidiosa is a funny and at times embarrassingly relatable story about trying to become a more empathic human being and friend. — Criss Cuervo

In The Hawk, washed-up professional golfer Lonnie “The Hawk” Hawkins (Will Ferrell) has made a miracle comeback into the PGA tour—this time around, facing a tough competitor in his son, Lance (Jimmy Tatro).

It’s a screwball comedy, full of dirty jokes and a rapid-fire rapport between Lonnie and his not-quite-ex-wife, Stacy (Molly Shannon). A makeshift-caddy he “picked up at Walmart” (while chatting up the parking lot, from his camper van) adds to the fun, serving up an unlikely duo that seem happy just to be alive.

Even when he’s pretty down and out, Lonnie is looking on the bright side of things. Everything is done with gusto—he chats up the golf ball before each swing, he devours plates of Buffalo Wild Wings, he plays mini golf with his son and makes it a full-on competition.

“Son, never play golf angry,” he tells Lance. For Lonnie, life is all about golf—and golf is all about fun. Why spend all your time on something if you aren’t doing it for the joy of it?

I especially love Lonnie’s adoration for his estranged wife, a firecracker in her own right. In episode four, when the Hawk crashes his son’s engagement party–yes, you read that right–and then attempts a getaway with his caddie in the van, Stacy hurls the cake at them, along with a string of expletives.

It doesn’t even faze him. “You gotta love the passion!” Lonnie gushes. “That is one hot tamale!” — Hope Reese

Heated Rivalry follows stick-crossed lovers Shane and Ilya as they figure out how to be intimate within the highly competitive, homophobic environment of professional hockey.

At first, Shane and Ilya swap fluids more readily than emotions. But over the course of six episodes, the two learn to love across gaps in culture, language, and experience. In one pivotal scene, Shane invites Ilya to speak vulnerably about family dynamics in Ilya’s native Russian—despite not understanding a word of it.

That works because Shane does not focus attention on himself. Instead, he stays present on the phone, serving as a witness to Ilya’s visceral emotions. Their connection lies in felt care, in hearing the texture of another’s heartbreak.

In similar fashion, Ilya grounds Shane in stressful encounters. He supports Shane through panic without wallowing in his lover’s fear and entices Shane to relish pleasures outside of straight standards. As a couple, they help each other explore complex parts of themselves. We see a similar process unfold in another couple in the show, as they learn to respect each other’s different needs.

People might tune into Heated Rivalry for locker room towel drops and HBO’s glossy cinematography. They should stay to see a couple learn to love each other’s differences. The show exemplifies how bridging differences, at its best, means supporting others to more fully encounter themselves, and how in the process our relationships can grow.

With plenty of additional homoerotic stick-and-ball games to pull on from the novels of Rachel Reid, the next season of Heated Rivalry is expected to air on HBO Max and Canada’s Crave in Spring 2027. — Luke Brown

The monstrously successful HBO series Game of Thrones famously tossed out a lot of good-and-evil fantasy tropes to tell a morally complicated story of how power corrupts even the best people. A prequel called House of the Dragon shares that worldview.

That’s why a new prequel called A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms came as something of a surprise to fans. Unlike the other two series, this is not a story about a struggle for power. Instead, it focuses on a rather naive, not-too-bright “hedge knight” named Duncan (Peter Claffey) seeking to be hired by whatever lord will take him. Though Duncan is taller and stronger than most, he’s touchingly childlike as he tries to find his place in the merry old kingdoms of Westeros.

The first two episodes are funny and charming, as Duncan acquires a squire in the form of a little bald boy going by the name Egg (Dexter Sol Ansell), and fumblingly tries to enter a jousting tournament. And then Duncan finds himself on a collision course with the great and powerful of Westeros. When violence finally comes, there’s nothing casual about it. We feel the force of every blow, the tragedy of every death, and the erosion of Duncan’s innocence.

I’m giving A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms the award for integrity, but I can’t tell you why without spoiling the ending. Let’s just say that the series turns out to be the story of how one simple young man discovers what is most important to him—and finds the courage to stick to that path even in the face of isolation, temptations, and threats. — Jeremy Adam Smith

How do you exit a life you were born into?

That’s the question behind Lucky, starring Anya Taylor-Joy. Lucky was raised by her con-artist father and taught the “family business” from a young age—but when we meet her, she navigating internal conflict with the life she’s been told to live and the life she wants for herself.

Her desire to quit is complicated by her marriage to Cary, the son of a ruthless mob leader, who was also socialized into a life of crime. The couple plan one final con as their exit strategy, but it goes terribly wrong. Lucky sets out to resolve the botched plan, reconcile her marriage, and protect both her life and her father’s.

Her intelligence is put to the test as she puts the pieces together, connects the dots, and outsmarts her rivals. In the process she discovers her own virtue—and that the intelligence which made her a successful con artist can also set her free.

Through the course of the series, she realizes that she does not have to pay for the sins of her father. Having never been given the opportunity for self-determination, she makes peace in the best way she knows how and chooses a new path. — Shanna B. Tiayon

The Murderbot Diaries are a series of science-fiction novellas by Martha Wells, about a genderless, robotic soldier who manages to achieve free will. Since discovery will result in deactivation and since it doesn’t like humans very much anyway, Murderbot conceals its freedom of mind and consumes a lot of human-made entertainment inside its own head, even as it externally follows every order given to it by the evil corporation that owns it. Murderbot even has a favorite TV show: the Star-Trek-like Rise and Fall of Sanctuary Moon.

As a result of this isolation, the novellas are built almost entirely around its memorable inner monologues, with a voice that is by turns snarky, vulnerable, cold-blooded, disgusted, and puzzled. One of the things that makes these books distinctive is that Murderbot is not Pinocchio—it has no wish to become a “real boy.” It doesn’t want a gender, or the same feelings and drives as people. It just wants to be itself—and to have that difference respected.

While the Apple TV show based on the books is not quite that original or interesting, Murderbot retains its focus on finding the balance between belonging and difference. When we meet Murderbot (Alexander Skarsgård), he’s been hired to protect a group of polyamorous hippie scientists who hail from a utopian colony that exists independently of the corporations that rule the rest of the galaxy.

They are Murderbot’s first encounter with humans not shaped by dehumanizing hyper-capitalistic values, and they observe Murderbot in a way the corporate humans cannot. Over the course of the first season, they begin to realize that there is more to Murderbot than meets the eye, and they learn to see and appreciate Murderbot on its own terms. But what’s crucial is not that they perceive the humanity in Murderbot—it’s that Murderbot sees the humanity (and the goodness) in them.

In time, it becomes an accepted part of their society—without becoming more like them. Even so, the first season concludes on a tough-minded, melancholy note, as Murderbot itself can’t quite feel like it belongs. Will it someday find a place among these crunchy idealists? Stay tuned: The second season is coming in 2027. — Jeremy Adam Smith

Have you ever been among people but you didn’t feel part of the crowd? Like they were in on a joke that you just couldn’t get?

Welcome to Carol Sturka’s world. The science-fiction premise of Pluribus is that an alien virus transforms humanity into one big happy hive mind—everyone except Carol and 13 other people who seem to have immunity.

Even among those remaining individuals, though, Carol stands out as a malcontent. We sense that she’s been a fish out of water for her whole life.

Indeed, not every viewer will find Carol relatable or likable. When we first meet her, she’s at a bookstore event promoting her highly successful romantasy series. With her fans, she’s charming, funny, and kind. When she gets into a limo after a big sendoff by readers, the drivers asks if she’s famous.

“That depends,” Carol replies. “Are you a big fan of mindless crap?”

In short, Carol hates herself, her work, and her readers. And as we discover, her personality is snarky, uptight, stubborn, and uncompromising. When the virus hits, it’s an apocalyptic event, but Carol doesn’t change one bit—and her traits become both a blessing and a curse. The whole world wants to be kind to her, but Carol can’t accept kindness. That drives her to fight back. And that’s what ultimately makes her the hero of the story.

In the end, Pluribus is a very Greater Good thought experiment. It takes prosociality to its limits and finds that a total togetherness, empathy, and cooperation to the exclusion of selfishness, crankiness, and boundaries is a self-destructive dystopia, not a utopia. The show seems to argue that humanity needs difficult people like Carol to survive, and so invites us to appreciate what the Carols of the world have to offer. — Jeremy Adam Smith

In Shrinking, talking and listening are the name of the game. Jimmy (Jason Segel) is a widowed psychotherapist who helps patients navigate anxiety, OCD, PTSD, and other ailments as he himself is floundering.

But he’s not alone. His teenage daughter, overbearing-but-lovable neighbors, and coworker-friends (with Harrison Ford as the curmudgeonly head of the practice) are all there to help him when his life goes off the rails after his wife was killed by a drunk driver.

In its three-season run, the series is heavy-handed with its use of therapy-speak among the whole gang, whether between therapist-to-patient, therapist-to-daughter, or therapist-to-therapist. Characters “feel seen.” More times than I can count, they tell each other “I hear you.” We witness, in detail, exchanges inside the therapy office—and outside of it, in a rogue practice known as “Jimmying,” where therapy takes place at such venues as, for instance, the boxing ring.

In other words, there’s a lot of “deep listening”: the practice of being present, open, and empathic with another person, in a way that goes beyond just hearing and understanding words. #10321. How to Stay Informed But Not Overwhelmed by Election News

You pick up your phone to check one thing—perhaps the weather, a text message, or the score of a game. A few minutes later, you have read three election headlines, watched a clip from a debate, skimmed dozens of comments, and somehow learned that democracy, the economy, your health care, and the future of the planet may all be in danger before breakfast. If this sounds familiar, you are not alone. Election season can make the news feel unusually personal. The issues on the ballot can affect our finances, health, schools, communities, and rights. At the same time, the way we now encounter political information—constantly, emotionally, and often in fragments—can leave us feeling overloaded rather than informed. There is even a name for this experience: election stress. The Mayo Clinic describes it as the overwhelming anxiety some people feel during an election cycle. And surveys from the American Psychological Association suggest that elections have become a significant source of stress for many Americans. In the lead-up to the 2024 presidential election, for example, 72% of adults said they were worried that the results could lead to violence, and 56% said they feared the election could mark the end of democracy in the United States. It is understandable to feel unsettled when an election seems to carry serious consequences. But anxiety is not always a problem to get rid of; in small doses, it can direct our attention toward what matters. It may motivate us to learn, vote, volunteer, have difficult conversations, or support people affected by political decisions. The problem begins when information meant to help us engage instead keeps our nervous systems in a constant state of alarm. Why elections can feel so stressful Part of election stress comes from the uncertainty it elicits. We do not know who will win, what policies will change, or how the people around us will respond. Research suggests that uncertainty itself can be stressful because people naturally want to understand and predict the environment around them. When we feel unable to do that, we may feel less safe and less in control. News can intensify that uncertainty. Negative news is often more attention-grabbing than positive news, and conflict, danger, and outrage make especially compelling headlines. That does not mean journalists are intentionally trying to harm us. Reporting on threats and problems is an essential part of journalism. But a steady diet of alarming information can shape how we feel about the world, especially when it leaves little room for context, solutions, or signs of human cooperation. This can create a difficult cycle. When people feel anxious, they often seek out more information in an effort to regain a sense of control. Yet the more election news they encounter—particularly news marked by uncertainty, conflict, and emotional intensity—the more anxious they may feel. Then they check again. In other words, we may turn to the news to calm ourselves down, only to leave feeling more activated than before. Why today’s media environment makes it harder Political stress is not new. But the way we receive political information has changed dramatically. When John F. Kennedy and Richard Nixon debated on television in 1960, viewers watched in a far more contained media environment. There was no endless stream of clips, reactions, memes, push notifications, and commentary arriving at every hour of the day. People could discuss the debate afterward, but they were not also carrying the debate around in their pockets. Today, election news can follow us almost everywhere: to the grocery store line, the waiting room, the car, the dinner table, and bed. Social media makes it easier for people to participate politically and amplify voices that were once excluded from public conversation. Those are meaningful benefits. But the same technology can make political information feel impossible to put down. It also encourages us to consume news in small, emotionally potent pieces. A headline while brushing our teeth. A video clip while waiting for coffee. A dramatic post at a stoplight. These snippets can provoke strong feelings without giving us enough context to understand the story fully. That matters because headlines often tell us what to feel before they tell us what happened. Pew research found that five out of eight Americans (63%) said they only read news headlines rather than full news articles, specifically when scrolling through social media. When people repeatedly take in emotionally charged fragments of news, they may feel a growing sense of threat while having less detailed information to guide their judgments. When stress turns into news avoidance Faced with this constant emotional demand, some people keep checking the news compulsively. Others decide they have had enough and stop paying attention altogether. News avoidance can offer temporary relief. If every update makes you tense, angry, or helpless, closing the app may feel like self-protection. In fact, a report from the American Press Institute found that a third of Americans want to disengage from news because it feels stressful and overwhelming. But avoiding news altogether comes with costs, especially during an election year. It can make it harder to participate in democratic life, understand issues that affect our communities, or distinguish reliable reporting from misinformation passed along by friends, influencers, or highly partisan accounts. The goal, then, is not to consume more news or no news. It is to develop a healthier relationship with news: one that helps us stay informed without asking our nervous systems to be on call all day. Three ways to stay informed—and calm 1. Choose a news source that includes solutions, not only problems. During stressful times, it can help to seek out reporting that does more than identify what is going wrong. Constructive journalism still covers serious problems, but it also provides context, examines solutions, and asks what people and communities can do next. This approach is not about pretending everything is fine. It is about giving readers a more complete picture: What is the problem? Who is affected? What is being tried? What can citizens realistically do? Solution-oriented reporting may be especially helpful during elections because it can replace helplessness with a more grounded sense of agency and hope for the future. When you choose a news source, consider asking: Does this reporting leave me better informed? Does it help me understand the issue, or does it only make me feel alarmed? Does it explain possible solutions as well as the threat? 2. Balance distressing news with inspiring media. We do not need to force ourselves to be positive about difficult realities. But we can balance the emotional weight of election coverage with media that remind us of human goodness, beauty, courage, and connection. Research suggests that stories of kindness, moral courage, and collective care can evoke feelings such as elevation, gratitude, and hope. These emotions can broaden our view of what is possible and help us feel more connected to other people. And new research from the University of Santa Barbara suggests that these emotions can even reduce stress. This might mean watching a short video about neighbors helping one another after a disaster, reading a profile of local organizers working across differences, listening to music that helps you settle, or returning to a film or show that leaves you feeling more hopeful about people, or nostalgic about the past. Think of this as building a more balanced media diet. Election news can be an important part of that diet, but it should not be the only thing we feed our minds. Just as eating one healthy meal a week does not transform our physical health, one inspiring story will not undo chronic stress. Over time, though, regularly choosing media that restore us, physically and mentally, can help cultivate resilience, hope, and perspective. 3. Create boundaries around when you check the news. Finally, consider treating news less like a snack and more like a meal. Snacking on news all day can mean consuming a lot without ever feeling nourished. You absorb a headline here, an alert there, and a few comments in between—but never get enough context to make sense of what you are seeing. The result can be a lot of emotional calories and very little understanding. Instead, choose one or two intentional times to engage with the news. You might read a full article over breakfast, listen to a trusted news program on your commute, or set aside 15 minutes at lunch to review the day’s major stories. If you are prone to checking headlines compulsively, digital wellness strategies such as app limits or notification settings can make those boundaries easier to keep. It may also help to avoid making late-night news “snacking” part of your bedtime routine. By the end of the day, our emotional and cognitive resources are often already depleted. A stream of alarming headlines can quickly turn into doomscrolling: the repeated consumption of negative online content that leaves us more agitated and less able to sleep. Giving news its own place in the day does not mean becoming indifferent. It means making room to take in information carefully, in context, and with enough emotional space to respond thoughtfully. Staying engaged without staying alarmed Election stress is real because elections matter. Feeling concerned about the future does not mean that something is wrong with you; it may mean you are paying attention. But paying attention does not require constant exposure to distress. We can choose reporting that informs and empowers us, balance difficult coverage with media that reminds us of human possibility, and set boundaries that give our minds a chance to rest. The aim is not emotional numbness. It is becoming the kind of informed citizen who can face difficult news, stay connected to what matters, and still have enough calm and hope left to act.

#10296. Let’s Use the Emergent CSS random() Function in all the Browsers

The creator of the TV show The Good Place wrote a tie-in book about moral philosophy which includes a chapter called “The Luck of the Draw,” discussing how the myth of meritocracy leads people to “underestimate the role that luck has played in their lives.” Given how God seems to play dice with the universe, there is something compelling in the way art imitates life when websites embrace controlled chaos in their designs. The jury is out on whether extreme versions of this nondeterminism such as generative UI are a helpful usage of unpredictable UX. Indeed, when I see the YouTube comments reacting to Google’s upcoming usage of GenUI in search, maybe it’s taking the idea too far down a bad path. But there is still something about the idea of a webpage that exists in a state of subtle flux each time you land on it, the same way you can’t step into the same river twice.

I’m a consultant who often works on short-term, greenfield projects, which provide me with a window into the zeitgeist and the trends companies think are the future. It’s no coincidence that the idea of randomness permeated one of my recent projects. That’s epitomized by a burst of confetti to give the user a sense of excitement when they run a random draw they configured. And like many a UI feature in the corporate world, the simple idea of confetti was subject to several revisions to make every randomized particle align with the client’s brand.

In fact, the requirements became custom enough that we ended up ditching the JavaScript plugin we were using and rolled our own confetti implementation! This illustrates the tension between the conflicting needs for chaos and control in UX, even in a fun feature like random confetti.

Wouldn’t it be nice if we could wield controlled presentational randomness in the presentation layer without leaving CSS?

If unpredictable user experiences are having a moment, it follows that CSS will do its part to make randomized layouts easy to implement. The creators of CSS have always been on a mission to harvest common UI patterns into declarative CSS standards. In keeping with that spirit, we see that in late 2025, Safari became the first browser to support the CSS random() spec, as part of an update that emphasized “letting you solve common use cases with HTML and CSS alone, paving the cowpaths, and reducing the need for JavaScript or third-party frameworks.”

Since then, cool demos and discussions of random() keep popping up. For instance, Schalk Neethling showed us how CSS random() can give us fine-grained control over the infamous confetti effect, and Alvaro Montoro made a strong argument that CSS turns out to be the most suitable language for such tasks. He points out this approach is in line with the Rule of Least Power, which encourages “solving a problem using the least powerful language capable of expressing and solving it.”

Now the bad news: half a year after Safari introduced CSS random(), there isn’t clarity on when it will land in the other browsers. At time of writing, there are signs of life that both Chrome and Firefox have been working on it, but no guarantees about when we will be able to use it outside of the Apple world, even behind a browser flag.

So, it seems currently I can only try the online demos of CSS random() on my work MacBook and not on my PC where I do my personal projects. I am tempted to write my own implementation, but the syntax is surprisingly intricate, mostly because of elaborate random caching and keying semantics, combined with the options for base values and intervals. Even if I could manage to get all those details correct, CSS random() is part of an editor’s draft spec that’s in the “early exploration phase” and “major breaking changes are expected.”

On top of that, from my dive into CSS polyfills in my article on ::nth-letter, we know the whole idea of a CSS polyfill can be a minefield.

With all these obstacles in mind, a person would have to be a special breed of crazy to attempt to polyfill CSS random().

One of the commenters on a neat YouTube demo of the feature marvelled that it’s a “feature that works ONLY IN SAFARI?!? Did the Earth get flipped upside down?” Indeed, I am more accustomed to getting my first opportunity to experience emergent features in Chrome, which means my friends on iPhones often can’t run my experiments.

And yet, in the case of random(), it’s darkly poetic that a feature based on chance appears in an unexpected place where many of us can’t use it. In fact, even Safari users may benefit from my css-random-polyfill package, because Safari updates are tied to the OS, meaning not everyone can upgrade to the latest version of the browser. Besides, we know how much Apple loves it when you hack their stuff to improve compatibility.

Jokes aside, Apple seems serious about the “hackability” and transparency of everything about the open source WebKit engine that powers Safari, and most of the demos I’ve used to test my polyfill are forks of demos from the WebKit blog, in which the Apple Safari team showed off the possibilities for CSS random() back when it was in Safari preview.

Here’s my cross-browser version of the first demo from the Safari team’s article. It’s a randomly scattered field of stars fading in and out at random intervals. The larger, four-pointed stars all tilt at the same randomly selected angle. All stars have subtle, randomly hued shadows around them.

To migrate the Safari-only original to a version that works in Chrome and Firefox, we need to change the HTML to reference my polyfill script and add the randomized marker class to all elements that we want to polyfill.

As for the CSS, unlike my :nth-letter polyfill which uses a nonstandard selector that has to be translated into valid CSS at runtime — and introduces drawbacks in the process — this time we need to support a new function in CSS instead of a new selector. It turns out the CSS we can use in this situation is technically valid, even in browsers that have never heard of CSS random(). More later on why it is valid, but for now, just notice that anywhere we want a random value, we store it in an intermediate custom property, and we always have to follow the convention that the property name starts with the prefix --random.

This starfield demo showcases a few different variations of the supported random() syntax, such as the optional third argument for specifying a step interval which, in this case, is used to randomly select only whole number values within the range:

…and the element-shared base value, which we use here to tilt every four-pointed star by the same randomly selected angle.

Note: In the original starfield demo, most of the random values were used inline, which is admittedly more elegant. The spec that includes random() makes it clear that this kind of function “can be used in place of any part of any property’s value,” just like calc() or min(). So, by requiring extra ceremony and conventions, the polyfill is supporting a subset of what we will get with native random(). To see the glass half-full, it means the CSS stays compatible with the native implementation: we could delete the script reference to the polyfill once native support goes baseline and our code will still work, like it does today when it detects native support in Safari. in this case the polyfill does not process random() calls at all and it lets Safari do all the work. This is a compromise I can live with, especially if the alternative is to press our noses against the glass of Safari-only demos on YouTube and make comments such as one viewer did: “Can’t wait to use this in prod in 4 years.”

Chris Coyier said of the original starfield demo from Apple that he found it “pretty darn compelling!” I agree, and when I was testing my polyfill, that demo was fun to watch randomly twinkling, refresh and see the stars scatter differently using an emergent, declarative CSS standard. By contrast, I can’t say I have ever sat around wishing I could create a 100×100 CSS grid with randomly multicolored cells, so this example from the Safari team feels a bit like a contrived excuse to randomize something. #10291. What is Pathways? Single-controller training for large models

Sorry, your browser doesn't support playback for this video

Today, we announce the General Availability of Pathways on Google Cloud Platform. In this post, you will learn about Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own large models, including Gemini. We discuss how Pathways addresses the coordination challenge by introducing one client that describes the whole computation as a graph, and how the distributed runtime places it, moves the tensors, and keeps the accelerators busy.

Most large-model training runs as Single Program, Multiple Data (SPMD) programs. Every accelerator runs the same program in lockstep and coordinates through collectives like AllReduce.

It works well when your job is the same program running on every device. It struggles when the job is no longer uniform, like a pipeline whose stages wait on each other or an RL loop where a sampler, a reward model, and a trainer are different programs that have to overlap.

Large runs also have to survive hardware failures, which interrupted Gemini 2.5 training multiple times per hour. In SPMD, one lost machine stops every process and sends the run back to its last checkpoint. In each case, the challenge is coordination. You have to decide what runs where and in what order, how tensors move between devices, and how the job keeps going when a machine drops out.

In this post of the series “Pathways on Google Cloud”, you will learn about Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own large models, including Gemini. The post discusses how Pathways addresses the coordination challenge by introducing one client that describes the whole computation as a graph, and how the distributed runtime places it, moves the tensors, and keeps the accelerators busy.

By the end, you'll know where SPMD is suboptimal, what single-controller execution changes, and how Pathways coordinates a whole training job across a cluster.

If you train PyTorch models, you almost certainly use Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) or Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP). DDP runs a copy of your script on every accelerator and averages gradients with a collective operation such as AllReduce. FSDP shards parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across workers so that the model fits at all.

Sorry, your browser doesn't support playback for this video

DDP and FSDP both follow the Single Program, Multiple Data (SPMD) execution model. Every process runs the same copy of your script, owns its accelerators for the whole run, and moves in lockstep with the others, because the only way they coordinate is through collectives like AllReduce. Inside one tightly connected group of accelerators those collectives run over a fast interconnect and are cheap. Scale the job across groups and the same collectives cross the datacenter network, which is far slower. On a TPU v5p, for example, each chip gets 1,200 GB/s of bidirectional chip-to-chip bandwidth inside its group, but only 50 Gb/s (gigabits, about 6 GB/s) to the datacenter network which is roughly two orders of magnitude less.

And because every step blocks on its collectives, that communication lands directly in step time. The more groups you span, the larger the share of each step spent moving data, so throughput grows slower than the number of accelerators you add.

As long as your training job is one program that fills the hardware you give it, this execution model fits well. You write your model as if there were one device, and the collectives do the rest. But fitting a model into memory is only part of the problem. Keeping every accelerator busy is the harder part, and this is where the SPMD model starts to show its limits.

Take pipeline parallelism as an example. You split a model into blocks across devices, and each device now runs different code over smaller micro-batches. Activations flow forward, activation gradients flow back, and while device 0 works on the first micro-batch, devices 1 and 2 wait. Apart from the idle accelerators you keep paying for, this drops utilization and throughput, even though every device has work in the steady state.

Another example is RL post-training for an LLM. You have a sampler that generates rollouts, a reward model that scores them, and a trainer that updates the policy and sends the new weights back to the sampler. These are three different programs that should overlap; otherwise the sampler is idle while the trainer runs, and the trainer is idle while the sampler generates.

In both scenarios, the job is now several different programs running on different devices, and their dependencies can no longer be managed through a single collective. You now have to coordinate which devices share which tensors, which processes run while others are still finishing, and how early to enqueue the next buffer so an accelerator never waits for input. Because each worker only has visibility into its local devices and data, no individual process can manage all of these dependencies itself. That has several consequences.

First, because each process owns its accelerators for the whole run and every collective blocks until all devices finish, one failed machine prevents the collective from completing and stops the entire job, forcing a restart from the last checkpoint. You cannot add devices mid-run, and you cannot give devices back.

Second, because each process holds its accelerators from start to finish, capacity can't move to where it's needed. Within one job, a stage that needs fewer chips than another still keeps the extra ones. They sit idle, you keep paying for them, and no other part of the job can use them until the run ends. Across a team, the same happens at cluster scale. Every job owns its accelerators for as long as it runs, so sharing a cluster means splitting it into per-job allocations, and each new job brings up its own processes on its own hosts.

Finally, the development process itself gets harder. On one host you iterate fast because you run the script, drop into a Python shell, set a breakpoint, and debug. This is not true once the job spans many hosts. You need a launcher to push your script to every host, you hit coordination bugs that never showed up on one machine, and there's no single process to break into. When something hangs, each worker is stuck at a collective waiting for the others, with no one place that shows the whole job.

So the common challenge here is the coordination itself. As soon as the job stops being one program in lockstep, you have to decide what runs where, in what order, and how tensors get from one device to the next.

One way to describe this is as a graph, where each block is a node, each tensor that moves between blocks is an edge, and the devices are where the nodes are placed. You can wire that graph up by hand in SPMD, one process at a time. But what if a single client declared that graph, including what each block does, what it needs, and where it runs, and a distributed runtime handled the transfers, dependencies, and ordering along its edges?

That is the idea behind Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own largest models, including Gemini. Pathways runs JAX programs on TPUs and it is available on Google Cloud.

With Pathways, you define the end-to-end computation from a single Python client, without writing custom coordination code, and a distributed runtime executes it. That client is decoupled from the hardware. It runs on an ordinary CPU machine and you write your program against virtual devices; a resource manager maps them onto real chips, and a worker on each accelerator host runs the compiled programs it receives.

Because your code refers to virtual devices rather than physical ones, the runtime is flexible to allow users to change the devices used for a given computation. This allows you to stay in control while the runtime handles the mapping to hardware. In an RL loop, for example, the trainer and the samplers rarely want the same number of chips, so you can give the sampler stage more chips by asking for more virtual devices.

Also, the same capability helps when a host fails or the hardware isn't ready yet. If a host dies, the resource manager remaps the work onto healthy chips and the run keeps going instead of relaunching the whole job, which is the basis for elastic training. And because the program targets virtual devices, compilation can start before the physical chips are provisioned, so setup overlaps with provisioning instead of waiting on it.

The resource manager owning the chips also means one Pathways cluster can serve several clients, so researchers on a team can share its capacity. With the Shared Pathways Service, the cluster stays up as a long running service, which lets you develop interactively on TPUs hosted on GKE. Each client connects on demand from a laptop or VM, gets its own slices (groups of tightly connected TPU chips) from the shared pool, and hands them back when it disconnects. When every chip is busy, the next workload waits until capacity frees up.

Take the three-block model from Figure 2. Block A runs on one group of devices, block B on a second, block C on a third. You declare what each block computes, its target devices, and where its outputs go. Pathways expresses those dependencies as a distributed dataflow graph, in which each block is a compiled SPMD program and each edge is a tensor the runtime has to deliver.

In a single SPMD program, the framework handles the collectives that keep the devices in sync within a group, with or without Pathways, and your code doesn't change. The difference shows up once the job runs several programs on different groups (Multiple Programs, Multiple Data, MPMD), like these three blocks or the sampler, reward model, and trainer of an RL loop. Then no single process sees both sides (the same limit from before, where each worker only knows its own devices), so getting a tensor from block A to block B is a hand-off you have to coordinate yourself.

Pathways closes that gap. You declare the dependency once, and the runtime moves and reshards the tensor for you, over the fast interconnect within a group and over the datacenter network between groups.

To keep the accelerators busy, Pathways prepares launches ahead of time with futures and parallel asynchronous dispatch. While block A is still computing, block B can't run yet, but its input shapes and dtypes are already known, so the runtime allocates B's buffers and schedules the transfer before A's output even exists. Host-side setup for every block happens up front, and each block launches when its inputs arrive.

This is also what keeps the single client from becoming a bottleneck. Because dispatch is asynchronous, the coordination work overlaps with the compute already running on the chips, so the client stays off the critical path instead of stalling the accelerators between steps.

In Pathways, the runtime decides execution order when a group has more than one operation ready. In pipelined training, B's devices often hold both the forward pass for an upcoming micro-batch and the backward pass for an earlier one. If each device picked on its own, their collectives would conflict and the group would stall.

Pathways has a central scheduler that puts every program's operations into one consistent order, so operations that must communicate start together instead of deadlocking.

And because it's still one client, the development loop we mentioned before comes back. It's the same single Python process whether you drive one host or thousands of hosts, so you can drop into a shell, set a breakpoint, and inspect the run as it goes. When something stalls, there's one place that knows what's queued on every device and which dependency hasn't been met, instead of a hang spread across separate processes.

Put together, Pathways separates model building from execution. You choose how the model partitions across hardware using the client. And the runtime handles the transfers, ordering, and scheduling that keep accelerators busy, across the whole cluster rather than just your slice of it.

Described this way, Pathways can start to sound like an orchestrator you already know. If scheduling work, moving tensors, and keeping a whole cluster busy brings Ray to mind, it should. But the two work at different layers.

Ray is an open-source framework for orchestrating Python-native AI workloads across large clusters. It provisions the cluster, schedules your Python code as tasks and actors, and launches your workload across hosts, holding data loading, training, serving, and tuning under one control plane. To Ray, an accelerator is a resource to schedule.

What Ray doesn't change is how your training runs on the chips. The job inside each worker still runs as SPMD. The framework inserts the collectives, and each process sees only its own devices. Ray places and starts that job but it doesn't turn it into a single program that drives the whole cluster.

That is the layer Pathways works at. It changes the execution model underneath, so one client coordinates every chip. Because they sit at different layers, the two are complementary. In principle, Ray can orchestrate the cluster and the pipeline while Pathways handles how the training step executes.

So concretely, Ray's job is about orchestration while Pathways' job is about execution. With Ray, you tie your data, training, serving, and tuning together and spread heterogeneous work across accelerators and clouds. With Pathways, you run one model across many groups of chips, express patterns beyond SPMD, and recover from a failed host instead of relaunching the whole job.

When a training job stops being one uniform program, the challenge becomes coordinating the dependencies between stages, the tensors that cross the network, and the ordering that keeps every accelerator fed.

SPMD requires you to address these dependencies. With Pathways, you describe the computation once, from a single client, as a graph of blocks and the tensors between them. And you have a runtime that places the blocks on virtual devices, moves and reshards the tensors, orders the work across the cluster, and remaps around a failed host.

With Pathways, model building and execution are separated and you reason about the whole cluster instead of one slice of it.
